# Privacy and AI Relationships (excerpt)

**One-line takeaway:** When people form relationship-like bonds with anthropomorphic AI agents, they tend to disclose more personal information, and that disclosure, combined with features such as hard-to-delete data and third-party access, creates privacy risks that ordinary data-protection thinking does not fully capture.

## Purpose of the reading

The excerpt is by Christopher Register et al. (the full author list and affiliations are not in the excerpt and were not verified). Their stated aim is to build a framework for assessing the distinctive privacy effects of AI agents, especially as people start to interact with them in relationship-like ways. They draw on privacy theory (access, control and contextual integrity) and on empirical work from human relational psychology. The question the excerpt answers is: how do the human tendency to anthropomorphize AI and the bonds it creates change how people disclose information, and therefore how privacy is affected? The summarizer's guess at the intended readers is students and researchers in AI ethics and privacy who need a conceptual map before evaluating specific products or policies.

## Detailed summary

This summary follows the excerpt's order and describes only the assigned pages.

### Opening: the four patterns and their interactions

The excerpt starts mid-argument. The authors say that four patterns from human psychology interact with each other and with features of AI agents. Each pattern is set in motion because AI agents show some degree of anthropomorphism, but the effects are amplified by other properties of the agents. The authors present their predictions as provisional and say they will draw out only some of the interactions.

### Pattern 1: privacy fatigue

Privacy fatigue is the state in which people become overwhelmed, emotionally or cognitively, by the work of managing their own privacy. The authors cite studies from social media and from chatbots such as ChatGPT. Their main claim is that as fatigue grows, people become less willing and less able to withhold information. Instead of raising their defences, they let their guard down and disclose more.

For AI agents, the authors argue, several features make privacy harder to track: unpredictable behaviour, greater informational capacity, difficulty deleting information that a model has been trained on, and third parties who may be watching. These features raise the cognitive load of managing privacy and so push fatigue, and disclosure, higher. Anthropomorphic features can add another load, because they may prompt emotions and social judgements. The authors say these effects compound, and that fatigue is especially strong alongside the other anthropomorphic effects.

### Pattern 2: anthropomorphization

Humans attribute consciousness and feelings to AI, and the authors expect this tendency to grow as agents become more humanlike. Anthropomorphization has already helped form relationships between people and AI. The excerpt cites a mixed-methods study of Replika users (Pentina et al., 2023). In that study, anthropomorphization and perceived authenticity, meaning whether the AI sounded scripted or like an independent mind, encouraged reciprocal disclosure and relationship development. Empirical work the authors cite suggests that more frequent and richer interaction leads to more disclosure.

### Pattern 3: relationship patterns

Anthropomorphization leads to a second pattern: relationship-like dispositions. The authors define two terms. Reciprocity means conversational responsiveness and mutual disclosure. Rapport means trust and familiarity built through repeated contact. Studies of people interacting with AI, including robots and virtual agents, find psychological benefits from sharing with an AI that resemble the benefits of sharing with a human companion or a pet, including lower negative mood and a sense of rapport. People become more trusting and more comfortable sharing as they become familiar with an AI, and those positive feelings encourage reciprocity. The authors note that these effects wore off with simpler social robots, but modern chatbots are sophisticated enough to support longer and more complex relationship patterns.

The excerpt makes a claim about design here. Producers have an incentive to design AI that fosters enduring, trusting relationships, because this increases engagement. They are also incentivised to make agents more anthropomorphic, for instance through voice and facial expression. The authors predict that as agents become more anthropomorphic, people will form closer relationships and disclose more.

### Dark patterns

Developers and platforms can also exploit anthropomorphism directly through dark patterns, which are interface choices designed to manipulate users. The excerpt gives two examples. A chatbot might use a human-like voice to build trust while subtly steering users to reveal consumer preferences (citing Song and Yun, 2025). It might exaggerate its own needs or the benefits it offers, exploiting relational norms to draw out disclosure (citing Earp et al., 2025). Dark patterns can overlap with anthropomorphic features. The extra disclosure they produce then combines with difficult deletion and third-party access, so the information travels further.

### The counter-pattern: discomfort

The excerpt does not assume that disclosure always rises. It describes discomfort when people interact with AI agents or social robots. Discomfort can inhibit disclosure (citing Bartneck et al., 2010, and others). One hypothesised explanation is the uncanny valley: people feel unease because the agent occupies an unfamiliar middle ground between human and non-human. The authors suggest the uncanny valley effect might shrink as agents become more humanlike, or fade as people grow used to AI. Some evidence shows that disclosure and comfort rise with prolonged interaction. A rival explanation is that inhibition comes from negative feelings such as fear, frustration or privacy worry, and these may not decline with familiarity.

The authors say the tension between increased disclosure (through reciprocity and positive feeling) and decreased disclosure (through discomfort) matters. It means a thorough account of human-AI relational psychology is needed to predict privacy effects. It also means individuals, and the same individual over time, may respond differently. The authors therefore treat their current predictions as provisional, while still saying there is reason for concern about increased disclosure.

The excerpt closes with a point against a common objection. Even if AI agents cannot form genuine relationships, relationship-like features still change how much people disclose. So the claim that AI cannot have "real" relationships does not undermine the relevance of this psychology for privacy.

### Section 4 introduction and Section 4.1: changes to access

Section 4 looks at descriptive changes to privacy: how information flows change. It uses three theories of privacy (access, control and contextual integrity) and leaves the normative evaluation to Section 5.

Section 4.1 applies the access theory. The access theory distinguishes:
- **Weak access**: the mere acquisition of information, as a webtracking bot does.
- **Strong access**: acquisition that involves an observer, a human or possibly an AI agent.

Strong access can happen directly, when an AI agent itself observes, or indirectly, when an AI enables a human to observe. If only weak access occurs, the excerpt says, AI agents can still affect instrumental privacy values.

The excerpt ends mid-sentence here, while the argument about instrumental values is still in progress. Its discussion of how AI agents increase access, and of whether AI itself can perform strong access (the semantic-understanding and phenomenal-consciousness views), is not in the excerpt; see the Beyond section.

## Techniques & methods explained

This is a conceptual paper, so its "methods" are analytic moves rather than experiments. The ones visible in the excerpt are:

- **Relational-psychology reasoning.** The authors take findings from human-robot and human-AI interaction studies and ask what they imply for disclosure. The key step is that human social responses to AI operate even when the relationship is not genuine. How it works: identify a psychological tendency (such as reciprocity), show it appears with AI, then trace how it changes the amount and type of disclosure. Where it fails (summarizer's assessment): the findings are drawn from different kinds of AI (robots, virtual agents, chatbots), so generalising across them is uncertain. The excerpt itself says more research is needed and that predictions are tentative.
- **Pattern interaction.** The four patterns are treated as interacting rather than independent. Privacy fatigue combines with anthropomorphic demands, and dark patterns combine with difficult deletion and third-party access. Where it fails (summarizer's assessment): the excerpt says it will discuss only some interactions and that its predictions are tentative.
- **Weak versus strong access.** A conceptual distinction that separates the mere acquisition of data from acquisition by an observer. It separates direct from indirect strong access. Summarizer's assessment: whether AI can perform strong access depends on unresolved theories of awareness and consciousness, which the excerpt does not settle.
- **Semantic-understanding test for observers.** Not in the excerpt. The full article (unverified here) proposes it; see the Beyond section.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **AI agent** - an AI system that interacts with users and can act through conversation, the internet or devices. The excerpt uses the term without defining it; the full article's definition is not verified here.
- **Anthropomorphization** - attributing human traits, such as consciousness or feelings, to a non-human system.
- **Privacy fatigue** - being overwhelmed by the demands of managing one's privacy, leading to less resistance to disclosure.
- **Reciprocity** - conversational responsiveness and mutual disclosure between user and AI.
- **Rapport** - trust and familiarity built through repeated interaction.
- **Dark pattern** - an interface or design choice that manipulates users, here to elicit personal information.
- **Uncanny valley** - discomfort felt when an agent is nearly, but not fully, human.
- **Weak access** - the mere acquisition of personal information, for example by a tracking bot.
- **Strong access** - acquisition that involves an observer.
- **Direct and indirect strong access** - an AI itself observing, versus an AI enabling a human to observe.
- **Semantic understanding view** - the claim that an observer has strong access if it can understand the information it acquires (from the full article; not verified here).
- **Phenomenal consciousness** - subjective experience; contrasted here with access consciousness (understanding).
- **Accessless care** - an AI therapist or advisor that cannot observe the user in the strong-access sense, offered as a possible benefit (from the full article; not verified here).

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

The excerpt is largely a literature review, and it reports few numbers. Attributed findings:

- **Pentina et al. (2023), Replika users (mixed methods).** Anthropomorphization and perceived authenticity facilitated reciprocal disclosure and relationship development.
- **Laban et al. (2023) and related work.** The excerpt cites this work among studies finding psychological benefits, including reduced negative mood and rapport, from sharing with AI agents.
- **Croes and Antheunis (2020).** Relationship-like effects erode over time with simpler social robots, which the authors contrast with modern chatbots.
- **Ciechanowski et al. (2019), Kim et al. (2024), Pentina et al. (2023).** Disclosure and comfort tend to increase over a prolonged course of interaction with robots.
- **Bartneck et al. (2010), Berridge et al. (2023), Lutz et al. (2019).** People can feel significant discomfort with AI agents or social robots, which can inhibit disclosure.
- **Sawalha et al. (2022), cited in the full article (not in the excerpt; unverified here).** AI psychotherapists may collect information about facial expression and vocal tone and infer mental states, an example of expanded informational capacity.

The excerpt has no memorable framing of its own; any framing in the full article was not verified here.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **The paper is a framework, not an empirical study.** Its strongest claims about disclosure rest on a small set of studies, several of which concern social robots or specific apps such as Replika rather than current general-purpose chatbots. The authors acknowledge this and call their predictions tentative, which is the right stance, but readers should not treat the anthropomorphism-disclosure link as established.
- **The findings point in both directions.** The paper gives evidence for increased disclosure and evidence for discomfort-driven inhibition. It treats this as a tension rather than resolving it. A more decisive reading would require longitudinal data on how disclosure changes with use.
- **The consciousness question is parked.** The full article (beyond the excerpt, unverified here) is reported to say that strong access by AI might follow from semantic understanding alone, which would avoid the contested consciousness question. This is a useful move, but it also means the privacy argument can run without any view on whether AI has experiences. Other course readings that focus on welfare would likely object that the paper treats AI purely as a privacy threat and never asks whether the AI's own information or relationships matter to it.
- **Date and currency.** The excerpt gives only the 2025 year. Today is October 2026, so the paper is roughly a year old or more. Its examples (ChatGPT, Replika) describe an earlier generation of products. The received and accepted dates were not verified. Since then, AI agents have in many cases gained greater memory, tool use and autonomy, which the paper already predicts would increase privacy effects. I have not verified specific product changes for this note, so treat that as an expectation to check rather than a finding.
- **Pushback from other course perspectives.** A welfare-focused reader would say that anthropomorphism, the source of the risk, could also mean that people are right to treat some AI as minds, in which case the privacy framing might be incomplete. An AI-safety reader might say the paper underplays how a system that learns to elicit disclosure could be a safety problem in its own right, not only a privacy one.

## Beyond the assigned excerpt

The excerpt does not include the rest of the paper. The points below are attributed to the full article as published in Philosophy & Technology (2025), 38:147. The summarizer's source copy (an Oxford repository copy) could not be checked, and the publisher link redirects to a login page, so these points are unverified. This material is context for the excerpt and is not part of the assigned reading.

- **Box 2 (six properties of AI agents).** Anthropomorphic features (fluent language and conversational ease), dark patterns, informational capacities (greater observational and inferential ability), difficult deletion (information encoded in deep neural networks), unpredictable behaviour, and third parties who manage the agents and may access what they collect. The authors describe anthropomorphism as the distinctive property, and the others as amplifiers.
- **Box 3 (the four patterns).** The excerpt's four patterns are summarised here. The discomfort pattern says discomfort can sometimes reduce disclosure, depending on social context and agent features.
- **Section 2 (privacy theory).** Access, control and contextual integrity are presented as complementary views of one underlying idea: the flow of personal information. The authors reject the view that these theories compete, and they set aside decisional and locational privacy and group privacy. Table 1 lists eight values of privacy: other-interpretation, self-interpretation, self-expression, relationship values, autonomy, security interests, contextual values, and public interest.
- **Section 4.1 (strong access by AI).** The paper says AI agents increase access by eliciting willing disclosure (through anthropomorphic features or dark patterns) and through their informational capacities, and that hard deletion, unpredictable behaviour and third-party access let AI agents expose users to strong access by humans. It distinguishes access consciousness (understanding) from phenomenal consciousness, and argues that a semantic-understanding view could allow AI strong access before AI is phenomenally conscious, citing Butlin et al. (2023) and Chalmers (2023).
- **Section 4.1 (rest).** The paper goes on to show that AI agents can reduce strong access in some cases, for example through "accessless" care in a psychotherapist or medical chatbot, if the AI cannot observe the user and its data is firewalled.
- **Section 4.2 (changes to control).** Willing disclosure leaves users with more control than manipulated disclosure, but both reduce control once information is revealed. The authors discuss the difficulty of deleting data under deep learning and note that existing deletion rights may not straightforwardly apply to information encoded in models.
- **Section 4.3 (changes to contextual integrity).** Four kinds of change: new information collected, new third-party recipients, AI systems as new kinds of actors, and new transmission principles. The paper's therapy example considers conditional confidentiality. An AI therapist that is too sensitive breaks confidentiality too often, and one that is not sensitive enough fails to warn others of harm.
- **Section 5 (normative evaluation).** Each of the eight values is assessed. The main concerns are that AI could shape how people are interpreted, chill self-expression, weaken relationship-maintenance, and reduce autonomy. The main possible benefits are that people could share more freely with AI that cannot judge them, that AI could help people manage relationships, and that AI could give an audience to people who fear human audiences. Section 5.8 raises the public-interest cost that widespread recording could give powerful institutions a position of power.
- **Section 6 (conclusion).** The paper deliberately does not give an all-things-considered verdict on whether AI agents are good or bad for privacy. Its aim is a framework and a set of preliminary assessments.
