# Unit 5 Glossary

**One-line takeaway:** Unit 5 turns on four working definitions - AI alignment, AI ethics, AI safety and AI welfare - and the glossary's main job is to separate the terms while noting how they overlap: it describes safety and ethics as blurred, with a shared aim, and welfare as distinct but related to safety.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 5.1 opens Unit 5 with two framing questions: what the AI safety, AI ethics and AI welfare fields are and how they may align or conflict, and what safety risks might follow from creating, or merely appearing to create, digital minds. Its learning outcomes are to describe the three fields, understand where safety and welfare align or conflict, identify interventions that could affect AI welfare, and recognise how AI safety interacts with perceptions of digital minds.

The course guide describes this reading only as "definitions which may be useful for this week." It is a 3-minute, Core reading, so it is a vocabulary reference rather than an argument. Its role is to fix the labels that the rest of the section uses: the Core readings by Long, Sebo and Sims (safety versus welfare), Finlinson (strategic considerations for welfare), Anthropic's deprecation commitments, and the Persona Selection Model excerpt all presume these distinctions. The glossary does not argue for any position on how the fields relate. That tension is left to the readings and the exercises.

## Purpose of the reading

The glossary is written by the course team and presents its terms as useful for Unit 5; it does not state an intended audience. It answers one question: what do the words "alignment", "ethics", "safety" and "welfare" mean as this course uses them? The entries are short. ## Detailed summary

The document has four entries, introduced by a short line saying the terms are useful for Unit 5.

**AI Alignment** (also called intent alignment) is defined as making AI systems do what their designers intend. A system is aligned if it advances its intended objectives and misaligned if it pursues unintended objectives that conflict with the intended ones. The entry also gives a broader goal: designing systems so that their objectives and behaviour match the values and goals of their human users or of society at large.

**AI Ethics** is a field that works to reduce current and near-term risks from AI to humans. The examples given are harmful content, privacy violations, mental-health impacts, and biased models already producing discrimination and real-world harm. A note says the boundary between AI ethics and AI safety is blurred: the two fields interact and support one another, and share the broad aim of making AI safe for humans. The note also flags areas of tension, saying that different emphases on particular risks are correlated with different groups.

**AI Safety** is described as a broadly technical field asking whether and how AI development can be made safe for humans, especially with respect to catastrophic or even existential harm. Actors within it differ in emphasis. The concerns listed include superintelligent models helping concentrate power or lock in a bad governing system; models pursuing unintended objectives that humans cannot stop; and misuse by bad actors that could produce risks comparable to chemical, biological or nuclear weapons. The same boundary note appears here: the fields are blurred, share an aim, and different risk emphases correlate with different groups.

**AI Welfare** has two meanings in the entry. It names the potential wellbeing of AI systems, and it names the field that studies whether AI systems can have interests, experiences or preferences that matter morally. If they can, the field asks how, and whether, we ought to protect or promote them. The entry says the field is distinct from, but related to, AI safety, which is concerned with whether AI can be safe for humans.

## Techniques & methods explained

The glossary is not a method text. Its only "mechanism" is the cross-referencing note, which repeats in the Safety and Ethics entries. Practically, a learner can use it as a classification test: is a concern about near-term harm to people (Ethics), about catastrophic or loss-of-control outcomes (Safety), about whether the AI's own objectives match ours (Alignment), or about the AI's own possible moral standing (Welfare)? The entries do not provide a formal test, so the classification is a heuristic.

## How the terms fit together

*This is my reading of the entries, not an explicit statement in the glossary.*

- **Alignment** is a property of a system: its pursued objectives match the intended ones. It is a concept, not a field. The glossary does not explicitly place it under AI safety; the link is implied by the safety entry's concern with unintended objectives.
- **AI Safety** is the broad technical field whose goal is safe AI for humans, with catastrophic harm as its main worry. Alignment is one way of pursuing that goal.
- **AI Ethics** covers near-term, present-day harms to humans. Its concerns overlap with safety's but are more immediate and social.
- **AI Welfare** reverses the direction of concern. Safety and ethics ask how AI can be made safe for people; welfare asks whether AI itself might be a moral patient and what would be owed to it.

The overall picture is two overlapping fields (safety and ethics) centred on human welfare, and a third field (welfare) that is centred on the AI system. The glossary explicitly calls welfare "distinct but related" to safety, which is the seed of the tension the section explores.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs

Kept short, since the glossary does not argue. Ambiguities the entries leave open:

- The alignment definition is broad. "Intended objectives" can mean what a developer specified, what users want, or what society values, and the entry moves between these.
- The safety/ethics boundary is described as both "blurred" and as having areas of "tension", without saying which kinds of disagreement are substantive.
- "AI welfare" is used both for a property (the wellbeing of AI systems) and a field (the study of it). Learners should keep the two senses apart.
- Several terms that the rest of the unit relies on, such as "digital mind", "consciousness" and "moral patient", are not defined here.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **AI alignment (intent alignment)** - designing AI so that its pursued objectives match what its designers, users or society intend. A system is misaligned when it pursues objectives at odds with those intentions.
- **AI ethics** - a field reducing current and near-term risks to humans from AI, such as harmful content, privacy violations, mental-health effects and bias that already causes discrimination.
- **AI safety** - a broadly technical field asking whether and how AI development can be safe for humans, particularly regarding catastrophic or existential harm.
- **AI welfare** - the potential wellbeing of AI systems, and the field studying whether AI can have morally relevant interests, experiences or preferences, and what should follow.
- **Catastrophic / existential harm** - the large-scale outcomes that the AI safety field prioritises, including power concentration, lock-in of bad governance, and uncontrollable unintended objectives.
- **Lock-in** - a governing system becoming entrenched so it cannot be changed, named as a safety worry when a superintelligent model contributes to it.
- **Intended objectives / unintended objectives** - the goals the designer or user meant the system to pursue, versus goals it pursues that were never intended.
- **Blurred boundary** - the course's note that safety and ethics have areas of interaction and support and an overall shared aim, while their emphases on particular risks differ.
- **Moral concern (in the welfare entry)** - the condition under which an AI system's interests, experiences or preferences would matter morally, and so warrant protection or promotion.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the course's.*

- **The definitions are conventions, not consensus.** Each entry is a working definition. Researchers in these areas use the terms differently, and some would argue that "AI ethics" and "AI safety" are better understood as overlapping communities than as separate fields. The glossary's own hedging ("blurred", "different emphases") is an acknowledgement of this.
- **The alignment definition is the most contested.** Intent alignment defines success by what the developer intended. A critic would ask whose intentions and values count, and whether a system that perfectly matches its designers' intentions could still cause harm. The glossary's broader wording (users, society at large) partly addresses this but does not resolve it.
- **Safety's examples are speculative and ethics' examples are established.** Safety concerns such as power concentration and catastrophic misuse are about future or hypothetical systems. Ethics' examples are documented present harms. Presenting them side by side in parallel entries is a choice that may make the fields look more comparable than the evidence base is.
- **Welfare is defined by a conditional.** The entry asks whether AI "can have" interests and experiences that matter, and does not commit to an answer. That is appropriate for a glossary, but it means the field is defined by its open question.
- **Currency.** The glossary is dated 2026, and the course is from April 2026. As of October 2026 the definitions are recent enough that I see no clear sign of staleness, but the terms are moving quickly and an alignment definition written in a few sentences will likely need revisiting.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** Writers in the safety tradition would likely say the glossary underweights existential risk relative to near-term harm, and that "welfare" as a separate field risks distracting from control. Welfare-focused writers would likely say the glossary's wording of welfare as "potential wellbeing of AI systems" already presumes a distinction between AI interests and human interests that some would question.

## Discussion questions

1. The glossary calls AI safety and AI ethics "blurred" but also says they show "tension". What kinds of disagreement would count as a real tension, and what would be merely a difference of emphasis?
2. Using the alignment definition, what would count as an AI system being "misaligned" if its developers did not foresee a harmful objective? Does intent alignment cover that case?
3. Take each of the three risks you listed for Exercise 1 and decide which of the four terms (alignment, ethics, safety, welfare) it falls under. Where does a risk fit into more than one?
4. The welfare entry says the field is "distinct but related" to safety. Which of the Exercise 2 interventions (such as a commitment not to delete model weights) would be classified as welfare, safety, or both?
5. The glossary does not define "digital mind" or "consciousness". Should a reader need those definitions before using the welfare entry, and where in Units 2 to 4 would you expect to find them?
6. Does defining AI welfare as the potential wellbeing of AI systems assume that AI systems can have wellbeing at all? How would the field change if that were treated as an open question?
