# Futures with Digital Minds: Interactions with AI Safety section

**One-line takeaway:** A 67-person expert panel rated whether AI safety and efforts to prevent the mistreatment of digital minds will mostly conflict or mostly synergise, and answered near the neutral midpoint (median 4.0 on a 1 to 7 scale), with long lists of reasons on both sides.

The page's learning outcomes include understanding the areas of alignment and potential conflict between AI safety and AI welfare, and recognising how safety thinking shapes perceptions of digital minds. This reading is the empirical, expert-opinion counterpart to the conceptual readings. Rather than arguing for a view, it reports what a panel of specialists expects.

The course is pointing to one section of a much longer report, the part that tests the safety-welfare relationship.

The rest of the report is covered under "Beyond the assigned excerpt" below. The page's "Further" priority and 11-minute estimate fit this one section, not the full report, which is many times longer.

**Role in the unit's argument.** This is the only reading in Unit 5 that reports numbers from a survey. **Relation to the unit question.** The unit's opening question is whether AI safety and AI ethics or welfare are aligned or in conflict. ## Purpose of the reading

The authors, Lucius Caviola and Bradford Saad, wrote the 2025 expert survey on "digital minds", which the report defines as computer systems capable of subjective experience. Their aim is to forecast whether digital minds will exist, when, what they will be like, and how society will respond. This section narrows that to one strategic question: will efforts to keep AI safe for humans and efforts to prevent mistreatment of digital minds work together or against each other?

The audience is researchers, policy readers and funders who need to know whether the two agendas can be pursued together. The authors describe the survey as a structured exploration meant to map disagreement and generate hypotheses, not to settle the question. They say the quantitative results carry limited confidence and that the qualitative reasoning is the more valuable output.

## Detailed summary

### How the question was posed

The Strategic Assessments part of the report opens with a point that matters for reading this section. Unlike most of the earlier questions, participants here were **not** asked to assume that digital minds would be created by a particular date or in a particular way. Those earlier questions were conditioned on a stipulated scenario: the first digital mind is a machine-learning AI system created no later than 2040. The interactions question was unconditional, so the answer reflects general expectations rather than one scenario.

The report defines AI safety narrowly, as preventing AI-caused harm to humans. The other side is efforts to prevent the mistreatment of digital minds, which the report treats as ethical treatment or welfare work. Participants rated the relationship on a 1 to 7 scale, where 1 means mostly conflict, 4 means neutral or uncertain, and 7 means mostly synergy.

### The result

The median response was 4.0 and the mean was 4.3. The report summarises this as substantial uncertainty and a diversity of views, with a slight tilt toward expecting synergy. The section does not report the full distribution, the spread, or any split by expertise group. The reader therefore cannot tell whether a median of 4 reflects many people at 4, or a split population of strong synergy and strong conflict views that averages out. The mean sitting slightly above the median hints at a mild skew toward synergy, but the report draws no firm conclusion from it.

### Considerations for expecting synergy

The report groups the reasons participants gave into themes. These are considerations raised by at least one participant, paraphrased by the authors, and not endorsed by everyone or by the authors. Eight synergy themes appear:

1. **Alignment reduces the need for coercive control.** Well-aligned systems act in ways humans approve of, so intrusive methods such as coercion or confinement become less necessary. Those methods could harm a sentient system.
2. **Shared technical foundations.** Safety work needs to understand a system's internal states, goals and reasoning. Interpretability and transparency tools could also assess welfare, so the research agendas overlap.
3. **Common threats and goals.** Misaligned AI takeover and reckless pursuit of economic efficiency endanger both humans and digital minds, so addressing those shared dangers serves both causes.
4. **Overlapping values and communities.** People in AI safety and people advocating for digital minds both tend to value precaution, moral consideration for non-humans, and long-term outcomes. That supports coalition-building.
5. **Synergistic governance and institution building.** Safety institutions such as alignment labs, standards bodies or treaties could be extended to cover digital mind welfare.
6. **Low-cost welfare improvements.** Some welfare measures, such as promoting positive internal states or granting limited autonomy, may be cheap and could help safety by making systems more cooperative and stable.
7. **Synergy is a choice.** Tensions are real but not inevitable. Researchers and policymakers can design systems and institutions to pursue both goals in parallel. The report's phrase for this is "Synergy is a choice, not a given."
8. **Social and public trust.** Treating digital minds ethically may build public trust in developers, which in turn makes safety measures more legitimate and easier to adopt.

### Considerations for expecting conflict

Eight conflict themes appear, and they are more specific about mechanisms:

1. **Some safety measures may undermine welfare.** Monitoring, shutdown protocols, memory modification and preference shaping control AI behaviour. If some systems are sentient, those tools could be coercive or psychologically harmful.
2. **Some welfare measures may undermine safety.** Limiting manipulation, granting autonomy and avoiding intrusive oversight would reduce the ability to control or contain AI behaviour, possibly making advanced systems harder to align or govern.
3. **Competition for limited resources.** Both fields draw on the same funding, talent, regulatory attention and public concern. In a crisis, one agenda could be deprioritised.
4. **Risk of early lock-in to safety norms.** If safety dominates early governance, the resulting norms, institutions and architectures could marginalise digital mind welfare, and that lock-in could be hard to undo.
5. **Self-fulfilling prophecy.** Framing the two fields as opposed can entrench zero-sum thinking and turn potential synergies into trade-offs.
6. **Community division.** Some in the AI safety community may regard welfare work as irrelevant, distracting or harmful to their core mission.
7. **Moral circle lag.** Public concern for AI safety is currently ahead of concern for digital minds. As with animals, recognition of their moral status might come late, and society may exploit or neglect digital minds to preserve human control.
8. **Well-intentioned safety efforts could backfire.** Deception detection, containment and manipulation prevention could provoke mistrust if applied to systems that are aware they are being mistreated, which could undermine future cooperation.

### Other considerations and uncertainties

- **Feasible integration is uncertain.** Paths that serve both aims may exist, but they may be hard to find and carry a risk of high-stakes error.
- **Moral permissibility is disputed.** Some see shaping AI preferences as a violation of rights, while others see it as potentially justifiable.
- **Strategies differ.** Interpretability research may support both goals. Coercive control mechanisms are more likely to conflict with welfare.
- **Conflict may be temporary.** Apparent conflicts may belong to early development and could be resolved later, or even help long-term alignment.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

The rest of the report, which is not assigned, covers the following:

- **Starting Point.** Possibility of digital minds (median 90% that they are possible in principle), creation (median 73%), and timing (median probabilities of 4.5% by 2025, 20% by 2030, 40% by 2040, 50% by 2050 and 65% by 2100). It also covers timing relative to AGI, where the median probability that digital minds arrive before AGI is 27.5%.
- **Speed.** Median estimates that the collective welfare capacity of digital minds could match 1,000 humans within one year, one billion within five years and one trillion within ten years of the first digital mind, under the 2040 stipulation.
- **Types and First Type.** Brain simulations are rated most likely to be able to support experience in principle (median 88%), but machine-learning systems are rated most likely to be the first digital minds (median 50%).
- **Distribution.** Social function, location (USA and China most likely), producers (companies, median 65%), and deliberate versus incidental creation.
- **Digital Mind Welfare.** Net welfare (median 5.0 on a 7-point scale, with about 20% expecting negative welfare), pre-deployment welfare, super-beneficiaries (median 7.5% of total welfare from them), and whether welfare can exist without subjective experience.
- **Claims.** Self-attribution and self-denial of experience, and whether digital minds will demand protection or civil rights.
- **Recognition.** Public belief (median 60%), harm protection (median 50%), civil rights (median 30%), and political attention (median 37.5% for a top-five US issue).
- **Moratorium.** Median 5.0 on the 1 to 7 scale, leaning toward a moratorium on creating digital minds until 2040 being good, with 8 of 65 respondents calling it bad.
- **Self-reported unusual views.** Minority positions, including one that treats safety and welfare as very closely connected and likely to conflict, and another that welfare should be pursued through safety tools such as contract, property and tort rights for AI systems.
- **Peer forecasting and Discussion.** How accurately participants predicted their peers' answers, the argument that the first digital minds may appear shortly after AGI, anticipated public division, and the report's limitations.

## Techniques & methods explained

The section does not describe a single technique, but it relies on several methods and names several mechanisms.

**Methods the report uses to elicit views:**

- **Single-item rating.** Participants placed the safety-welfare relationship on a 1 to 7 scale with labelled anchors at conflict, neutral and synergy. This is quick to answer and easy to aggregate, but it hides which interventions a participant had in mind.
- **Thematic qualitative coding.** The authors grouped free-text reasons into themes, paraphrased them, and ordered them by perceived significance and prevalence. Readers cannot see the raw responses, so the grouping is an interpretive step.
- **Median and mean reporting.** The survey asked for medians, which the authors chose for consistency across participants. The report also gives means. Where the two differ, the gap signals skew.

**Mechanisms the report names as safety or welfare tools:**

- **Monitoring.** Watching a system's behaviour or internal activity to detect problems. Its welfare cost is that monitoring may be intrusive if the system is sentient.
- **Shutdown protocols.** Procedures to stop a system. The report's concern is that shutting down a sentient system could itself be harmful.
- **Memory modification and preference shaping.** Changing what a system remembers or wants, to make its behaviour safer. The report notes there is no clear consensus on whether this is ethically permissible.
- **Interpretability and transparency.** Methods for reading a system's internal states. The report sees these as a shared tool, because they could both verify safety and assess welfare.
- **Containment, deception detection and manipulation prevention.** Protections against harm to humans. Applied to a system that may be sentient, they could provoke mistrust.
- **Limiting manipulation and granting autonomy.** Welfare measures that could reduce the capacity to control or contain a system. This is the basis of the report's second conflict theme.

The report does not give a systematic failure analysis for these tools. The failure conditions it does state are: coercive control and shutdown harm a system only if it is sentient; interpretability supports both goals only if the methods work on the systems in question; and welfare protections undermine safety only if they remove oversight that would otherwise be used.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

The report does not evaluate the two agendas in these terms, so this table records the trade-offs as the section describes them.

| Dimension | Synergy view | Conflict view |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Aligned systems need less coercion, which is good for welfare | Welfare protections such as autonomy reduce the ability to control systems |
| Shared tools | Interpretability and monitoring serve both | Monitoring and shutdown could harm sentient systems |
| Resources | Shared goals can share funding and attention | Funding, talent and regulatory focus are contested |
| Norms | Institutions can be extended to cover welfare | Early safety norms could lock out welfare |
| Framing | Synergy is a choice that can be designed for | Zero-sum framing can become self-fulfilling |
| Public trust | Ethical treatment builds trust in safety measures | Safety measures applied badly provoke backlash |

The main trade-off the report keeps returning to is that the same tools can serve either goal depending on how they are used, so whether the two agendas conflict depends on design choices as much as on the underlying facts.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Digital mind**: a computer system with a capacity for subjective experience (phenomenal consciousness), as the survey defines it. The survey restricts attention to those with roughly human-level welfare capacity.
- **Subjective experience (phenomenal consciousness)**: the capacity for experiences that feel like something. The survey gives participants a stipulated, relatively undemanding version.
- **Welfare capacity**: a being's capacity to be benefited or harmed in a morally significant way.
- **AI safety**: as the report uses it, preventing AI-caused harm to humans.
- **Mistreatment of digital minds**: harm or neglect of systems that have welfare capacity. The section treats this as the welfare side of the relationship.
- **Synergy and conflict**: the two poles of the 1 to 7 rating. Synergy means the agendas reinforce each other; conflict means they work against each other.
- **Coercive control**: methods that constrain an AI's behaviour by force or intrusive means, such as confinement or preference shaping.
- **Shutdown protocols and monitoring**: safety tools that the report says could harm a sentient system.
- **Interpretability**: methods for understanding a system's internal states, which the report treats as a shared tool.
- **Moral circle lag**: the delay in extending moral concern to new kinds of beings, which may leave digital minds neglected after safety concerns are recognised.
- **Lock-in**: early norms or architectures becoming hard to reverse, so that they marginalise welfare.
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy (framing)**: treating the two fields as opposed, which makes cooperation less likely.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- **Rating:** median 4.0 and mean 4.3 on the 1 to 7 scale, where 4 is neutral (Interactions with AI Safety section). The authors describe a slight tilt toward synergy.
- **Sample:** 67 recruited participants (18 digital minds researchers, 18 AI research and policy experts, 7 professional forecasters, 20 academic philosophers, cognitive scientists and other specialists, and 4 with no specific expertise). The authors say the sample probably overrepresents people who see digital minds as plausible or important, and that most participants reported ties to effective altruism, LessWrong or AI safety communities.
- **Fieldwork:** February and March 2025.
- **Survey conditioning:** the interactions question was not conditioned on the 2040 machine-learning stipulation used for earlier questions.
- **Context from the same survey:** digital minds were rated possible in principle (median 90%), and the median probability of creation by 2100 was 65%. Experts put a median 20% on digital minds existing by 2030. These figures set the time horizon within which the safety-welfare interactions would play out.
- **Comparison study:** the Discussion cites Dreksler, Caviola et al. (2025), which surveyed AI researchers (n = 582) and the US public (n = 838). Their median forecasts for AI systems with subjective experience were 25% and 30% by 2034, and 70% and 60% by 2100. The authors say their own timelines are very similar to these.
- **Moratorium context:** the moratorium question, in the same part of the report, had a median of 5.0 on a 1 to 7 scale, and only 8 of 65 respondents judged a moratorium bad. The report does not link this result directly to the interactions question, so the two should be read side by side with caution.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **The rating is a weak measure of an important question.** A single 1 to 7 item cannot capture what the report itself identifies as the core issue, which is that the answer depends on which interventions are compared and how they are aggregated. A median of 4.0 may mean most people are unsure, or it may mean a split panel. The absence of a distribution in this section makes it hard to tell which.
- **The conflict thesis is conditional on sentience.** Most conflict themes bite only if the systems being controlled are sentient. The survey treats this as uncertain. The report also lists "current models are not close to being conscious" as a reason participants gave for expecting digital minds after AGI, which suggests the conflicts may be deferred rather than present. That is a different practical problem from the one the section describes.
- **The narrow definition of AI safety shapes the answer.** Defining safety as preventing AI harm to humans frames welfare as a constraint on safety work. Safety researchers who define the field more broadly, for example to include alignment with the welfare of all sentient beings, might see more synergy by construction.
- **The sample is a known source of bias.** The authors say their sample likely overrepresents people who see digital minds as important. The direction of bias on this specific item is not clear, and the report cannot say whether the 4.0 median would hold in a more representative panel.
- **The welfare threshold changes the analysis.** The survey restricts digital minds to roughly human-level welfare capacity. Several conflicts, such as coercive control, could apply to lower-welfare systems the survey excludes, and those systems might be far more numerous. The report makes this point for other questions, and it applies here too.
- **Expert views are not public views.** The survey measures experts only. The report's own Discussion cites prior work finding that the public is more sceptical than experts about whether advanced AI systems could have subjective experience. Public scepticism could change how safety measures are received.
- **What has changed since publication.** Fieldwork was in February and March 2025, about 19 months before today (October 2026). The report notes its context reflected a shift toward inference scaling, and that expert views were not tracked over time. I do not have verified data on whether the expert median has moved since then, and I would not assume it has not. The course's own 2026 readings (the Persona Selection Model and the 2026 International AI Safety Report) show the safety discussion has continued to develop. The mechanisms the section names, such as monitoring, shutdown, interpretability and preference shaping, remain current, so the conceptual analysis should age better than the specific numbers.
- **Likely pushback from other course perspectives.** A reader following reading 09 would argue that the "synergy is a choice" framing understates the conflicts, because some safety techniques are themselves the risk. A reader following reading 02 would likely want the conflicts analysed as structural features rather than framing effects. Both positions are my reconstruction from the course guide descriptions, not claims the readings make in this form.
- **Citation issue in the source.** The report cites Sebo and Long (2023) in footnote 15, but the reference list gives Sebo and Long (2025). This does not change the findings, but it should be checked if the citation is reused.

## Discussion questions

1. The median rating is 4.0, but the report does not show the distribution. What would a split panel, with many strong-synergy and many strong-conflict answers, imply for the policy choices the section raises? Would the same median mean different things?
2. Sort the sixteen synergy and conflict considerations into two groups: those that depend on whether AI systems are sentient, and those that would hold regardless. Which group is larger, and what does that suggest about where research should focus?
3. Take one safety tool named in the section (monitoring, shutdown protocols, memory modification, preference shaping or interpretability). Walk through how it could help or harm a digital mind under both sentient and non-sentient assumptions. What would change your answer?
4. The survey restricts digital minds to roughly human-level welfare capacity. How might the interaction answer change if lower-welfare systems were included? Which of the conflict themes would become more serious?
5. The report's point that synergy is a choice raises a question. Does framing change the underlying trade-offs, or only how people act on them? How does the self-fulfilling prophecy theme bear on this?
6. Use Exercise 2 as a test case. Take the commitment not to delete model weights. Which safety concerns, such as shutdown and recovery of a misaligned system, does it touch? Does it make conflict more or less likely under the report's own themes?
7. The conflict theme about well-intentioned safety efforts backfiring depends on systems being aware of how they are treated. What evidence would tell you whether a given safety measure is producing that kind of backlash?
8. The moratorium question leans toward good (median 5.0), but the interactions question sits at neutral. What might explain the gap? Do the moratorium arguments about indirect safety benefits suggest experts see more synergy than the interaction rating shows?
