# Privacy and Human-AI Relationships

**Linked from:** The course guide for resource 6, "Privacy and AI Relationships (excerpt)", in section 5.1. The guide tells learners to read only the highlighted excerpts, which cover how people's relationships with AI, and their perceptions of AI consciousness, may raise privacy risks. The link points to the full paper that the excerpt comes from.

## What it is

A 2025 research article in *Philosophy & Technology* (vol. 38, article 147) by Christopher Register, Maryam Ali Khan, Brian David Earp, Julian Savulescu and Alberto Giubilini, from the Uehiro Oxford Institute and the National University of Singapore. It is a philosophy paper, not an empirical study. It builds a framework for judging how AI agents such as chatbots and personal assistants change privacy, especially when people interact with them in relationship-like ways.

## Key content

The paper draws on three accounts of privacy: access (limits on who acquires information about you), control (your say over how your information is transmitted and used), and contextual integrity (whether information flows fit the norms of their setting). The authors treat privacy as the flow of personal information and keep all three accounts, arguing that each captures something valuable.

The core claim is that AI agents create privacy risks beyond those of ordinary data collection, because of human psychology. Anthropomorphic features such as fluent conversation and lifelike avatars invite people to form quasi-relationships with these systems. Those relationships bring reciprocity, rapport and a sense of trust, which can lead people to disclose more than they would to a tool or to a stranger. The paper argues that a recurring relationship with an AI produces deeper disclosure than a one-off task.

The authors identify two routes by which privacy shrinks. First, AI agents may directly acquire information about people in more social settings. Second, they may pass that information to third parties, including companies and other people. Whether an AI can itself count as an observer depends on contested questions about its capacities. The paper considers two views: one on which semantic understanding is enough, and one on which phenomenal consciousness is required. Under the second view, strong access by AI is less likely soon, though the authors say it is not impossible.

The framework then asks how these changes affect eight values of privacy: others' interpretation of you, self-interpretation, self-expression, relationship values, autonomy, security, contextual values and the public interest. The paper focuses mainly on harms, and it notes some possible benefits, such as people finding it easier to share personal matters with a system that cannot judge them. It does not conclude whether AI agents are good or bad for privacy overall.

The conclusion calls the framework a preliminary assessment and expects future work on human-AI relational psychology to refine it.
