# Mapping the Field of Artificial Consciousness

**One-line takeaway:** This is a directory of 47 organisations working on AI consciousness, AI welfare and digital minds, grouped into research, training/fieldbuilding and media, and it is most useful as a map for choosing where to look next and how to get involved.

## Where this sits in the course

**Section context.** Section 8.1 is the closing section of the course. Its page says the unit is for consolidating what the learner has studied and for preparing a contribution to the group. The page poses three questions: what are the main organisations in digital minds work and how might you get involved, what are you taking away from the course, and what four-minute contribution would you like to share. Its stated learning outcomes are to identify relevant organisations and institutions, examine current research and identify gaps, analyse principles for research on digital minds, and articulate your own thinking. The main exercise is a free-text brainstorming document in which the learner reflects on the course and develops one idea into a short four-minute presentation for their group.

**What the course guide says about this reading.** The guide gives one line: the reading "details organisations currently working in the digital minds space." In paraphrase, the course wants the learner to know who is doing this work, so that the reflection and next-steps part of the unit is grounded in real institutions rather than only in abstract questions.

**Role in the argument.** This is a reference resource, not an argument. Its job in the section is the first learning outcome (identify relevant organisations and institutions) and the first of the three page questions (the main organisations and how to get involved). It supports the exercise indirectly: a learner who has used the map can name specific groups and specific research programmes when they say where the field is heading or where the gaps are. It is rated Further, so it is not required reading for the exercise, but it is the only reading in this section that answers "who" rather than "what should we think".

**Priority.** Further, estimated 16 minutes. Among the Section 8.1 readings, the course rates the Sebo "Principles for AI Welfare Research" pieces and the Otherminds resources page as Core, and this map sits with the other Further readings.

## Purpose of the reading

PRISM is a UK-based nonprofit that builds coalitions around conscious or seemingly conscious AI. The map was produced by PRISM as a field-building directory. Its stated purpose is to show who is working on AI consciousness, AI welfare and digital minds, so that newcomers, researchers, funders and journalists can find each other. It answers the question "which organisations exist in this space, what does each one do, and where do I start?"

The directory is for a broad audience: people entering the field (the training section is explicitly for "routes into the field"), researchers looking for collaborators, and media looking for experts. It is a snapshot of the field at a point in time, and the version the course links to says it was updated in July 2026.

## Detailed summary

The page is a single directory with three top-level groups, each with sub-groups. The counts are 47 organisations in total: 26 in Digital Minds Research, 8 in Training and Fieldbuilding, and 13 in Media. Each entry has a short description and a country tag. The entries carry no external links; the only links on the page are site navigation, a suggestion form, a correction form and a link to the raw data as JSON.

The descriptions below are paraphrased from the entries as retrieved on 2026-10-08. They are not the verbatim page text; see the coverage note at the end of this section.

### Digital Minds Research (26)

**Lab teams (programmes inside AI companies or large labs), 5 entries.**

- **Anthropic, Model Welfare (USA).** An Anthropic research programme on AI welfare and moral consideration, linked to its alignment and interpretability work. The directory gives its founding as late 2024.
- **Araya, Inc. (Japan).** A Tokyo AI company studying consciousness and what could make artificial systems conscious. Led by Ryota Kanai.
- **Conscium (UK).** An AI safety company exploring what role machine consciousness could play in safety. Led by Daniel Hulme.
- **Google DeepMind (UK).** No public dedicated programme on AI consciousness, though individual researchers have published on it.
- **Google, Paradigms of Intelligence (USA).** No public dedicated programme either. Researchers including Geoff Keeling and Winnie Street have published on AI welfare, and the directory mentions a forthcoming book from Cambridge University Press.

**University centres (academic research groups), 10 entries.**

- **Jeremy Coller Centre for Animal Sentience, LSE (UK).** Covers animal sentience, invertebrate farming ethics, and AI and sentience. Led by Jonathan Birch.
- **Leverhulme CFI (UK).** A Cambridge interdisciplinary centre on the long-term implications of AI, including consciousness and welfare. It houses Cambridge Digital Minds.

- **Reflexion Lab (international).** A cognitive neuroscience lab at UC Irvine and UCL studying metacognition and consciousness, including tests that could apply to AI. Led by Megan Peters.

- **BAMΞ, Bamberg (Germany).** Applies mathematical methods to consciousness, including an AI consciousness sprint. Co-organises the Models of Consciousness conferences.
- **MINT Lab (USA).** A Johns Hopkins philosophy and computation lab working on AI normative competence and moral status. Led by Seth Lazar.
- **NYU Center for Mind, Brain, and Consciousness (USA).** Works on foundational mind-brain questions, including debates about LLM sentience. Co-directed by David Chalmers and Ned Block.

**Nonprofits and institutes (independent research organisations), 9 entries.**

- **Astera Institute, Neuro and AGI (USA).** A philanthropic institute taking a neuroscience-informed, long-term basic research approach to safe AGI.
- **California Institute for Machine Consciousness (CIMC) (USA).** Studies how artificial conscious systems could be built. Runs the MC conference series. Led by Joscha Bach.
- **Co-Sentience Initiative (international).** Aims to prepare society for artificial minds. Its CF Debate database collects arguments for and against computational functionalism. Led by Chris Percy.
- **Eleos AI Research (USA).** A nonprofit on AI wellbeing and moral patienthood, offering research and recommendations. Led by Robert Long.

- **Sentience Institute (USA).** A nonprofit on digital minds and moral circle expansion. Runs the AIMS Survey on public attitudes.
- **The Harder Problem (USA).** A nonprofit on societal readiness for artificial sentience. Maintains the Sentience Readiness Index. Based in Portland.

**Scientific communities (professional societies), 2 entries.**

- **Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness (ASSC) (international).** A professional society running what the directory calls the field's largest consciousness conference. Publishes *Neuroscience of Consciousness*.

## Techniques & methods explained

This is a directory, so there is no method in the research sense. The organisation of the map itself is the useful part, and it is worth understanding as a way to navigate the field.

- **Three-way split by activity.** Research, Training and Fieldbuilding, and Media. Research is what produces knowledge about digital minds. Training and fieldbuilding is what grows the number of people working on it. Media is what shapes how the public and policymakers think about it. A learner looking for a role should decide which of these they want to join.
- **Sub-grouping by institutional type.** Within research, the split is between lab teams (inside AI companies), university centres, nonprofits and institutes, and professional societies. The type tells you about the funding model, the freedom to publish and the likely independence of the work. Lab teams may have access to models; university centres may have more academic freedom; nonprofits may be more policy-oriented.
- **Country tags.** Each entry is tagged with a country or "international". The field is concentrated in the USA and the UK, with some presence in Japan, Germany and international bodies. This matters for fellowship eligibility and visa requirements.
- **Lead names.** Many entries name a lead researcher. This is a quick way to find people to follow, for example Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, Lucius Caviola and Jonathan Birch, who appear in several places.
- **Crowdsourced maintenance.** The page has suggest-an-organisation and suggest-a-correction links. The directory is therefore a living list, not a peer-reviewed survey, and entries reflect what the maintainers know and what people submit.

## Fit & trade-offs

**Strengths (as the directory presents itself).**
- Broad coverage of the field in one place, across research, training and media.
- Short entries that tell you what each group does and who leads it.
- Explicit routes into the field: fellowships, courses and mentorship programmes with stated tracks.
- Machine-readable raw data is available for anyone who wants to build on it.

**Limits and caveats.**
- It is self-curated and not a neutral survey. PRISM is itself listed as one of the 47 organisations, and it also produces the directory. The learner should treat its inclusion criteria as a choice the maintainers made.
- Entries are one or two sentences. They do not assess quality, output or influence. A group that is listed is not thereby endorsed.
- Several entries say the organisation has no public dedicated programme on AI consciousness (for example Google DeepMind). That is a useful signal, but the directory does not say how it was checked.
- The date is a moving target. The version retrieved says "Updated July 2026", while the course page and the reading metadata date the reading 2025. The reading's link is also stale (it now redirects).
- The directory includes the course itself, which shows it is partly a promotional or ecosystem document. The learner should weigh that.

**Trade-offs for the learner.** A map like this helps you find where to go next quickly. It does not tell you which group is doing the most important work, and it cannot replace reading their actual outputs. The learner should use it to pick two or three organisations to examine, not as a ranking.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Digital minds** - the course's umbrella term for AI systems, or other artificial entities, that might have minds, experiences or moral status that matter for how we treat them.
- **AI consciousness** - whether an artificial system has subjective experience; the directory frames this as a research question that several groups study empirically or philosophically.
- **AI welfare** - the study of whether AI systems could have wellbeing, and how that would bear on how they are treated.
- **Moral patienthood** - the property of being an entity whose interests and wellbeing matter morally. Eleos AI Research is described as working on this.
- **Lab teams** - research programmes inside AI companies, for example Anthropic's Model Welfare programme.
- **Fieldbuilding** - activities that grow the number and quality of people and institutions in a field: fellowships, courses, coalitions and convening.
- **Computational functionalism** - the view that mental states are defined by their computational or functional role. The Co-Sentience Initiative's CF Debate database is organised around arguments for and against it.
- **Sentience Readiness Index** - a measure maintained by The Harder Problem, which tracks societal readiness for artificial sentience.
- **AIMS Survey** - a survey on public attitudes to AI minds, run by Sentience Institute.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Assumptions.** The directory assumes that the field is real and growing enough to need a map. It also assumes that listing an organisation is a neutral act. Neither is obviously true. The field is small, and the list is shaped by who the maintainers know.
- **Self-interest.** PRISM produces the directory and is one of its entries. The course itself is listed. Those facts do not make the map wrong, but they mean it should be read as an ecosystem document with an interest in growing the field.
- **Contested claims.** Phrases such as "the field's largest consciousness conference", "the only lab-internal welfare programme" and the claim that some groups have "no public dedicated programme" are self-descriptions. I have not verified them. A learner should check any claim they plan to repeat in the group presentation.
- **What has changed.** The reading is dated 2025 in the course, but the directory version I retrieved says it was updated in July 2026, and today is October 2026. The course link is stale: the PRISM URL now redirects to the digitalminds.guide field map. The learner should cite the current directory, not the course-listed URL.
- **Gaps.** The directory has no information on funding, staff size, output or outcomes. It cannot tell you whether a group has influenced policy or practice. For that, the learner needs the other readings in this section.
- **How other perspectives in the course would push back.** The Sebo principles reading would ask whether the field's institutions reflect pluralism and probabilistic thinking, which the directory cannot show. The governance readings would ask whether listing research groups is a substitute for building the policy capacity that the course treats as urgent. The AI-safety perspective would ask whether welfare groups are being given the same attention as alignment work.
