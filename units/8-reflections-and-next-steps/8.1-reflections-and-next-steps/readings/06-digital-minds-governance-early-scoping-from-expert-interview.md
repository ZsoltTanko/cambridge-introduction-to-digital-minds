# Digital minds governance: early scoping from expert interviews

**One-line takeaway:** Caviola and Smith interviewed 29 experts to map a near-empty field, and the main lessons are that digital minds governance is important and neglected, that most experts think binding legislation is premature, and that AI welfare and AI safety need close coordination rather than being treated as opposed.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors want to scope digital minds governance, which they describe as a new field that "barely exists." Their guiding question is: given radical uncertainty about AI consciousness and moral status, what should society do? They separate questions by time horizon. Near-term questions concern how AI companies and policymakers should handle the possibility of AI moral status and how to respond to public opinion. Mid-term questions concern institutions, legal standing for AI systems, and whether creating digital minds should be restricted. Long-term questions concern the scale of creation, integration into society, human-AI coexistence and the governance of digital minds in space.

The audience is people who could enter or fund the field, along with the field's own practitioners. The authors interviewed experts "to compare notes and generate ideas." The piece is therefore a scoping report and a set of hypotheses to test, not a settled policy recommendation.

## Detailed summary

### Method and sample

Between February and April 2026 the authors interviewed 29 experts, mostly for about an hour each. The group included 9 philosophers, 5 legal scholars, 5 field-builders and nonprofit professionals, 4 cognitive science or consciousness researchers, 4 policy and governance professionals, and 2 AI lab researchers working on model welfare and AI ethics. The authors give three caveats. The sample was not designed to be rigorous or representative and was skewed toward AI safety researchers in the US and UK. Not every interviewee was asked the same questions, so percentages refer only to those who discussed a topic. Views are anonymised and paraphrased.

### Public engagement and messaging

Interviewees generally preferred quiet, gradual institution-building to loud public messaging. The risks they named were that digital minds advocacy could look like industry hype, could draw opposition from AI companies worried about regulation, could be seen as a distraction from the "real" problems of AI governance, and could split communities that are otherwise aligned on AI safety. The authors do not say to avoid public engagement altogether. They advise prioritising "quality and nuance" over volume and recognition. Interviewees also disagreed about which framing to use: welfare and consciousness, pragmatic points about AI safety and corporate incentives, or dignity and rights. The authors conclude that messaging strategies need testing.

### Legislation and legal status

Most experts think it is too early for legislation. Almost all opposed legislative action, a few had mixed views, and one supported some form of legislation. The reasons given were scientific uncertainty about consciousness, uncertainty about which policies would help, the risk of "crying wolf" and damaging the field's credibility, the danger of locking in governance frameworks that are hard to reverse, possible backlash from affected groups, and the difficulty of knowing whether such laws would help or hurt alignment and human empowerment. Major legislation protecting AI welfare could also look undemocratic while public salience is low. Those with mixed views tended to favour narrow, targeted legal steps now.

The most concrete example discussed was a set of bills introduced in several US state legislatures. These would ban rights, responsibilities and legal personhood for AI systems, and in some cases declare that AI systems cannot be conscious, sentient or self-aware. Interviewees generally agreed that current AI systems should not have rights, but all 27 who discussed the bills raised concerns. Some thought bans on legal personhood could be premature and could harm AI welfare or human safety by blocking mutually beneficial trade with AI systems. Others thought it inappropriate for governments to legislate on open scientific questions about consciousness. Views varied on how serious the problem is, and many thought the bills were unlikely to matter much in practice.

### Reversibility of policy

The authors identify one consideration as crucial: how far policy decided now will shape the long-run future. Near-term policies might be swamped by larger historical forces, in which case it makes more sense to work on those forces directly. Alternatively, early policies might lock in governance regimes through path dependence, which is common in technological systems. That second possibility supports delaying binding decisions until there is more confidence.

### AI welfare and AI safety

The authors call the relationship between AI welfare and AI safety "one of the most important open strategic questions" in the field. Interviewees raised two linked points. Cooperative arrangements between humans and AI systems, such as mechanisms for trade or negotiation, might serve both safety and welfare. But legal protections for AI welfare could weaken human control over AI systems. Many interviewees held nuanced positions and wanted governance that satisfies both goals. The authors argue that the two communities need to coordinate closely to find interventions that help both, and that treating welfare and safety as opposed could produce bad outcomes in either direction.

### Politics, coalitions and public opinion

Interviewees said AI welfare is currently a low-salience issue, and that neither the political left nor the right is clearly friendly or hostile to it. There is broad, cross-cutting anti-AI sentiment, from worries about chatbots and children to environmentalist opposition to data centres. Many anti-AI groups are sceptical of digital minds concerns because they see them as part of AI hype. Interviewees also expected public belief that AI systems are conscious to grow, which could become a volatile political force. Such belief could bring better protections for AI systems, but it could also be exploited by AI companies or by the AI systems themselves to manipulate or disempower people.

### Religious and non-Western perspectives

The authors say the field is philosophically narrow, relying heavily on computational functionalism and Western analytic philosophy. Interviewees expected religion to shape how people think about digital minds. Two philosophers of religion they interviewed thought traditional Christian institutions would by default be sceptical of AI consciousness claims. Some suggested eastern religions might be more open, and that new religious movements could arise with novel views about AI. China and other non-Western perspectives were named as a major gap. Some interviewees thought China might be more open to considering digital minds, and that concepts of harmony and coexistence might be prominent there. The authors note that very few researchers in China work on these topics.

### Funding and field-building

Interviewees who discussed field-building and funding supported it universally. The research areas they flagged were technical research on AI welfare and mental capacities, philosophical foundations, macrostrategy and high-level governance, forecasting, longitudinal public-opinion tracking with consistent methods, legal scholarship on AI personhood, historical study of social movements and moral circle expansion, and work on model specifications and model character at AI labs. All 25 interviewees who discussed international expert coordination on digital minds called it a robustly positive strategy. The authors say they are working with colleagues on a related project to launch later in 2026.

### Conclusions and how to get started

The authors' overall judgement is that digital minds governance is "important and neglected." The field has no clear paradigms, little infrastructure, no talent pipeline, few organisations, few jobs and no settled research agendas. It is still unclear what concrete action to take. Because the field is small, a motivated person can get close to the frontier within months. Backgrounds in law, policy (including AI policy) and the social sciences are especially useful, and the authors say you do not need to be a philosopher of mind, an ethicist or an ML researcher to contribute.

They list organisations: the NYU Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy as an academic hub; Cambridge Digital Minds, which runs a fellowship, an online course and an annual strategy workshop; and Eleos AI Research, which works on AI welfare and partners closely with Anthropic. They also name AI safety fellowships that increasingly offer digital minds or AI welfare mentorship, including Future Impact Group, Neuromatch, MATS, Pivotal and SPAR. Their practical advice has three parts. Read the literature, starting with introductory materials such as the Digital Minds Beginner's Guide, the course, and an open list of strategic questions. Be cautious about public advocacy and unilateral political moves, because the field is fragile and early mistakes could cause damage. And talk to people, since most people in the field are reachable.

## Techniques & methods explained

The reading does not use a formal method. Its one analytic device is the expert interview. The authors asked most interviewees the same broad question about what should be done, asked a subset about specific policies, and summarised the answers as the proportion of interviewees who held a view, always qualified by how many were asked. They also used a time-horizon split (near, mid, long term) to organise governance questions and a path-dependence argument about reversibility to decide whether to act now or wait. Neither device is validated; they are organising tools for a scoping exercise.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Digital minds governance** - the emerging field asking what society should do given uncertainty about whether AI systems are conscious or have moral status.
- **Moral status** - whether an entity matters morally in its own right; the reading treats it as a live question for AI systems.
- **Radical uncertainty** - the authors' framing of the situation: no consensus on AI consciousness and no reliable test for it.
- **Path dependence** - the property by which early choices lock in later outcomes; the reason the authors give for caution about early legislation.
- **Crying wolf** - the risk that premature or exaggerated claims about digital minds damage the credibility of the field when they prove unfounded.
- **Legal personhood** - the status that lets an entity hold rights and responsibilities; the subject of the US state bills discussed.
- **Low salience** - the observation that AI welfare is not yet an issue voters or parties care much about.
- **Macrostrategy** - high-level research on which large-scale priorities and pathways matter most, listed among the research areas to fund.
- **Model spec and model character work** - internal lab efforts to specify how AI models should behave and what character they should have, named as a research area.
- **Computational functionalism** - the view that mental states are defined by their functional organisation; the reading calls it a dominant but narrow assumption in the field.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- 29 experts interviewed between February and April 2026, mostly for about an hour each (authors' count).
- Discipline breakdown: 9 philosophers, 5 legal scholars, 5 field-builders, 4 consciousness researchers, 4 policy professionals, 2 AI lab researchers.
- Almost all interviewees opposed legislative action on digital minds at present; one supported legislation of some kind (authors' summary).
- All 27 interviewees who discussed the US state bills raised concerns about them, though most agreed current AI systems should not have rights.
- All 25 interviewees who discussed international expert coordination said it is a robustly positive strategy.
- Interviewees with views on field-building universally supported more of it, and the authors list eight research areas they judged important.
- The authors describe the field as having no clear paradigms, little infrastructure, no talent pipeline, few organisations, few jobs and no settled research agendas.
- Their advice on the time needed to reach the research frontier: a motivated person can get close in months.
- The authors' footnotes say they plan a comprehensive report on the state bills in the coming weeks, and that surveys their lab is working on support the reading's points.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Method.** The evidence base is 29 informal interviews in which the questions were not identical across interviewees, skewed toward US and UK AI safety researchers. The authors flag this, but the headline counts (for example "all 27" or "all 25") describe only the interviewees asked a specific question and are not survey estimates. They are best read as signals about the expert conversation, not measures of the field.
- **Selection and self-interest.** The interviewees are people already in the field's network, and the organisational list includes Cambridge Digital Minds, which runs the course the learner is taking. A learner should weigh that list as a set of relevant organisations rather than a neutral ranking, and check the others (for instance by reading the Otherminds page in reading 01).
- **Staleness.** The interviews were conducted in February to April 2026, and the piece was published in 2026. As of October 2026 the authors' promised report on the US state bills and their international coordination project may have been released, and the state bills' status may have changed. I have not checked these; the learner should verify current legislative status before relying on the bill discussion. The Eleos and Anthropic partnership is described as it was at publication.
- **Contested claims.** The claim that a motivated person can reach the research frontier in months is an opinion, not a measured result. The claim that AI welfare and safety should be coordinated is a normative judgement that some AI safety researchers may reject, for example if they think welfare protections risk undermining human control more than the authors allow. The "public belief in AI consciousness could be manipulated" point is plausible but speculative.
- **Missing perspectives.** The authors acknowledge that religious and non-Western views are underrepresented. A critic would also point out that the sample gives little voice to the AI systems and the people whose welfare is at stake, and that the piece's policy caution could be a default that favours inaction when the stakes may be high.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** Advocates of the Principles for AI Welfare Research tradition would likely accept the call for calibrated, pluralistic thinking but could argue that waiting on policy carries its own risks if welfare harms are already occurring. Those focused on the AI welfare debate would likely press the claim that welfare and safety conflict less than the reading suggests, or that the cautious-messaging advice trades off against the need to raise salience quickly.

## Discussion questions

1. The authors say the field should prioritise "quality and nuance" over "volume and recognition." What would count as a good test of that claim, and what would make quiet institution-building fail?
2. The reversibility argument supports delaying binding policy, but the path-dependence argument also works in the other direction. Can you construct a case where waiting is the riskier choice?
3. Most interviewees opposed legislation, yet nearly all agreed current AI systems should not have rights. How can those two positions be reconciled, and does the state bills example support or undermine that reconciliation?
4. The authors call the welfare-safety relationship "one of the most important open strategic questions." Give one intervention that would plausibly help both, and one that would plausibly help one at the other's expense.
5. Interviewees expected belief in AI consciousness to become a political force. What evidence from the other units would you need to judge whether that belief would protect AI systems or be used to manipulate people?
6. The reading lists religious and non-Western perspectives as gaps. Pick one gap and explain how it would change one of the governance recommendations.
