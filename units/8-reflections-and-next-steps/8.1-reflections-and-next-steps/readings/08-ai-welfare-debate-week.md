# AI Welfare Debate Week

**One-line takeaway:** A week-long EA Forum event in July 2024 asked whether AI welfare should be an EA priority, and its tagged posts and retrospective give a snapshot of how the community argued about digital minds at that time.

## Purpose of the reading

The event was run by the EA Forum team, with Toby Tremlett as the main organiser. It asked Forum users to debate the statement that AI welfare should be an EA priority. The Forum defined "EA priority" operationally as allocating 5% of unrestricted talent and 5% of unrestricted funding to the cause.

The event was designed to surface arguments that change minds, not just to collect opinions. The retrospective (also tagged to the event) reports on whether it did. It is aimed at EA Forum readers, and the topic page is the index of posts tagged to the event.

## Detailed summary

### The event format

- Dates: 1 to 7 July 2024.
- A banner on the Forum front page let users place an icon on an agree-disagree axis and move it at any time during the week. Voting was non-anonymous, and users had to click to view results.
- An in-post slider was added as a second voting surface.
- Posts, comments, quick takes and link-posts on the topic were to carry the debate-week tag. Organisers planned to invite well-informed authors, but any user could take part.
- A "most influential posts" leaderboard credited debate posts when a user moved their icon. Points depended on how far the icon moved, and only each post's largest recorded shift counted.
- Guidance on posts said they did not need to be formal arguments. Link-posts, book reviews and lists of cruxes were welcome. Discussion was meant to centre on digital minds and AI welfare rather than AI in general. Familiar cause-comparison arguments were discouraged, and the organisers did not want much of the week spent on whether AI will ever be sentient.

### The retrospective: what happened

The retrospective, as summarised by the fetch, reports:

- The organisers' participation goal was 50 people who voted, commented or posted. Over 558 people voted and 27 authors wrote or co-wrote at least one post.
- A mind-change goal of 25 users shifting their slider position by 25% was exceeded, with 53 unique users doing so.
- 28 posts carried the debate-week tag. Seven reached 50 or more karma. The top post reached 77 karma. One post was curated.
- Most high-karma posts came from authors the organisers had contacted directly.
- Debate-week posts drew roughly 127 hours of engagement, about 11.6% of total engagement that week. There was no clear rise in daily engagement, and the event overlapped with a June controversy about the Manifest conference that had already raised activity.
- The front-page banner was seen as a success, though users found hundreds of votes hard to visualise.
- The leaderboard failed. Only about 30 users cited posts when they changed their minds, and an announcement post ranked highly by mistake. The author suggests removing or redesigning it.
- The author judged the event successful on its explicit goals but mixed on engagement, new users and messaging. The next debate question should be more specific and empirical. Future debate weeks might run every six to eight weeks.

## Techniques & methods explained

This reading is a community event, not a research method, so the "techniques" are the event's own mechanisms.

1. **Agree-disagree slider as a pre/post measure.** Users place themselves on an axis and can move the marker during the week. A change in position is treated as evidence that a post changed their mind.
   - How it works: the marker is recorded over time; users who move it can credit posts that influenced them.
   - What it is for: making persuasion visible and measurable.
   - Where it fails: it depends on users citing posts. Only a minority did, so attribution is weak. Voting was non-anonymous, which may affect how people vote.
2. **Influence leaderboard.** Points are awarded in proportion to the size of a shift, counting only each post's largest recorded shift.
   - Where it fails: the retrospective reports that it surfaced an announcement post by mistake and had too few citations to be informative.
3. **Tagging and curation.** Posts carry a shared tag so that a single topic page collects the debate, and the team chose which posts to curate.
   - Where it fails: high-karma posts were concentrated among authors the organisers contacted, so the visible debate is partly shaped by who was invited.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **EA priority (as defined here)** - allocating 5% of unrestricted talent and 5% of unrestricted funding to a cause.
- **Debate week** - a time-boxed EA Forum event built around one statement and a shared tag.
- **Agree-disagree slider** - the user-placed marker that records a view and can be moved.
- **Mind-change** - a move on the slider of at least the threshold used in the retrospective (25%).
- **Karma** - the Forum's score for a post, used as a rough proxy for community interest.
- **Digital minds** - AI systems or other entities that may have minds, including possible consciousness or welfare.
- **AI welfare** - the wellbeing of AI systems, if they have it, and the moral weight it should carry.
- **Moral patient** - an entity whose wellbeing matters morally. The "LLMs cannot usefully be moral patients" post argues against this for current LLMs.
- **Sentience** - the capacity for subjective experience, here treated as the key cruxes of the debate.
- **Cruxes** - the factual or conceptual disagreements that, if resolved, would change a view.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Age of the material.** The event is from July 2024. Today is October 2026, more than two years on. The sibling readings from 2025 and 2026 (Eleos AI, Rethink Priorities' AI Cognition, the 2025 survey) postdate the debate. Treat the posts as a record of 2024 community thinking, not current consensus.
- **Population.** The debate was run within the EA Forum, so the views reflect an EA-aligned audience. Many participants were invited, and the most visible posts came from them. The retrospective acknowledges this.
- **The operational definition of "priority".** Defining priority as 5% of talent and funding turns a moral claim into an allocation question. Critics could reasonably argue that the definition sets the terms of the debate, and the retrospective's suggestion of a narrower, more empirical question points the same way.
- **Measurement.** The slider and leaderboard give the appearance of measuring persuasion. The low citation rate and the confound with the June controversy mean the mind-change figures should not be read as evidence that posts changed minds.
- **Pushback from other course perspectives.** Sebo's pluralism argues against single-priority framings, so he would likely resist treating AI welfare as one cause to rank. The governance and research readings treat the field as a set of parallel programmes, not a single priority. The sceptical post on LLM moral patienthood would push back against any priority claim that assumes current systems are patients. These are inferences from the readings, not claims the sources make about this event.
