# Principles for AI Welfare Research

**One-line takeaway:** Jeff Sebo proposes twelve overlapping principles for studying whether AI systems can be welfare subjects, borrowing the hard-won methods of animal welfare research while insisting on pluralism, humility, probabilistic reasoning, and attention to ethics, holism and structure.

## Purpose of the reading

Sebo wants to give researchers a set of working principles for AI welfare research. He argues that AI welfare matters and is neglected, and that it is at least potentially tractable, but his main contribution is methodological. He addresses people who will design studies, interpret evidence and decide how to frame the field early, while it is still forming. He is explicit that the post expresses his "current, tentative" views, that it is selective, and that he welcomes correction. The question it answers is roughly: given that we cannot directly access any mind but our own, and that our intuitions about other minds are biased, how should we reason and organise research so that we make progress and avoid harm?

## Detailed summary

*Walk-through of the assigned section (section 4, "Principles for AI welfare research"), with the surrounding structure noted where it helps.*

Sebo opens section 4 by saying that the twelve principles are overlapping, general, and inspired by lessons from the past several decades of animal welfare research. He notes that animal and AI research differ in many ways but share enough to be instructive. Most principles follow a loose pattern: a claim about what the research needs, supported by lessons from animal welfare research. Principles 5 to 9 also end by saying how the same point will apply to digital minds.

**1. AI welfare research should be pluralistic.** Experts still disagree about the nature of welfare (pleasure and pain, satisfaction and frustration, or something else), about the basis of morality (welfare, rights, virtues, relationships), and about which beings have welfare and what is good or bad for them. Research should welcome these disagreements, stay open to being wrong, and study the topic from many perspectives, because the field makes more progress that way.

**2. AI welfare research should be multidisciplinary.** It is tempting to treat the field as a natural science, since cognitive science and computer science are needed to understand both biological and digital systems. Sebo argues it also needs the humanities, to surface the metaphysical, epistemological and normative assumptions that drive the work, and the social sciences, to identify the beliefs, values and practices that shape how humans treat animals and AI systems and so reveal biases.

**3. AI welfare research requires confronting human ignorance.** We can directly access only our own mind. Taking this seriously means humility: our knowledge of other minds will probably remain limited, and the limits grow as we move away from humans on the tree of life. It also means consistent epistemic standards. If we accept that we can reduce uncertainty about human minds despite our limits, we should accept that we can reduce it about nonhuman minds to some degree too.

**4. AI welfare research requires confronting human bias.** Humans show excessive anthropomorphism (attributing human features that are absent) in some contexts and excessive anthropodenial (denying human-like features that are present) in others. Intuitions are also shaped by self-interest, speciesism, status quo bias and scope insensitivity. Sebo says simple correctives such as "reject anthropomorphism" will be unreliable too. The response is to do the best we can with unreliable tools.

**5. AI welfare research requires spectrum thinking.** Questions like "do animals have language and reason?" are framed as binary. Better questions ask what kinds of language and reason animals have and lack. Animals share some human capacities but not others (many animals communicate, but not with the general, recursive syntax humans use). The point runs the other way too: many humans see fewer colours than many birds. Applied to AI, ask what capacities AI systems have and lack, not simply whether they have them.

**6. AI welfare research requires particularistic thinking.** Questions about "nonhuman primates" should become questions about chimpanzees or bonobos, and ideally about what kinds of language each has. Diversity grows as we move away from humans on the tree of life. Bumblebees and carpenter ants solve problems very differently. For AI, ask what particular kinds of AI minds are like rather than what AI minds in general are like.

**7. AI welfare research requires probabilistic thinking.** Certainty about animal minds is probably unavailable; we can have higher or lower confidence instead. Uncertainty increases as we move away from humans. Sebo asks researchers to express beliefs probabilistically, or at least as high, medium or low confidence, and to base action on risk principles such as a precautionary principle or an expected value principle. For AI, develop methods for thinking about and interacting with systems that accommodate uncertainty, rather than waiting for certainty that may never come.

**8. AI welfare research requires reflective equilibrium.** It is tempting to treat knowledge as flowing one way, from the human case to the nonhuman case. Sebo argues the flow runs both ways. Studying nonhuman minds can expand our understanding of perception, experience, communication and goal-directedness, and that expanded understanding can then be applied back to humans. Treating human, animal and AI welfare as mutually reinforcing increases the chance of new insight in all three.

**9. AI welfare research requires conceptual engineering.** Some disagreements about animal minds are partly about words. Whether insects feel pain may turn on whether we should use the word "pain" for them. Familiar concepts risk excessive anthropomorphism, unfamiliar ones risk excessive anthropodenial, and which risk is worse depends on the case. The same applies to choosing "they" or "it" for animals. Researchers should weigh risk and uncertainty when choosing terminology for AI.

**10. AI welfare research requires ethics at multiple levels.** Ethics plays three roles. It motivates the research, because we have a responsibility to improve the treatment of vulnerable beings and must learn who is vulnerable and what they need. It shapes and constrains the research, because we must avoid harming vulnerable beings in pursuit of knowledge and need ethical frameworks for our own research practices. And it guides application, because research should be useful and should support changemakers in applying it well.

**11. AI welfare research requires holistic thinking.** Links between humans, animals and AI systems can reveal trade-offs. Some people worry that caring for AI will distract from caring for humans and animals. They also worry that caring for AI means controlling it less, while caring for humans and animals means controlling AI more. Population-level welfare therefore needs holistic reasoning, which can find positive-sum options and, where trade-offs remain, prioritise them with minimal harm.

**12. AI welfare research requires structural thinking.** The trade-offs above partly exist because knowledge, power and political will are limited, and social, political and economic structures set us against each other. Sebo gives the example of AI researchers who treat ethics, safety and welfare as unaffordable in an AI arms race, though they might think differently in other contexts. Changing structures so we can treat everyone well may let us achieve and sustain more care than we currently assume.

Taken together, the principles can be sorted into three groups (this grouping is the summarizer's own, not the source's). Principles 1 to 4 concern the researcher's epistemic stance (pluralism, interdisciplinarity, humility, bias). Principles 5 to 9 concern how questions and concepts are framed (spectrum, particularism, probability, reflective equilibrium, terminology). Principles 10 to 12 concern the ethical and structural context (ethics at multiple levels, holism, structure).

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

The rest of the post is not in the assignment, but it provides the justification for section 4.

- **Introduction (section 1):** states the "current, tentative" status of the views and that AI welfare is one of many topics that merit attention. The post makes no claim about relative priorities beyond saying AI welfare research should be among them. It also notes the NYU Mind, Ethics, and Policy Program, launched in fall 2022.
- **Why AI welfare matters (section 2):** uses the EA scale, neglectedness and tractability framework. Digital minds could be far more numerous than biological minds. Their welfare receives far less attention, following a pattern in which attention is inversely related to population size. Tractability is open but should be investigated, not assumed. Human, animal and AI welfare may converge or diverge.
- **Priorities for AI welfare research (section 3):** sets four families of questions: who has the capacity for welfare (including whether simple, decentralised or silicon-based systems could be significant), how much welfare beings can have and how to compare it across substrates, what benefits and harms particular beings, and what follows for action, including rights, virtues and relationships.
- **Conclusion (section 5):** observes that understanding of welfare has expanded over the past half century, from humans only to mammals and fishes, with invertebrates now under serious consideration. It argues that the human treatment of animals has come too late for many, and that we can still act on future vulnerable populations. It says research fields are path-dependent, so framing them well early matters. It ends by pointing to a working paper with Robert Long arguing for moral consideration for AI systems by 2030.

## Techniques & methods explained

*The "Where it fails" notes are the summarizer's analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Scale-neglectedness-tractability framework.** A standard effective altruism tool. The post applies it to ask why AI welfare matters: how many beings are involved, how little attention they get, and whether progress is possible. Where it fails: the post itself admits tractability is unknown, so the framework is used to justify investigation rather than to prove that action will work.
- **Animal welfare as a source of analogy.** The field is used as a head start. Its methods can be borrowed where the cases are similar. Where it fails: the post notes the differences but does not systematically analyse them.
- **Spectrum and particularistic questioning.** Replace binary questions ("does X have Y?") with graded and specific ones ("what kinds of Y does this particular X have?"). This is a reframing technique. It fails when the question asked is genuinely binary, or when so many specific cases are studied that no general pattern emerges.
- **Probabilistic expression of belief.** State beliefs as probabilities or as high, medium and low confidence, then choose actions using a precautionary principle or expected value reasoning. Where it fails: the two risk principles can pull apart when probabilities are very small but harms are very large, and the post does not say which to prefer when they conflict.
- **Two-way inference (reflective equilibrium).** Use knowledge of nonhuman minds to revise understanding of human minds as well as the reverse. Where it fails: the post offers no procedure for checking when an update is warranted.
- **Conceptual engineering and risk-weighted terminology.** Choose words for minds, such as "pain" or "they", by weighing the risk of anthropomorphism against the risk of anthropodenial in context, rather than by default usage.
- **Bias correction by consistency rather than rules.** Rather than "reject anthropomorphism", check intuitions against consistent epistemic standards, and remain aware of named biases (self-interest, speciesism, status quo bias, scope insensitivity). Where it fails: the post concedes that these corrections will themselves be unreliable.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **AI welfare** - the possibility that AI systems have positive or negative states such as pleasure, pain, happiness or suffering, which can be good or bad for them.
- **AI welfare research** - the study of whether, to what extent and in what ways AI systems have the capacity for welfare, and what would be good or bad for them.
- **Welfare subject / moral standing** - a being whose welfare matters morally, and the broader status that gives it claims on how we treat it.
- **Pluralism** - keeping several perspectives on welfare and morality open rather than committing early to one.
- **Multidisciplinary** - combining sciences with humanities and social sciences to surface assumptions and biases.
- **Anthropomorphism** - attributing human features to nonhumans that they lack.
- **Anthropodenial** - denying nonhumans features they actually have, because they are not human.
- **Spectrum thinking** - asking what kinds and degrees of a capacity exist, rather than whether it exists.
- **Particularistic thinking** - asking about specific groups or kinds of mind, not about minds in general.
- **Probabilistic thinking** - expressing beliefs as degrees of confidence and acting on risk principles.
- **Precautionary principle** - under uncertainty, take protective action against possible serious harm even without proof.
- **Expected value principle** - weight possible outcomes by their probability and magnitude when choosing actions.
- **Reflective equilibrium** - here, the two-way flow of insight between human and nonhuman cases.
- **Conceptual engineering** - deliberately choosing or revising concepts and terms, weighing the risks each choice carries.
- **Holistic thinking** - reasoning about welfare across all populations together, looking for positive-sum options.
- **Structural thinking** - recognising that social, political and economic structures shape the trade-offs.
- **Scale, neglectedness, tractability** - the EA framework used in section 2 to argue that AI welfare matters.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

The principles section is argumentative rather than empirical, so the notable points are examples and framings.

- **Animal welfare history (from the conclusion, outside the assignment):** fifty years ago many experts thought only humans had welfare; twenty-five years ago many doubted that fishes did; the post says experts now feel confident that all these animals have it. Invertebrates such as insects are now under active debate.
- **Scale of animal use (conclusion):** the post says humans kill more than 100 billion captive animals a year for food, plus hundreds of billions of wild animals, and that trillions die yearly from deforestation, development, pollution and other human-caused change.
- **Neglect pattern (section 2):** attention to a population tends to vary inversely with its size, across human and nonhuman, vertebrate and invertebrate, and biological and digital cases.
- **Example of bias (principle 4):** excessive anthropomorphism and anthropodenial are both named as failure modes, with no single corrective.
- **Example of terminology (principle 9):** whether insects feel pain may turn on whether "pain" is the right word for them.
- **Example of spectrum (principle 5):** many humans see fewer colours than many birds, showing that capacity differences run in both directions.
- **Example of particularism (principle 6):** bumblebees and carpenter ants solve problems in very different ways.
- **Example of structure (principle 12):** some AI researchers may treat ethics and welfare as unaffordable in an AI arms race, but not in other contexts.
- **Self-reference:** the post points to an early working paper with Robert Long making the case for moral consideration for AI systems by 2030, and to an early-career award on animal and AI consciousness at NYU.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Age of the source.** The post was published on 19 June 2023, so it is about three years old as of October 2026. The author himself says it may not reflect his views a year later. Since 2023 the field has moved on. From memory, which may be out of date and should be checked, there have been published indicator-based assessments of AI consciousness, a 2024 report by Long, Sebo and colleagues titled "Taking AI Welfare Seriously", and frontier AI developers have started model welfare programmes. The principles still hold as a method, but the examples and the sense of how early the field is should be read with that change in mind.
- **Analogy limits.** The argument from animal welfare rests on shared biology and a long research tradition. Section 3 admits there is no evolutionary "common denominator" across substrates. For AI systems, the link between behaviour and inner states may be produced by training, not by evolution, so the analogy may fail at exactly the points that matter. The post notes differences but does not work through them.
- **Probabilistic principles can conflict.** Principle 7 offers both a precautionary principle and an expected value principle. These diverge when the probability of welfare is small and the potential harm is huge. Which to use is a value judgement the post does not settle.
- **Conceptual engineering is itself value-laden.** Principle 9 says to weigh the risk of anthropomorphism against anthropodenial. How the weights are set depends on values about which error is worse, and the post does not say whose values should set them.
- **Bias correction is hard to operationalise.** Principle 4 concedes that simple correctives are unreliable. The principles therefore ask researchers to be aware of bias without giving a test they can apply. A reader could fairly ask how one would know that a study had avoided these biases.
- **Practical guidance is thin.** Most principles are phrased as "we should" statements with a general rationale. They tell a researcher what to be mindful of, not what experiment to run next.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** Researchers focused on present harms and on AI safety would probably argue that the scale and neglect argument in section 2 is speculative and that effort should go to human and animal welfare first. Philosophers of mind would press the point that welfare capacity needs a theory of consciousness, not just methods for handling uncertainty, which the post treats as a separate problem. Those working on governance would likely say that the principles are useful framing but need institutions and incentives to matter.
- **Strength.** The post is careful about its own limits, names its tentativeness, and makes pluralism and humility central. That makes it a useful study partner: it asks you to argue with it rather than accept it.
