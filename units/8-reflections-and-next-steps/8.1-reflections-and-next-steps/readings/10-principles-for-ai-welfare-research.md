# Principles for AI Welfare Research

**One-line takeaway:** Sebo proposes twelve overlapping methodological principles, borrowed from decades of animal welfare research, for studying whether AI systems can be welfare subjects: stay pluralistic, combine disciplines, think in degrees and spectrums, correct for human bias, and treat ethics and social structure as part of the research itself.

## Purpose of the reading

Jeff Sebo, writing in the context of the NYU Mind, Ethics, and Policy Program (which he and colleagues launched in Fall 2022, with a focus on invertebrates and AI systems), writes this post to set out his current, self-described tentative views on three things: why AI welfare matters, what research priorities follow, and what principles should guide that research. He is writing for researchers entering the field, and he hopes the post will be a "useful starting point" rather than a full treatment. The question the principles answer is: given that we cannot directly access other minds and we are uncertain whether AI systems have welfare, how should researchers proceed so that their work is rigorous and does not repeat the mistakes made with animals?

## Detailed summary

Sebo draws the principles from animal welfare research, which he says has a head start of roughly half a century. He acknowledges that the two fields differ in relevant ways but argues that the similarities make animal research a useful source of lessons. He presents the twelve principles as "overlapping," so they should be read as a set of mutually reinforcing considerations, not as a checklist to apply in order.

### Principle 1: Pluralism
Experts still disagree about the nature of welfare (pleasure and pain, satisfaction and frustration, or something else) and about the basis of morality (welfare, rights, virtues, relationships). They also disagree about which beings have welfare capacity. The field should welcome these disagreements, stay open to being wrong, and study AI welfare from several perspectives.

### Principle 2: Multidisciplinarity
Although the work involves cognitive science and computer science, Sebo argues it is not only a natural science. Humanities work is needed to expose the metaphysical, epistemological and normative assumptions that guide research. Social science is needed to expose the beliefs, values and practices that shape how people treat animals and AI systems, and that may bias them.

### Principle 3: Confronting human ignorance
We only directly access our own minds, so knowledge of other minds is limited, and the limits grow as we move away from humans on the tree of life. Sebo draws a symmetry argument. If we accept that we can reduce our uncertainty about other humans' minds despite our limits, we should accept that we can reduce uncertainty about nonhuman minds in the same way. Humility and consistent epistemic standards are both required.

### Principle 4: Confronting human bias
Human judgement about minds is distorted in two directions: excessive anthropomorphism (attributing human features to nonhumans that lack them) and excessive anthropodenial (denying human-like features to nonhumans that have them). Intuitions are also shaped by self-interest, speciesism, status-quo bias and scope insensitivity. Sebo says simple correctives, such as "reject anthropomorphism", are unreliable. We still have to do the best we can with imperfect intuitions.

### Principle 5: Spectrum thinking
Questions such as whether animals have language or reason are often posed as yes-or-no. Sebo argues the better question is which kinds of language or reason a being has and lacks. He gives the example that many animals share information but lack the recursive syntax humans use, and he notes that humans see fewer colours than many birds. He expects the same point to hold for digital minds.

### Principle 6: Particularistic thinking
Questions are often framed at the level of a whole group, such as "nonhuman primates". Sebo argues for asking about specific species (chimpanzees, bonobos), and ideally about the specific kinds of capacity they have. He gives the example that bumblebees and carpenter ants solve problems and communicate in very different ways. Diversity increases as we move away from humans on the tree of life. The same applies to AI systems: the question should be about particular kinds of AI minds.

### Principle 7: Probabilistic thinking
Certainty about animal minds is likely unavailable, and uncertainty grows with distance from humans. Sebo asks researchers to express beliefs in probabilities, or at least as high, medium or low confidence, and to base action on risk principles such as the precautionary principle or expected-value reasoning. For AI he recommends building methods that accommodate uncertainty instead of waiting for certainty that may never arrive.

### Principle 8: Reflective equilibrium
Information usually flows one way: from what we know about human minds to animal minds. Sebo argues the flow runs both ways. Studying nonhuman minds can widen our understanding of perception, experience, communication and goal-directedness, and that understanding can then be applied back to humans. Treating human, animal and AI welfare as mutually reinforcing should produce new insights in all three areas.

### Principle 9: Conceptual engineering
Some disagreements about minds are conceptual rather than empirical. Whether insects feel pain can turn on whether the word "pain" should be applied to them. Applying a familiar concept risks anthropomorphism, and applying an unfamiliar one risks anthropodenial, so the choice depends on which error is worse. Choices such as "they" versus "it" carry similar stakes. Researchers should treat terminology choices as risk decisions too.

### Principle 10: Ethics at multiple levels
Sebo names three roles for ethics. Ethics motivates the research, because we have a responsibility to improve treatment of vulnerable beings and to learn what they need. Ethics shapes and constrains the research, because we must avoid unnecessary harm in pursuit of knowledge and need ethical frameworks for research practice. Ethics also governs application, because research should be useful and should support changemakers in applying it well.

### Principle 11: Holistic thinking
Human, animal and AI welfare are linked, and the links can create tradeoffs. One perceived tension is that concern for AI may distract from concern for humans and animals. Another is that caring for AI may mean controlling AI less, while caring for humans and animals may mean controlling AI more. Holistic thinking helps find positive-sum options where they exist and helps prioritise thoughtfully where tradeoffs remain.

### Principle 12: Structural thinking
Sebo argues that the perceived tradeoffs come partly from limited knowledge, power and political will, which are shaped by social, political and economic structures that set groups against each other. He gives the example that some AI researchers see AI ethics, safety and welfare as luxuries under an AI arms race but might hold a different view in other contexts. He argues that improving the structures could let us sustain more care for all three groups than we currently assume.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt
The rest of the post is not assigned, but it supplies the context for the principles.

- **Introduction (sections 1):** Sebo frames AI welfare as the possibility that AI systems have welfare states such as pleasure, pain, happiness and suffering, and argues that this requires AI welfare research. He says the research will be difficult and that the head start from animal welfare work is real but partial.
- **Why AI welfare matters (section 2):** He uses the effective-altruism scale-neglectedness-tractability framework. On scale, digital minds could eventually outnumber biological minds, so even a small probability of welfare across many beings could mean large expected welfare in total. On neglect, attention runs inversely to population size for humans, nonhumans, vertebrates, invertebrates and biological and digital beings. On tractability, he says it is an open question that should be investigated, not assumed. He also argues that human, animal and AI welfare may be linked, with both convergence and divergence of interests.
- **Priorities for research (section 3):** He names four families of questions: which beings have welfare capacity and moral standing (including whether sentience is sufficient and whether simple silicon systems could count); how much welfare beings can have and how to compare welfare across substrates; what benefits and harms particular beings, and when a life is above a worth-living threshold; and what follows for action, including rights, virtues and relationships, not only welfare.
- **Conclusion (section 5):** He notes that expert views on animal welfare have expanded over the last 50 years, from humans only to most vertebrates and now possibly invertebrates. He says that animal treatment in the past has been badly inadequate: humans kill more than 100 billion captive animals a year for food, and hundreds of billions of wild animals, with trillions more dying from human-caused environmental change. He argues that the field's early framing will shape its path ("path dependent"), and he points to a working paper with Robert Long arguing for moral consideration of AI systems by 2030.

## Techniques & methods explained

The principles are methodological, so the "techniques" are the argumentative and research moves Sebo recommends.

- **Scale-neglectedness-tractability (importance framework):** Judge a cause by how large the problem is, how little attention it gets, and whether progress is possible. Sebo applies it to AI welfare. *Where it fails:* the framework is a prioritisation heuristic. Sebo notes tractability is unknown, and the framework does not say how to measure welfare.
- **Expected-value and precautionary reasoning:** Act on probability-weighted outcomes, or take extra care when harm might be severe and irreversible. Sebo recommends both for uncertain minds. *Where it fails:* when the probabilities are themselves unknown, or when small probabilities multiplied by very large populations drive the conclusion, the outcome depends heavily on the estimate.
- **Expressing confidence in levels:** Use probabilities or high, medium and low confidence levels for beliefs about minds. *Where it fails:* the post does not say how to calibrate those levels.
- **Consistency check (symmetry of epistemic standards):** If we infer human minds from behaviour and evidence, we should apply the same standard to nonhumans. *Where it fails:* Sebo accepts that the limits grow with phylogenetic distance, so the check does not remove the difficulty.
- **Bias audit:** Identify anthropomorphism, anthropodenial and related biases, and then account for them. Sebo explicitly rejects simple corrective rules. *Where it fails:* the post offers no concrete procedure for doing this.
- **Spectrum and particularist decomposition:** Replace yes-or-no questions with questions about which kinds of capacity a particular being has. *Where it fails:* decomposition adds questions, and the post does not say how to prioritise among them.
- **Conceptual risk analysis:** Choose terms by comparing the cost of anthropomorphising against the cost of anthropodenial. *Where it fails:* the comparison depends on value judgements that the post leaves to the researcher.
- **Two-way reflective equilibrium across human, animal and AI minds:** Use findings about one kind of mind to revise the understanding of the others. *Where it fails:* the post gives no test of when a revision is warranted.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **AI welfare** - the possibility that AI systems have positive or negative states that can be good or bad for them.
- **Welfare subject** - a being that can have welfare states, which is the prerequisite for moral consideration.
- **Anthropomorphism** - attributing human features to nonhumans that lack them.
- **Anthropodenial** - denying human features to nonhumans that have them.
- **Spectrum thinking** - asking what kinds and degrees of a capacity a being has, not whether it has the capacity at all.
- **Particularistic thinking** - asking about specific kinds of being, not groups.
- **Probabilistic thinking** - expressing beliefs as degrees of confidence and acting on risk principles.
- **Reflective equilibrium (as used here)** - the two-way exchange of insight between human, animal and AI minds.
- **Conceptual engineering** - choosing or redefining terms, with attention to the risk of misapplying them.
- **Precautionary principle** - taking protective action under uncertainty when harm may be serious.
- **Expected value** - probability-weighted outcome, used to compare actions under uncertainty.
- **Scale, neglectedness, tractability** - the effective-altruism framework for judging the importance of a cause.
- **Path dependence** - the claim that a field's early framing shapes its later development.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Date and currency:** The post was published on 19 June 2023, about three years and four months before the current date (October 2026). It predates most of the empirical work in this section, including the 2025 survey and the 2025 Eleos and PRISM pieces. Its principles are still a sound method statement, but its sense of what AI systems are like is dated. Its claims about digital minds are hypothetical. It does not engage with the specific capabilities of later systems.
- **Analogy strength:** The animal welfare analogy does most of the work. Sebo acknowledges differences, but he does not spell them out. Animal welfare researchers have direct behavioural and physiological markers in many species. AI systems lack the shared evolutionary ancestry that grounds those markers. This is a weakness the principles do not address directly.
- **Expected-value risk:** The scale argument (many digital minds, small probability each, large expected total) can be pushed to very large conclusions with small probabilities. The post does not give a method for setting or bounding those probabilities, so the scale argument rests on an estimate it cannot check.
- **Principles are generic:** Several principles (pluralism, multidisciplinarity, holism) are widely accepted and hard to disagree with. The more specific claims (conceptual risk comparison, two-way reflective equilibrium) are harder to operationalise. The post gives few tests that would show a principle was violated.
- **Hidden assumptions:** The post assumes that welfare is a real and measurable state in other minds, and that it can be good or bad for the being. Those assumptions are contested, and Sebo flags some of them (Principle 1). The post does not discuss whether AI systems may lack welfare entirely.
- **How other course perspectives would push back:** Readers focused on AI safety (Unit 5) may argue that welfare research distracts from safety, which is the tension Principle 11 raises. Governance readers (Unit 7) may argue that principles without institutions have little effect, which the post's section 3 and Principle 12 partly accept.
- **Post-publication status:** The author says the post represents current, tentative views that may change a year later. Treat it as a dated position that the field has since moved past in some respects, not as the field's current consensus.

## Discussion questions

1. Sebo says animal welfare research has given us a head start. Which of the twelve principles depend most on that analogy, and which would still hold if the analogy were removed?
2. Principle 4 rejects simple correctives such as "reject anthropomorphism". What would a more useful procedure for checking one's own bias look like, and what evidence would show it works?
3. Principle 9 says the choice between "pain" and a new term depends on which error (anthropomorphism or anthropodenial) is worse. How would you decide which error is worse for a given AI system? Who should make that call?
4. Principle 7 recommends expected-value reasoning under uncertainty. Use the scale argument in section 2 to test whether the reasoning could justify very large costs on very small probabilities. What extra safeguards would you add?
5. Principle 11 names a tradeoff between caring for AI and controlling AI. Which exercise from the brainstorming doc (Exercise 1) would you use to explore this, and why?
6. Compare Sebo's principles with the research priorities in reading 04 (Eleos AI) and reading 07 (AI Cognition). Which principles do those agendas already follow, and which do they seem to neglect?
