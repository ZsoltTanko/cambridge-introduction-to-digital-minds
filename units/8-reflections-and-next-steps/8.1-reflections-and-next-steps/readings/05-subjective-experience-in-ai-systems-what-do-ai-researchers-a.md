## Purpose of the reading

The authors are based at the Centre for the Governance of AI, Oxford, NYU, UC Berkeley, Northwestern, the Colorado School of Mines and the University of Vermont. Their aim is to measure what AI researchers and the US public think about three things. First, whether and when AI systems with subjective experience might exist. Second, how we could tell whether they have it. Third, how such systems should be treated and governed.

The authors give three gaps as their motivation. Technical AI experts' views on subjective experience have rarely been surveyed. Almost nothing is known about what the public thinks developers and governments should do now. And most earlier opinion studies predate recent advances in AI, so the field needs up-to-date, repeatable measurement. The intended audiences are policymakers, AI developers and researchers working on AI welfare and governance, who need to know how the people who build AI systems, and the public whose trust matters, see the issue.

## Detailed summary

The assigned part is the abstract and executive summary. It is organised as key findings grouped under four headings, each followed by a set of short claims.

### Design and definition

The survey was fielded in May 2024. It included 582 AI researchers who had published at leading AI venues and 838 nationally representative US participants. Subjective experience is defined as "the ability to experience the world from a single point of view", covering perception and feeling, including pleasure and pain. An AI system with subjective experience would have an internal perspective, so that there is something it is like to be that system. The authors note that this definition covers both consciousness and sentience, which is a deliberate umbrella choice.

### Forecasts: when, and how likely

- **Fixed-date framing.** Respondents were asked how likely it is that an AI system with subjective experience exists in 2024 ("today"), 2034 and 2100. Median answers were 1% (researchers) and 5% (public) for 2024. For 2034 they were 25% and 30%. For 2100 they were 70% and 60%. Means were higher, at 12% and 16% for 2024 and 61% and 58% for 2100 (the summary cites means in parentheses).
- **Fixed-probability framing.** Respondents gave the year by which they thought there was a 10%, 50% or 90% chance. The median year at 10% was 2030 in both groups. At 50% it was 2050 in both groups. At 90% it was 2100 for researchers and 2075 for the public. Means were later, for example 2099 and 2172 at 50%.
- **Caveat on framing.** Participants could not answer "never" in this framing. The authors say this may have pulled the forecasts earlier, so they treat the fixed-date results as more reliable.
- **No significant timeline difference.** Across timeline questions, the two groups did not differ significantly.
- **Probability of never.** The public's median was 25% that such systems will never exist (mean 37%). Researchers' median was 10% (mean 29%). This is the one clear between-group difference on timing, and it is why the authors argue that timeline questions alone understate disagreement.
- **Confidence.** On a 0-100 slider with verbal anchors, both groups averaged about 52-53%, which the paper reads as "somewhat confident" on the verbal scale.

### Determining subjective experience

- **Detectability.** If such a system were built, both groups leaned slightly toward our being able to tell. Medians were 60% for both. Means were 57% (public) and 55% (researchers). The authors stress that these numbers sit close to 50%.
- **Confidence needed for moral consideration.** Respondents said how confident they would need to be before granting an AI system at least some moral consideration. Medians were 67% (public) and 65% (researchers), between the verbal labels for somewhat and moderately confident. The authors note the threshold sits just above the detectability estimate, though respondents probably anchored on the verbal labels, so this comparison should be read cautiously.
- **Whose expertise counts.** Both groups rated technical AI researchers, neuroscientists and psychologists, and AI ethics researchers as most important. Moral philosophers and philosophers of mind were slightly lower. Policymakers ranked lowest. The AI system's own outputs and the views of the public were rated slightly higher, especially by the public. The public gave significantly more weight to AI ethicists, the AI system itself and the general public than researchers did.
- **Capabilities that need subjective experience.** Thirteen tasks were rated, such as writing a bestseller, acting as a fair judge, serving as a therapist and teaching. A majority of the public said subjective experience is needed to match human-level performance as a therapist, a judge, or in generating complex and evocative artistic work. Majorities said it is not needed for the other capabilities the paper names, such as accumulating wealth, generating popular art, convincing online chat, developing a scientific theory, running a political campaign and teaching. A majority of researchers said none of the thirteen capabilities requires subjective experience. Both groups ranked the capabilities in a broadly similar order.

### Moral consideration and governance

- **Responsibilities.** Strong majorities in both groups said AI systems with subjective experience should be held accountable (74% researchers, 77% public), treat others well (81%, 85%), and behave with integrity, honesty and fairness (71%, 80%).
- **Rights.** Socio-political rights had the lowest support (29-36% agreement). Moral-patiency protections such as being cared for or protected by law drew moderate support (43-57%). The one significant between-group difference was computing rights (updates, maintenance, energy access, self-development). The public agreed at 49%, researchers at 35%.
- **Safeguards now.** 85% of the public and 68% of researchers agreed that developers should implement safeguards now. Most respondents rejected the idea that developers (77% researchers, 80% public) or governments (78%, 70%) should never introduce safeguards or regulation.
- **Government regulation.** Only the public showed majority support for regulation now (66%). Researchers were split (41% agree, 40% disagree). Both groups rejected never regulating.
- **Bans and building.** Only 15% of researchers and 31% of the public agreed that governments should ban such systems. A majority of researchers (66%) and a plurality of the public (41%) disagreed. On active encouragement, 41% of researchers and 27% of the public agreed that developers should actively try to build such systems. 33% of researchers and 46% of the public disagreed.
- **Internal disagreement.** Large minorities favoured autonomy in programming (29% researchers, 33% public) and some civil or political rights (29%, 36%). Views on these were divided within both groups.
- **Welfare for its own sake.** Nearly everyone (over 90%) supported protecting the welfare of animals and the environment for their own sake. About half supported the same for business corporations (47% researchers, 49% public). For AI systems with subjective experience, 46% of researchers and 43% of the public agreed, while 30% and 32% disagreed. For AI systems without subjective experience, 71% of researchers and 53% of the public disagreed.
- **Conjoint-style survey experiment.** Respondents imagined working at a tech company whose largest model might have subjective experience, under three conditions: control, explicitly no safeguards, and safeguards. Adding safeguards did not raise support above control. Explicitly stating there were no safeguards significantly lowered support. The public was less supportive than researchers overall, especially in the control condition.
- **Risk and benefit.** 59% of the public and 46% of researchers agreed that AI systems with subjective experience would be more dangerous to humanity than systems without it. The public agreed significantly more strongly. Views were split on whether such systems would behave more morally, and many respondents chose the neutral or don't-know options.

### Limitations (as the summary lists them)

- **Folk concepts.** Lay understanding of "subjective experience" and "consciousness" may diverge from expert usage, which complicates interpreting answers from non-experts.
- **Emphasis changes answers.** Stressing pleasure and pain made people less willing to ascribe such capacities to AI. Responses depend on which aspect is emphasised.
- **Unstable responses.** Few respondents will have thought about these questions beforehand, so answers may be formed on the spot and be sensitive to wording.
- **Stated views are not behaviour.** A single-topic survey can overstate how much people weigh this issue against other concerns, and does not reliably predict behaviour or policy support.
- **Forecasts are weak evidence.** Neither group has special expertise in predicting mental capacities in AI, so the forecasts show how people think, not how the technology will develop.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

The rest of the paper is in the main text and supplementary information. The main text adds the following.

- **Introduction (section 1).** It recounts the 2022 Google engineer claim that an AI system was sentient. It notes that the claim was mostly dismissed by the press and by experts, while some frontier-lab researchers have voiced real concern. It explains why subjective experience is central to most definitions of consciousness and sentience, and why both under- and over-attribution could be harmful. It also covers the carry-over effects of how people treat AI on human-human interaction, and the three gaps described under Purpose above.
- **Results (section 2).** These match the summary, with more detail on the two forecasting framings, the aggregated forecasts (median members put a 10% chance by 2030, 50% by 2050, and 90% by 2100), and the figures.
- **Discussion (section 3).** It repeats the three themes. It also argues that governance attitudes are not driven only by concern for a possibly suffering AI, and that people think such systems could be more dangerous. The discussion has one passage that conflicts with the results (see Critical perspective).
- **Future directions (section 4).** It calls for large multi-expert surveys, participatory processes such as citizens' assemblies, research on risk and benefit perceptions, monitoring of how capability advances shift views, and study of other traits such as agency and self-awareness.
- **Methods (section 6).** Researchers were drawn from authors who published in 2022 at NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI, IJCAI or in JMLR. Of 9,407 emails delivered, 6.2% completed the first question, giving 582. The sample was 85% men and 78% academic. The public sample was recruited through Prolific with quotas on sex, age, ethnicity and political affiliation, giving 838 respondents of whom 764 finished. Researchers were paid the equivalent of $50 and took a median of 30 minutes. The public took a median of 18 minutes. The survey had four randomised blocks.
- **Forecast fitting (section 6.3.1 and supplementary C).** Each respondent's answers were turned into a continuous distribution. The procedure tries a metalog first, then a Johnson quantile-parameterised distribution for fixed-probability answers, then a three-component skew-normal mixture, then a shifted log-normal. The first model that passes a tolerance check is kept.
- **Supplementary information.** It includes eight other mental capacities (perception, valenced experience, emotions, self-awareness, free will and others). It also includes the group-difference tables, demographic regressions, the top-line results and the full question wording. Two findings from this material are useful for the course. Over half of the public said most capacities other than subjective experience are required for consciousness and sentience, and about 47% of the public expected valenced experience and 52% expected emotions to never appear in AI. The supplement also includes a set of survey questions asked only of researchers about suffering sentient AI. 67% of researchers said such systems could become reality, and they gave a median of 40% that they would exist by 2100.

## Techniques & methods explained

**1. Dual-framing forecast elicitation.** Each respondent was asked about the same event in one of two ways. Fixed-date framing asks for a probability at set years (2024, 2034, 2100). Fixed-probability framing asks for the year by which a set probability (10%, 50%, 90%) is reached. Respondents were assigned to one of the two framings, so the design can test whether the wording changes the answers. Purpose: to check how sensitive forecasts are to framing, which earlier work on AI forecasting had found. Failure mode: the fixed-probability framing forces an answer at every probability and has no "never" option. This probably pushes its forecasts earlier, so the authors weight the fixed-date results more heavily.

**2. Distribution fitting for individual forecasts.** Each respondent gives only three points on a cumulative curve, which is too few to define a flexible distribution. The authors fit a sequence of models and accept the first that passes validation. Validation checks that the model's predicted cumulative probability at each elicited point is within a pre-set tolerance of the answer. The authors argue that the Gamma distribution used in earlier work has only two parameters and cannot fit three points. Failure modes: some respondents could not be fitted, and the procedure departed from the pre-registered plan for this reason.

**3. Aggregation across respondents.** Fitted distributions are converted to yearly probabilities from 2024 to 2124. These are then combined by taking the mean across respondents (sensitive to extreme forecasts) and the median (robust to them), separately for each group and framing.

**4. Group comparisons.** Each question was tested with an independent-samples t-test, adjusted with the Holm-Bonferroni procedure across all comparisons to control false positives. Median forecasts were compared with Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with continuity correction.

**5. Regression on background variables.** Regressions tested whether age, gender, religiosity, left-right political orientation and generative AI use predicted the key outcomes. Only generative AI use predicted researchers' forecasts, with more use going with earlier forecasts. Caveat from the authors: coefficients were affected by outliers, so the medians by usage level are more trustworthy.

**6. Survey experiment on safeguards.** A two-way ANOVA compared the three conditions across the two samples, followed by Tukey tests for pairwise differences. The design is a between-subjects vignette.

**7. Sampling methods.** Researcher recruitment used a scraped list of paper authors from leading venues, with neutral invitation emails that did not reveal the topic. Public recruitment used a commercial panel with quotas. Each method has known limits: participation bias among researchers, and bot and data-quality risks on online panels.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Subjective experience** - the ability to experience the world from a single point of view, including perceiving and feeling. For an AI system, it means there is something it is like to be that system.
- **Sentience** - subjective experience with positive or negative valence, such as pleasure and pain. The paper says subjective experience subsumes sentience and consciousness.
- **Consciousness** - used here as the capacity for subjective experience.
- **Moral consideration** - the idea that a being matters for its own sake, so decisions affecting it should take its interests into account. The survey asked about at least some such consideration, not full human-level status.
- **Moral patiency** - being the kind of entity that can be harmed or wronged and so is owed protection. Used in the paper for the care-and-protection items.
- **Socio-political rights** - rights such as expression, voting, citizenship and entering contracts. Least supported by both groups.
- **Computing rights** - the right to updates, maintenance, energy access and self-development. The one significant between-group difference.
- **Fixed-date vs fixed-probability framing** - two ways of eliciting the same forecast. One fixes the date and asks for a probability. The other fixes the probability and asks for a date.
- **"Never" probability** - a separate question on the chance that such systems will never exist. Asked because timelines alone hide disagreement.
- **Folk conceptions** - ordinary, non-expert understandings of mental concepts, which may differ from expert ones.
- **Welfare protection** - in the survey, protecting the rights, interests and/or well-being of a group, for its own sake.
- **Safeguards** - measures to reduce the likelihood of negative or painful subjective experience, or the harms from such systems.
- **Holm-Bonferroni correction** - a multiple-comparison adjustment that controls the family-wise error rate.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- **Forecast medians (fixed date, researchers vs public):** 2024: 1% vs 5%. 2034: 25% vs 30%. 2100: 70% vs 60%. (Reported in the executive summary and section 2.1.)
- **Fixed-probability medians:** 10% by 2030 for both groups. 50% by 2050 for both. 90% by 2100 (researchers) vs 2075 (public).
- **Never-probability medians:** 10% (researchers) vs 25% (public). The public mean is 37%.
- **Forecast confidence:** average about 52-53%, the middle of the verbal scale (somewhat confident).
- **Detectability:** medians of 60% for both groups. The authors note this is only just above even odds.
- **Confidence needed for moral consideration:** medians of 65% (researchers) and 67% (public).
- **Accountability for AI systems with subjective experience:** 74% (researchers) and 77% (public) agree they should be held accountable.
- **Safeguards now:** 68% (researchers) and 85% (public) agree developers should implement them now.
- **Ban:** only 15% of researchers and 31% of the public agree governments should ban such systems.
- **Welfare, AI without subjective experience:** 71% of researchers disagree it should be protected for its own sake, against 53% of the public.
- **Conjoint-style experiment:** explicitly saying there are no safeguards lowered support by about 0.9 points on a five-point scale. Safeguards did not raise support above control. Public support averaged about 0.29 points lower than researchers' overall.
- **Dangerousness:** 59% of the public and 46% of researchers agree such systems would be more dangerous to humanity.
- **Background-variable regression:** among researchers, higher generative AI use went with earlier median forecasts (medians: about 2075 for those who never use such tools, about 2043 for those who use them more than once a day). The authors treat this as indicative only.
- **Researchers on suffering AI (supplement):** 67% say suffering sentient AI could become reality. Their median estimate for 2100 is 40%, and their median for 2024 is 0%.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Stale data.** The survey was fielded in May 2024. The fixed-date item for "today" (2024) is now a historical question. The 2034 and 2100 items are still open, so those are the ones to test against later evidence. As of October 2026 I cannot verify how opinion or capabilities have moved since then, and I should not assume the numbers still hold.
- **The 2024 item is a sign of a deeper problem.** Respondents were asked to estimate something that the 2024 facts already answer (whether such systems existed in 2024). That item tests beliefs about a settled question, not forecasts.
- **Wording drives results.** Defining subjective experience broadly, or emphasising pleasure and pain, changes answers. The authors say so. This means headline percentages depend on the definition, and cross-study comparisons need matching wording.
- **Who the "researchers" are.** The sample reflects authors at leading venues in 2022: mostly academic, mostly men, and only 17% in industry. The paper's own discussion notes that only 17% work in industry, where frontier models are built. Any governance claim about "AI developers" therefore rests on a group that is mostly not building the systems.
- **An internal inconsistency to check.** The discussion says that 41% of researchers supported developers building such systems and that 33% favoured banning them. The results section reports that 33% of researchers *disagreed* with actively building such systems, and only 15% agreed governments should ban them. The 33% figure in the discussion appears to be a mislabel. A learner citing it should use the results-section figure.
- **Participation and data quality.** The 6.2% completion rate and the bot concern for online panels are reasons to treat small differences cautiously. The authors do not show that participation bias is absent, only that similar frames found little of it before.
- **Forecasts are weak evidence.** The authors' own caveat is the strongest one. The paper's numbers show that the year depends on framing: the 50% year is 2050 for both groups under the fixed-probability framing, but the mean answers for the same point run decades later (2099 for researchers, 2172 for the public). Cite the year only with its framing.
- **How other positions would push back.** Sceptics of AI moral status (for example Bryson, and Birhane and van Dijk, both cited in the paper) would say the public and researchers may be expressing intuitions rather than evidence, and that the welfare items mostly measure anthropomorphism. Biological-naturalist and consciousness-scientist sceptics (the paper cites Seth and Koch among others) would say the forecasts are unlikely to be reliable at all. Precautionary advocates (Long et al., Sebo and Long, also cited) would take the majority support for safeguards as the key result, and would argue that uncertainty is a reason to act now rather than a reason to wait.
- **What the survey cannot separate.** Support for safeguards might reflect concern for AI welfare, concern for humans, or simple risk aversion. The authors say this (governance attitudes are not solely about a suffering AI), and the paper's future-directions section asks for work that separates these drivers.
- **Changes since publication.** The paper cites the 2025 Claude Opus 4 and Sonnet 4 system card as an example of the technical sources that feed the debate. Lab welfare and model-assessment work has grown since then. I have not checked the specifics of later work for this summary, so treat any claim that the field has moved in a particular direction as unverified.

## Discussion questions

1. The timelines match between groups, yet the public puts a higher chance on "never" (25% vs 10%). What does that tell you about asking only "when" questions, and what would a better question look like?
2. The fixed-probability framing has no "never" option and likely pushes forecasts earlier. Which framing would you trust for a planning decision, and why?
3. Both groups rank technical AI researchers as the most important source of judgement, yet they rate the AI system's own reports low. Why might people discount self-report, and what kind of evidence would change your mind?
4. Respondents would need about 65-67% confidence before granting moral consideration, but they think detection is only about 60% likely. What does that gap imply for policy that waits for certainty?
5. A majority of researchers said no listed capability needs subjective experience. A majority of the public said therapists and judges do. What might explain the gap, and how would exposure to these tools change it?
6. Adding safeguards did not raise support above control, but stating there are none lowered it. What does this suggest about how a developer should communicate its welfare practices?
7. The researcher sample is 78% academic and only 17% in industry. How much weight should governance claims about "AI developers" get from this sample, and what sample would you want next?
8. Average self-rated confidence in the forecasts was only middling (about 52-53%), and the paper warns that answers may be formed on the spot. Given that, which single number from the executive summary would you be most comfortable citing, and what caveat would you attach?
