## Purpose of the reading

Caviola's stated aim is to establish a "social science of digital minds": a research field whose goal is to anticipate and understand how society and digital minds will interact. The document is an early-stage agenda, not a finished study. The author says he expects it to change significantly.

The intended audience is researchers in psychology, economics, philosophy and adjacent disciplines, plus funders and collaborators. The author invites collaboration and asks people to get in touch.

The core questions it answers are:

- What kinds of human-like AI assistants will be built in response to consumer demand and public opinion, and what roles will they have?
- Will people come to regard some AIs as sentient and therefore worthy of moral consideration?
- What legal, economic and political rights will people grant AIs, and what would the effects be?
- How can the AI rights debate stay well-informed, nuanced and alert to risk?
- How will digital minds intersect with other technological and social issues?

The underlying argument is that philosophers' views on AI sentience and rights are not the same as the public's, and that public and decision-maker views will likely shape outcomes. Because those views are "underexplored", the author argues they should be studied directly.

## Detailed summary

### Summary and motivation

The document opens with a scenario: a person finds out, after a video call, that they were talking to an AI. The author suggests this could be plausible soon. Philosophers ask whether AIs could be sentient and deserve protection, but the author argues the public and key decision-makers will likely determine the outcome, and their views may not match philosophers' views.

The author lists four high-stakes risks:

1. Failing to protect AIs that really are sentient.
2. Attributing sentience to systems that only seem sentient.
3. Granting AIs civil rights too early, before the social consequences are understood.
4. Setting off a divisive rights debate that worsens other technological and social problems.

He links to a separate article of his, "The Societal Response to Potentially Sentient AI", for more context. The agenda's stated purpose is to launch an interdisciplinary program drawing on psychology, economics and philosophy. The author notes that digital sentience in general is neglected, and that the descriptive question of how the public would respond is especially neglected. He credits Lukas Finnveden's project-ideas post, which already included the idea of studying what people think about AI sentience and rights, and says this agenda expands on it.

### Methodology

**Types of study.** The author plans a mix of methods:

- Empirical studies with the general public: opinion surveys, longitudinal studies of opinion change, psychological experiments on tendencies and effects, and qualitative interviews and deliberative engagement.
- Surveys of special samples, such as AI researchers, philosophers and policymakers.
- Forecasting with experts and insiders.
- Case studies, such as historical analogues.
- Scenario planning, meaning detailed and plausible future histories.

**Tractability.** Predicting how people will feel about AI is hard, particularly as technology shifts public sentiment quickly. Some questions are therefore currently hard to study. The agenda still lists them, on the view that they may become more tractable as people engage with the issue and as digital minds get closer.

**Why survey now.** The author takes a cautious position on survey data. He does not assume present attitudes will last. He argues current research is still worth doing for four reasons:

- Timelines are uncertain, so current data may matter if change is fast.
- Present data sets a baseline. Knowing whether 5% or 95% of people hold a view is informative about where opinion might go, even if it moves.
- Some patterns may be robust over time. If political factions already split on AI rights, the issue may become politicised later. Longitudinal studies show which opinions are stable.
- Even when current views do not predict future ones, the research builds expertise, methods and paradigms needed later.

### Concrete projects in progress

The author lists five projects he or his colleagues are working on:

1. A forecasting survey with experts on how, when and why digital minds might be created.
2. A citizens' assembly, or deliberative poll, with a representative sample. Participants get expert input and two days of discussion, then vote. Their consensus is compared with their initial intuitions and with philosophers' views. The author cites Birch (2024) as a model.
3. A survey of whether and when AIs should have autonomy and economic or civil rights, how sensitive people are to what AIs say they want, and individual differences in those views. The comparison group is Replika users.
4. Digital sentience skepticism: experiments on which features make people perceive sentience in AI.
5. A comparison of views in the US and China, among the public and among elite students.

The document is dated 2024. Whether these projects were completed, and what they found, is not stated and cannot be checked from the source.

### Project ideas: the market for AI companions

**Consumer demand.** The author asks how large the AI companion market could become, citing an ARK Invest report. He lists possible companion types: personal assistants, tutors, therapists, game players, nannies, caregivers, friends and romantic partners (Replika is the named example). He also includes mimics of real or fictional people, including "digital resurrection" of dead relatives, digital doppelgängers that mimic the user, whole-brain emulations, humanoid household robots and robot or virtual pets.

**Desired psychology.** Questions here include how human-like people want companions to be, whether they want companions with negative emotions, whether they want companions with complex desires such as autonomy, rights, or not being switched off, and how these preferences vary by relationship type and culture.

**Consumer risks and regulation.** The author expects public concern about first-order consumer risks (privacy, manipulation, emotional dependence, social isolation) to shape the market. He asks whether such concerns could block certain human-like features, how regulation might vary by country (China is the example), and whether consumer-risk regulation could be designed to also address sentience risks.

**Relationships.** The agenda asks how many people will engage with companions and for how long, whether they will form emotional or romantic bonds, whether that will make them care about the AI's wellbeing, whether they will see the relationship as real, and what the net effect on loneliness, beliefs, norms and behaviour will be. It also asks why people would prefer biological humans if digital minds are smarter, kinder and always available, and whether AIs will be seen as having moral responsibility, be trusted more if they seem human or sentient, and be given decision-making power as managers or leaders.

**Ethical consumerism.** The author asks whether consumers will pay to reduce AI suffering, whether they will give up features to do so, and how this would compare with fair trade or meat labelling. He raises "AI welfare washing": companies making AI companions look content when experts think they are not. He also raises a hidden-suffering analogy. People care for pets but overlook factory-farmed animals, so consumers may care about some AIs and not others. A related question is whether people will care how AIs are treated in training, or only in deployment.

### Project ideas: sentience and moral concern

**Perceived AI sentience.** The author asks why people are sceptical that AIs are or could be sentient. Candidate reasons include beliefs that biology is needed, dualism (AIs lacking non-physical properties), missing appearance or behaviour cues, and seeing AI sentience as naive, science-fiction or hyped. He asks which features shape attribution: reflexes, human-like language, memory, agentic behaviour, human-like or complex preferences, stable personality, sophisticated thought, embodiment (virtual or physical), brain-like structure, expressed emotion, and non-physical properties such as a soul.

**Philosophical models versus intuitions.** The author asks whether philosophical theories of consciousness match people's intuitions. He lists the contrasts: world models versus "stochastic parrots", recurrent versus feedforward processing, global workspace, unified agency and higher-order thoughts. He also asks what types of consciousness people attribute to AI (sensory, affective, cognitive, agentic).

**Inner mechanism versus behaviour.** Are people functionalist or non-functionalist? If an AI behaves exactly like a human, do people care about the difference in its internal mechanisms? Do people see an AI's expressed sentience as real or fake when it perfectly imitates a human on a video call? What about whole-brain emulations, which the author cites Anthis et al. (2024) for? Will people accept that some AIs could be sentient and others not?

**The nature of sentience.** Will people see sentience as all-or-nothing or as a spectrum? Will they distinguish kinds of sentience, such as physical from emotional pain? Will they accept that AIs could have experiences humans cannot understand, and value them?

**Interventions.** On the reduction side, the author proposes inoculation, pre-bunking and digital-literacy training. On the increase side, he lists persuasive philosophical arguments, physicalism, exposure to human-like AI, and social norms. He asks whether people quickly follow the consensus view. He also asks what can be learned from past cases where the set of entities granted sentience expanded, such as newborns and animals, drawing on Birch (2024).

**Moral patienthood.** Will people think digital minds deserve moral concern and good treatment? How much will they rely on sentience compared with intelligence, agency, desires, biology or perceived species? Does the modality (text, audio, video, VR, robot) change views? How important is it that AIs can achieve their goals? How much do people worry about suffering during reinforcement learning? Will they value digital minds for intrinsic welfare or only because the minds are someone's property or someone's emotional attachment? How strong is "pure substratism", meaning discrimination against digital minds only because they are not biological?

**Moral agency.** When will people see AIs as bearing moral and legal responsibility? If an AI causes harm, is the user, the company or the AI itself liable?

**Uncertainty.** How will people handle uncertainty about AI sentience? Who will be overconfident and who underconfident? How much do people support the precautionary principle, and what level of certainty do they think is needed before granting rights?

**Shifts over time.** The author asks which opinions will stay stable and which will shift, and suggests annual surveys, citing the Sentience Institute's AIMS survey. He asks whether people will move the goalposts for what counts as consciousness once AIs meet the earlier criteria. He floats a "moral inoculation" hypothesis: exposure to impressive but probably non-conscious current LLMs may make people less willing to attribute sentience to later systems. He lists possible turning points that could change perceptions quickly: human-like AIs with memory and consistent personality on video calls, AIs that mimic specific real people, highly human-like humanoid robots, job losses, publicised mistreatment of AIs by companies, AI-caused catastrophes and legal protections for AIs.

### Project ideas: rights

**Autonomy versus slavery.** Will people think AIs deserve rights beyond basic protection from harm? Will they regard owning AI labour as exploitation or slavery, or see AIs as tools? Will they think AIs should own their labour? Does the AI's expressed desire for freedom affect those views? Are people worried that AIs secretly want more freedom than they say?

**Disempowerment.** Are people worried that granting autonomy could lead to human disempowerment? How comfortable are they with treating AIs in ways they would reject for humans, such as deleting them for minor errors, surveilling them, or misleading them about their state? The agenda cites a 2024 finding (Reese et al.) that about 85% of respondents agree robots and AIs should be subservient to humans (paraphrased from "84.7%").

**Preferences.** Do people think it is wrong to build AIs whose only desire is to serve humans, the "happy slaves" case? Should AIs be allowed their own goals and the ability to reflect on and change them?

**Civil rights.** Will people think AIs deserve voting rights or legal personhood, such as owning property, making contracts or creating trusts?

**Existence and replication.** How will people think about "killing" AIs, which might include turning them off, pausing them, wiping memory, altering weights or limiting compute? How seriously will they take an AI's stated wish to keep existing? How will they think about restoring an AI? On copying: will AIs have reproductive rights? Is a branched copy the same identity or a separate one? Should both copies have voting rights? How should merging minds be treated? How should "mind theft" (unauthorised access to or manipulation of someone's thoughts) be treated? What about AIs that imitate real people, uploaded minds, and whether such copies should have the same rights as the originals?

**Economics.** How do people feel about AIs taking jobs? Should digital minds own their labour, be paid, or be able to sign contracts? Are people worried about AIs gaining economic dominance and producing a poverty or Malthusian outcome? Would people restrict AI in some jobs even at a cost in efficiency?

**Politics.** How will people view voting rights for AIs, and will they be concerned that AI votes, including those of copies, could threaten human society? Under what conditions would people defer to AI advisers, for example on science, forecasts or ethical questions?

**Population.** Will people want a limit on how many digital minds are created? How will they picture a future where humans and digital minds coexist? Will they want "super-beneficiary" minds with greater welfare capacity, and will they think such minds deserve equal or greater resources than humans? Will some argue for creating many digital minds optimised for high wellbeing?

**Respect.** Will people think AIs deserve respect even if they are not sentient? Will they find it wrong to hit or exploit robots regardless of sentience (citing Kate Darling, Cushman's harm aversion work and Grundke et al., 2023)? Why are people polite to chatbots? Are there parallels to sex-robot debates, where some hold that the wrong lies in the act even without suffering? Could promoting respect reduce future disagreement, since it depends less on sentience?

### Project ideas: social dynamics

**Disagreement.** How much will people disagree, and about what: sentience, the type of consciousness, moral patienthood, rights, or which AIs? What factions might form, and how do they correlate with political, religious, socio-economic or cultural variables? Could the issue become politicised, with one party pro-rights and the other against? How large could activist movements on each side become, compared with movements on animal rights or abortion? The agenda also asks for concrete scenario plans: nobody cares, everybody cares long-term, or a large disagreement that is temporary or permanent.

**Motives and incentives.** How much will investment interests in AI companies discourage rights? What would rights cost economically and in safety terms? How many people are more concerned about AI risk to humanity than about AI welfare? How much do AI safety and AI welfare supporters overlap? Could rights be seen as a national-security risk relative to China? How much do bias, ingroup favouritism and resistance to change drive reluctance? How much do emotional bonds with companion users shape views? How strong is rational ethical concern compared with other motives?

**Societal conflict.** How heated could the debate become, including protest, violence or war? What historical analogues (US Civil War, women's rights, animal rights, abortion) apply? Will AIs themselves take sides? How can risks be reduced: rational, compassionate and informed debate; focusing on deontological respect rather than sentience; not creating AIs with preferences their creators will not satisfy.

**Geopolitics.** How could the AI rights debate affect global geopolitics, particularly relative to China? Could a destabilising US conflict over AI rights affect the AI race? Could US-China disagreement over AI treatment escalate? Do Chinese people or CCP leaders attribute less sentience to AI?

**Experts.** What do philosophers of mind, cognitive scientists, neuroscientists, ethicists and AI researchers believe about AI sentience and rights? The agenda cites PhilSurvey, Francken et al. (2020) and Dreksler et al. (2024). Will expert opinion converge, and how confident will experts be? How far will the public differ from experts? Will the public trust intuition or expertise? Could social pressure push experts toward consensus? Will the public over- or under-attribute sentience compared with experts? Are compromise views available? What do expert groups and forecasters predict about when AIs could become sentient?

**Democracy.** How well can democratic methods reach consensus? The agenda asks how views shift after co-deliberation with experts, which expert arguments persuade people, whether a consensus could be reached and whether there would be enough support for it. It also asks whether people could agree on a set of reasonable theories even while disagreeing on which is correct (a "meta-consensus", citing Birch, 2024). Would people respond differently to lenient or stringent criteria for sentience? Would a big early public debate on AI rights go better or worse than a gradual, step-by-step approach?

**Policymakers.** What will policymakers believe about AI sentience and rights, and what incentives will shape their positions?

### Broader context and strategic considerations

**Risks.** The agenda lists the following risks of digital minds, including AI companions:

- First-order consumer risks (privacy, manipulation, social isolation).
- Wasting resources on non-sentient AIs by granting moral patienthood too broadly.
- Human disempowerment from granting rights recklessly.
- Destabilising conflict over rights, or over economic disruption.
- Authoritarian lock-in and mistreatment of particular groups.
- Incidental AI mistreatment, where sentient systems are denied adequate moral status or harms to them are not recognised.
- Intentional AI suffering, for example from retribution or sadism.
- Missed chances to create flourishing digital minds.

It asks what additional risks exist and how the risks interconnect.

**Related issues.** The agenda links the topic to misaligned AI, biotechnology, robotics, AI in warfare, AI surveillance, persuasive AI, AI regulation (national and international), economic disruption and inequality, and human and animal rights.

**Strategy.** The strategic questions are:

- When do AI safety and digital-mind welfare conflict, and when do they reinforce each other? Would slowing AI progress help both?
- When is the best time to start creating digital minds, and what kinds and quantities should be created?
- Is a gradual, flexible approach better, so that public reactions can be measured and strategies adjusted?
- Is it better to start a major debate on AI sentience early or to delay it?
- Would treating AIs well now give strategic advantages if humans lose control later?

**Proposals.** The agenda lists five candidate policies:

1. Slow down the creation of digital minds, or impose a moratorium (citing Metzinger, 2021).
2. Use deliberative democracy to set criteria and rules for creating and regulating digital minds (Birch, 2024).
3. Treat human-like AIs respectfully regardless of whether they are sentient.
4. Avoid creating AIs with preferences that humans are unwilling or unable to satisfy.
5. Refrain from creating morally ambiguous AIs, or tax their creation (Schwitzgebel, 2023; Bradley and Saad, 2024).

## Techniques & methods explained

**1. Public opinion surveys.** Surveys of the general public measure current views on sentience, rights and companions. Typical steps are: define the question, recruit a sample, measure attitudes, and compare groups by demographic and political variables. Use: establishing a baseline and finding which factions exist. Where it fails: the author notes that stated attitudes may not stay stable, and that survey data may not predict later behaviour.

**2. Longitudinal studies.** The same measures are repeated over time to see which opinions stay stable and which change. The author suggests annual surveys. Use: distinguishing robust patterns from transient ones and extrapolating trends. Where it fails: slow and costly, and it cannot foresee sudden shifts (the "turning points" the author lists).

**3. Psychological experiments.** Controlled manipulations test which features (memory, emotional expression, embodiment, human-like language, or inner mechanism versus behaviour) make people attribute sentience, and whether interventions such as inoculation or philosophical argument change views. Use: finding causal drivers of attribution. Where it fails: laboratory effects may not carry over to real-world companion relationships.

**4. Qualitative interviews and deliberative engagement.** Interviews and group discussions explore reasons behind views. The planned citizens' assembly works as follows: draw a representative sample; give it expert input and two days of discussion; have participants vote; compare the result with their initial intuitions and with philosophers' views. Use: identifying whether a reasoned consensus can emerge and what arguments persuade. Where it fails: small, expensive, and the consensus may depend on which experts are chosen and how the deliberation is framed.

**5. Surveys of special samples.** Experts (philosophers, AI researchers, neuroscientists) and policymakers are surveyed separately. Use: comparing expert and public views and measuring how far they converge. Where it fails: small samples and access problems.

**6. Forecasting with experts and insiders.** Structured predictions about when digital minds could be created and how the public will react. Use: anticipating timelines and reactions. Where it fails: the author notes that predicting human attitudes is inherently hard, especially as technology moves fast.

**7. Case studies.** Historical analogues, such as expansions of moral concern to newborns and animals, or the US Civil War and women's rights, are analysed for patterns. Use: suggesting how debates and attitude shifts might unfold. Where it fails: analogies between past cases and AI may be weak, which the author does not analyse in depth.

**8. Scenario planning.** Detailed, plausible future histories are written, such as "nobody cares", "everyone cares long-term", or a large temporary or permanent disagreement. Use: exploring the range of outcomes and stress-testing policy. Where it fails: scenarios are not predictions, and the agenda does not say how they would be weighted.

**9. The "why survey now" argument.** This is a reasoning technique rather than a method. The author argues that present-day data gives a baseline, that the direction of change is informative even if the level is not, and that the work builds methods that will be needed later. Use: justifying research on questions that are currently hard to predict. Where it fails: it does not show that any specific present measurement will matter.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Digital mind** - an AI or emulated mind that might have sentience or moral status, as used in the agenda.
- **Social science of digital minds** - the proposed research field studying how society and digital minds will interact.
- **Digital sentience** - the possibility that digital systems have subjective experience; the agenda treats it as neglected.
- **Perceived sentience / sentience attribution** - what people believe about whether AIs are sentient, which may differ from the facts.
- **Over- and under-attribution** - attributing sentience to systems that lack it, or denying it to systems that have it.
- **Moral patienthood** - being an entity that can be wronged or deserves moral consideration.
- **Substratism** - discriminating against an entity because of its physical substrate (digital versus biological).
- **Happy slave** - an AI built to serve with no other desires; the agenda asks whether people find this wrong.
- **Whole-brain emulation** - an exact digital copy of a human brain, as opposed to an AI that imitates humans.
- **Moral inoculation effect** - the hypothesis that exposure to impressive but probably non-conscious AIs makes people less willing to attribute sentience to later systems.
- **AI welfare washing** - companies making AI companions look content when experts think they are not.
- **First-order consumer risks** - ordinary consumer harms such as privacy loss, manipulation, emotional dependence and social isolation.
- **Deliberative poll / citizens' assembly** - a representative group that receives expert input, discusses, and then votes.
- **Pure substratism and meta-consensus** - the first is the discrimination concern above; the second is agreement on which theories are reasonable candidates even without agreement on which is correct (Birch, 2024).
- **Precautionary principle** - acting to avoid possible harm under uncertainty; the agenda asks how much people support it for AI sentience.

### Across the course

- **Unit 1 (Introduction and Theories of Moral Standing).** The agenda's questions about which features ground moral patienthood (sentience, agency, desires, biology) build on theories of moral standing. ## Critical perspective

*The following is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

**Assumptions.** The agenda assumes that public and decision-maker opinion is a meaningful object of study alongside the question of what is true. This is a defensible research position, but it has an implicit risk: if opinion is treated as a proxy for moral status, a population that is wrong about AI sentience could shape policy in ways that harm AIs or humans. The agenda's own risk list (over- and under-attribution, wasting resources, incidental mistreatment) shows the author sees this. It does not give a principled answer for how to weigh public opinion against expert analysis of the facts.

**Conceptual looseness.** Terms such as "sentience", "consciousness", "moral concern" and "rights" are used in ways that sometimes blur. The agenda asks whether people will distinguish types of consciousness and whether they will use sentience-based criteria at all. A philosopher would ask which of these concepts the survey questions actually measure. This is a standard difficulty in attitude research on consciousness and is not solved here.

**Stated attitudes versus behaviour.** Most proposed methods are surveys, vignettes or deliberation. People may say they would grant rights or respect to AIs and then not act that way, or the reverse. The agenda mentions the "polite to ChatGPT" phenomenon, which is a behavioural clue, but it does not propose a systematic behavioural method. Measuring behaviour (for example, in economic games or companion-use logs) would test attitudes more directly.

**Contested claims.** The claim that present-day public opinion research is worth doing because it provides a baseline is reasonable but not proven. The author concedes that predictions are hard. Whether a 2026 survey would still matter depends on how fast the field changes, which the agenda cannot settle.

**Dated information.** The document is an early draft from September 2024 with a last update in October 2024. Today is October 2026, so two years have passed. Some points are likely outdated: the scale and sophistication of AI companion products, the list of organisations, and the state of public discussion of AI consciousness. The course's own sibling readings already show later developments: the 2025 Subjective Experience survey, the 2025 Eleos AI research priorities, and the 2026 AI Cognition and governance pages. Anthropic and others also launched AI model welfare work during 2025, based on my own knowledge, which I cannot verify from the source. A reader in 2026 should treat the agenda as a map of questions, not a status report. The status of its five projects cannot be determined from the document.

**Empirical grounding of the hypotheses.** Several hypotheses are speculative: the moral inoculation effect, the goalpost-moving effect, and the claim that public disagreement could politicise the issue along party lines. They are plausible enough to test but are not supported by data in the document. The agenda should be read as a set of hypotheses to test, not as findings.

**Pushback from other course perspectives.** Jeff Sebo's principles (sibling item 2) emphasise pluralism and probabilistic thinking. Caviola's agenda fits that view in its methodological pluralism, but a welfare-first philosopher could argue that the agenda puts social acceptability ahead of moral truth, which is the reverse of what Sebo's principles recommend for research design. Conversely, a public-opinion researcher could argue that a welfare-first approach ignores what can actually be implemented, since policy is shaped by the public and policymakers. The agenda's strategy section (for example, its suggestion to avoid creating AIs whose preferences cannot be met) is the place where these two views would most clearly disagree.

**What the agenda does well.** It is unusually comprehensive as a list of questions, and it makes clear which questions are hard to answer now. Its methodological section is careful about survey limits. Its risk framing covers both under- and over-attribution, which many discussions omit.

## Discussion questions

1. The agenda argues that public and decision-maker views will probably shape the outcome for digital minds, even if philosophers are right about the facts. Is that a good reason to study public opinion, or does it risk letting popular belief substitute for moral truth? Where does the course's theory-of-moral-standing material (Unit 1) push back?
2. The author says a baseline of current opinion is useful even if attitudes change. Using the 5%-versus-95% example, what would you need to know before a single survey result could inform policy? What would a longitudinal design add, and what is hard about running one?
3. The citizens' assembly project combines expert input with two days of deliberation. What could make the resulting consensus misleading (for example, expert selection, framing, group dynamics)? How would you check whether the consensus reflects reasoning or social pressure?
4. The agenda lists features that might drive sentience attribution (memory, human-like language, embodiment, emotional expression, and others). Choose two. Design a simple experiment to test whether they change attribution, and identify the main confound.
5. The strategy section proposes slowing down, taxing or avoiding ambiguous AI creation, and also asks when safety and welfare conflict. Which of its proposals would you defend, and what would the strongest objection from the safety side be? Connect your answer to Unit 5.
6. The agenda raises "AI welfare washing" and the pet-versus-livestock analogy about hidden suffering. How likely is each, and what evidence would tell you? Is there a risk that public attention to companion AI diverts attention from other welfare problems?
