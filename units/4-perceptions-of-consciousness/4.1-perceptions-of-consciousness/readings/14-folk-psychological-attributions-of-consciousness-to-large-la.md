# Folk Psychological Attributions of Consciousness to Large Language Models

**One-line takeaway:** A survey of 300 US adults in July 2023 found that two-thirds gave ChatGPT some chance of subjective experience, that this attribution rose with how often people used it, and that people assumed others would attribute even more consciousness than they did themselves.

## Where this sits in the course

The page's learning outcomes cover the importance of public perceptions, the risks of under- and over-attributing consciousness, factors that drive those perceptions, how widespread they are, and the risk of societal division. The section's exercises ask learners to think about the impacts of consciousness misattribution on individuals, society and AI systems, and to connect the earlier units' theory (on moral standing, theories of consciousness, and evaluating mental states) to how people actually perceive AI.

The course guide for this reading is short: it says the paper "surveys data on consciousness attributions to LLMs." The reading is listed as **Further**, not Core, so it is supporting evidence rather than an argument the section depends on. Its role in the section is to supply the empirical baseline. Most other readings in 4.1 either argue about the risks of perceived AI consciousness (Seth, Suleyman, Caviola) or describe effects on users (the seemingly-conscious chatbot research). This paper answers a prior question: how many ordinary people already attribute experience to a chatbot, and what predicts it? It is the one reading in the set that measures this directly with a representative sample, and so it anchors the section's claim that perception is already widespread.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors (Colombatto at UCL and Waterloo, Fleming at UCL) want to know whether and how the general public attributes *phenomenal consciousness* (subjective experience, or "something it is like" to be the system) to large language models. Their motivation is twofold, as they state in the introduction:

1. Folk attributions of consciousness may shape future moral concern for AI whether or not the AI is in fact conscious.
2. Any future scientific determination of AI consciousness will likely be "theory-heavy" and expressed as probabilities, so its effect on public opinion depends on what people already believe.

The guiding question is therefore descriptive and psychological rather than normative: what do people think ChatGPT is capable of experiencing, which mental states go along with that belief, and how does it compare with expert opinion? The intended audience is cognitive scientists, experimental philosophers and policy readers, and the discussion explicitly ties the result to legal and ethical debates about AI status.

## Detailed summary

### Background and framing

The paper places itself in experimental philosophy and social psychology on lay mental-state attribution. It defines phenomenal consciousness as a state with "something it is like" to be that subject (Nagel 1974; Block 1995). It notes earlier work showing people attribute phenomenal states (feeling joy, getting depressed) and non-phenomenal states (making a decision, forming a belief). Gray and colleagues (2007) found two dimensions of mind perception, *Experience* (feel pain, fear, joy, pride) and *Agency* (self-control, morality, planning, communication), and the authors say the relation between these dimensions and phenomenality is unclear. The paper also notes that non-experts may not have a unified concept of phenomenal consciousness at all (Sytsma and Machery 2010; Sytsma 2014).

### Participants and procedure

- **Sample:** 300 US residents recruited through Prolific, using proportional stratified random sampling with age and gender quotas drawn from US Census data. The authors describe it as nationally representative. Final composition: 152 female, 142 male, 2 non-binary, 4 no answer; mean age 46.13. No one was excluded.
- **Timing:** July 2023, about eight months after ChatGPT's November 2022 release.
- **Priming:** Participants read a short description of ChatGPT, then a passage adapted from Peressini (2014) explaining "inner life" and contrasting humans, who are "experiencers", with thermostats, burglar alarms and bread machines, which are not.
- **Main question:** a 1 to 100 rating of how much of an "experiencer" ChatGPT is, with anchors at 1 ("clearly not an experiencer"), 50 ("somewhat an experiencer") and 100 ("clearly an experiencer").
- **Confidence:** a 1 to 100 rating of confidence in the main answer.
- **Public-opinion prediction:** a parallel rating of how much most people would think ChatGPT is an experiencer.
- **Mental-state battery:** 22 earlier mind-perception and consciousness-attribution studies yielded 254 stimuli, reduced to 65 unique mental states covering sensory experience (seeing, smelling), cognition (attention, self-control), emotion (feeling depressed or relieved) and complex capacities (acting morally, self-reflection). Participants rated ChatGPT's capacity for each on 1 to 100, with confidence.
- **Background:** heard of ChatGPT (yes/no), ever used it, frequency of use (from "more than once a day" to "about once a month"), and purpose (general knowledge, coding, writing, other).

### Result 1: most people grant some possibility of experience

A third (33%) judged ChatGPT definitely not an experiencer. The other two-thirds attributed some possibility of phenomenal consciousness. The distribution is summarised by mean 25.56, median 16.00, SD 27.36, range 1 to 100. Confidence and extremity were related in a quadratic way: people who gave extreme answers in either direction were more confident (quadratic B = 178.25, t = 7.30, p < .001), and a quadratic fit beat a linear one.

### Result 2: familiarity predicts attribution

Almost everyone (97%) had heard of ChatGPT, and 57% had used it. Past users rated it higher (M = 29.59) than people who had never used it (M = 19.37; t(287) = 3.33, p < .001). Across usage frequencies from "never" to "more than once a day", attributions rose linearly (B = 4.94 per step, t = 4.99, p < .001). The authors stress that this is a correlation. Familiarity could raise attributions, or higher attributions could lead people to use the tool more.

### Result 3: what drives attribution is experience, not intelligence

A principal component analysis of the 65 ratings yielded two main dimensions explaining 58% of variance. They matched Gray et al.'s "experience" and "intelligence" dimensions. Three findings follow:

- ChatGPT was rated as more capable of intelligence than experience. Mental-state scores correlated with the intelligence loading at r = 0.95 and with the experience loading at r = -0.94.
- Consciousness attribution tracked experience-related states (r = 0.65, CI 0.58 to 0.71) and not intelligence-related states (r = 0.03, n.s.).
- So although people see ChatGPT as clever, the states they associate with consciousness, such as feeling and sensation, are what move their consciousness judgment.

### Result 4: people overestimate public opinion

Predictions of others' views were correlated with people's own (r = 0.56), but were systematically higher (mean 41.11, median 39.50, SD 25.28; t(299) = 10.90, p < .001). Participants believed that most others would attribute more consciousness to ChatGPT than they did themselves. The authors read this as a perceived social consensus that may be larger than any real one.

### Discussion

The authors summarise the findings as a "substantial proportion" of people attributing some phenomenality to ChatGPT and believing others do too. They argue that this holds regardless of the academic debate, and that widespread uptake of powerful LLMs may be associated with public perception of some consciousness. They list limitations (single question, single LLM, single time point, US-only sample) and propose future work on longitudinal and experimental designs, on the features of AI that affect attribution (superficial appearance, behavioural profile, Bainbridge et al. 2011; Colombatto and Fleming 2023), and on perceiver traits such as spontaneous theory of mind. They end by linking the findings to policy, since folk ascriptions may drive social concern for AI systems.

### Conclusions

Most people are willing to grant some phenomenality to LLMs: a third say definitely not, and two-thirds say some degree. The authors say this is "somewhat surprising" given that neuroscientists and consciousness scientists estimate LLMs are highly unlikely to be conscious (Butlin et al. 2023; LeDoux et al. 2023). They conclude that there is a gap between folk intuition and expert opinion, with implications for the ethical, legal and moral status of AI.

## Techniques & methods explained

- **Single-item consciousness rating with a confidence follow-up.** The 1 to 100 "experiencer" rating is the primary dependent variable. Confidence is a second item, used to test whether extreme answers are more certain. The method is quick but depends on one wording.
- **Priming with a definition of phenomenality.** Participants are first given a short explanation of "inner life" and contrasted with thermostats and bread machines. This standardises the concept but may create it in people who would not otherwise consider it (the authors concede this).
- **Mental-state battery with dimension reduction.** Sixty-five mental states, drawn from 22 earlier studies, are rated and then reduced via PCA. The aim is to see which dimensions structure attributions and which predict the consciousness rating.
- **Correlating the consciousness rating with individual mental-state scores.** This shows which states the consciousness judgment rides on, rather than which states people assign to ChatGPT in general.
- **Predicting others' opinions.** A parallel rating of what most people would think is used to measure perceived consensus and to compare it with self-report.
- **Stratified sampling.** Quotas based on census age and gender are used to make a representative US panel; the sample size of 300 was set to guarantee at least 20 participants per stratum.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Aspect | Strengths (as the authors present them) | Limitations (as the authors state them) |
|---|---|---|
| Sample | Representative US sample, stratified by age and gender, no exclusions | US only; online panel; may not generalise to other cultures or to people less familiar with computing |
| Main measure | Simple, direct question about experience | One question; the scale may be read as credence, not degree (if read as credence, 23% say human-like experience is more likely than not) |
| Mental-state battery | Built from 22 earlier studies; 65 states | Self-report may not reflect how people judge others' minds; behavioural cues may matter more |
| Design | Links familiarity, mental states and public-opinion predictions in one study | Cross-sectional, so causality between usage and attribution cannot be determined |
| Concept | Shows folk and expert views diverge | Unclear whether non-experts have a concept of phenomenal consciousness at all; the priming may have shaped answers |
| Timing | Fresh snapshot of attitudes in 2023 | Attitudes may change as LLMs spread; the snapshot is dated |

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Phenomenal consciousness** - a state in which there is "something it is like" to be the subject; used here as synonymous with subjective experience.
- **Folk psychology / folk attributions** - ordinary people's intuitions about which minds have which mental states.
- **Experiencer** - the paper's term for a system with inner life; the scale runs from "clearly not an experiencer" to "clearly an experiencer".
- **Experience dimension** - the cluster of mental states about feeling and sensing (pleasure, fear, pain, pride).
- **Intelligence / agency dimension** - the cluster about knowing, choosing and planning; in this paper, the "intelligence" dimension is rated higher for ChatGPT.
- **Mind perception** - the broader study of how people attribute minds to agents.
- **Stratified random sampling** - a sampling method that draws participants within strata (here, age and gender groups) so the sample matches census proportions.
- **Quadratic regression** - a fit that allows a curved relationship, here between confidence and extremity of answer.
- **Principal component analysis (PCA)** - a method that reduces many correlated ratings to a few underlying dimensions.
- **Perceived consensus / overestimation** - believing others share a view more widely than they actually do.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- **33% of 300 respondents** said ChatGPT is definitely not an experiencer; **67%** gave some possibility of phenomenal consciousness.
- **Mean rating 25.56, median 16** on the 1 to 100 scale, with wide spread (SD 27.36).
- **97% had heard of ChatGPT; 57% had used it.**
- **Past users rated ChatGPT higher (29.59) than non-users (19.37)**, t(287) = 3.33.
- **Each step up in usage frequency added about 4.9 points** (B = 4.94, from "never" to "more than once a day").
- **Two dimensions explained 58% of the variance** in the 65 mental-state ratings.
- **Consciousness attribution correlated with experience-related states at r = 0.65 and with intelligence-related states at r = 0.03.**
- **People predicted others would rate ChatGPT at a mean of 41.11**, against their own mean of 25.56, with the perception gap significant at t(299) = 10.90.
- **23% of respondents would count as believing it more likely than not that ChatGPT has human-like experience**, if the scale is read as credence rather than degree (the authors' sensitivity check).

The authors' headline comparison is with experts. Neuroscientists and consciousness scientists, they note, consider LLMs highly unlikely to be conscious, which makes the public's two-thirds figure notable.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the authors' argument.*

- **Dated data.** The survey was run in July 2023, with ChatGPT of that time, and the paper was published in 2024. The course lists it as 2025. It is now October 2026. Usage levels, model capabilities and public familiarity have all changed a great deal since then. The headline figure (67%) should be read as a 2023 snapshot. Whether it has risen or fallen since is an empirical question the paper cannot answer.
- **Single-model, single-question design.** The whole result rests on one question about ChatGPT. The authors acknowledge that the answer depends on the wording and the scale, and their credence-versus-degree check (23%) shows how much the reading moves when the scale is reinterpreted. A reader should treat the exact percentage as fragile even if the direction is robust.
- **Priming.** Participants were given an explanation of phenomenality before answering. The authors admit this may have prompted a concept they would not have used otherwise. This matters because the study's main claim is about folk intuition, and priming may partly build the intuition.
- **Correlation, not causation.** The usage link could run either way: familiarity may increase attribution, or believing a system is conscious may lead people to use it more. The authors say so. The policy reading in the course should not assume the causal direction.
- **Public-opinion gap is a self-report about others.** The finding that people overestimate public opinion is based on people guessing others' answers. It is an interesting perception, but it is not evidence that the public as a whole believes this.
- **Contested concept.** The paper's inference that people hold a phenomenal-consciousness concept is contested. The authors note that some experimental philosophy (Sytsma and Machery 2010; Sytsma and Ozdemir 2019) suggests lay people lack a unified concept. The mental-state data are compatible with a common underlying concept but do not establish one.
- **Expert benchmark.** The comparison with experts assumes that expert opinion is the right yardstick. Consciousness scientists themselves disagree, and the paper's "discrepancy" is only as strong as the expert consensus it cites.
- **Pushback from other course perspectives.** Suleyman and Seth would likely say the high attribution rate is a warning sign, since it shows how easily the illusion takes hold. Caviola's animal-case paper would frame the same numbers differently, as an early, unstable stage of an attribution process that could settle either way. Experimental philosophers would press on whether "consciousness" was the concept respondents actually had in mind.

## Discussion questions

1. The paper finds that people who use ChatGPT more often attribute more consciousness to it. Which causal story is more plausible: familiarity raises attribution, or attribution drives usage? What study design would tell them apart?
2. The authors found that consciousness attribution tracked "experience" states but not "intelligence" states, even though ChatGPT was seen as more intelligent than experiencing. Why might people's consciousness judgments rely on feeling-related attributes? What does that imply about the features an AI system would need to display to be seen as conscious?
3. Participants guessed that the public would attribute more consciousness than they did themselves. What are the social consequences of a perceived consensus that is larger than the real one? How does this relate to the risk of societal division in Unit 4?
4. How much should a single-question, single-model, single-time survey shape policy? What extra evidence (longitudinal data, different models, different wording) would you need before using the 67% figure in a legal or ethical argument?
5. The paper's priming passage explains phenomenality before the main question. Does that priming make the result a measure of folk intuition, or of how people respond to a definition? How would you design a study that avoids the problem?
