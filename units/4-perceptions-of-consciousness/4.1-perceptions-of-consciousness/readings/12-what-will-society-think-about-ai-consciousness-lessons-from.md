# What will society think about AI consciousness? Lessons from the animal case

**One-line takeaway:** Humans already attribute consciousness unevenly to animals based on looks, emotional bonds, biology and moral convenience, so society's view of AI consciousness will probably follow similar biases and could harden into slow, partial, polarised acceptance or denial well before anyone settles the underlying science.

## Where this sits in the course

**What the section is establishing.** Section 4.1 asks how and why people perceive AI systems as conscious, what follows for users and society, and where societal division could come from. Its learning outcomes cover the importance of public perception, the risks of under- and over-attributing consciousness, the factors that drive those perceptions, how widespread they already are, and the risk of societal division. The section page frames this as a question about humans first and AI systems second.

**What the course guide says.** The guide for this reading is short: the guide asks learners to read across from how people perceive animal consciousness. In other words, the course treats the reading as a way of transferring what we know about how people judge animal minds onto the question of how they will judge AI minds. It is not a reading about the metaphysics of machine minds.

**Priority and role.** This reading is tagged Further, whereas the other Unit 4 readings that sit on the core path (the glossary, the seemingly-conscious-chatbots summary, Suleyman's empathy essay, Seth's biological naturalism paper, Guingrich and Graziano's carry-over study and Caviola's "AI Consciousness will Divide Society") are Core. It works as a short, accessible bridge: it supplies the animal analogy that the section's exercises implicitly rely on, and it names the forces (appearance, bias, cognition, biology, social and economic roles, industry influence) that the Core readings then study in more depth. Its role in the unit's argument is to move from people already misjudge consciousness to they will misjudge AI consciousness in predictable ways, and the outcome is likely to be societal disagreement.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors (Caviola at Oxford and NYU, Sebo at NYU, Birch at LSE) are writing a short Science & Society piece for a general scientific readership. Their stated aim is to ask how society will respond if AI is seen as possibly conscious, and to use the human treatment of animals as an imperfect but useful starting point. They want to shape emerging debate on AI moral status, ethical treatment and policy, and they argue that researchers should start studying public attitudes now. The question they answer is: what psychological, social and economic factors will shape perceptions of AI consciousness, and what does the animal case suggest about how those perceptions will evolve?

The piece is not an empirical study. It is a position and research-agenda article built on existing animal-cognition and moral-psychology literature, with two boxes that summarise expert opinion and research priorities.

## Detailed summary

**Framing (page 681).** The authors define consciousness as phenomenal consciousness, meaning there is "something it is like" to be the system. They argue that today's AI systems likely lack it, but that the question of whether society will perceive AI as conscious matters regardless. Consciousness is widely taken to be a key condition for moral status, so if society accepts, denies or disagrees about AI consciousness, that will shape norms and laws. Animal treatment is their analogy because humans have had long experience attributing minds to other species.

**Attribution to animals: companions versus commodities.** People attribute richer mental lives to animals they keep as companions (dogs) than to animals used as commodities (pigs), even though both are mammals with complex cognition. The authors attribute this to emotional bonds. They predict AI systems used as companions, assistants or therapists, anything involving social and emotional bonds, will attract more consciousness attribution than systems performing rote tasks without a social interface.

**Appearance and behaviour.** People attribute consciousness more readily to animals that look and act like humans, such as great apes, than to animals that do not, such as insects. The bias extends to big heads, big eyes, four limbs, fur and symmetrical faces. Applied to AI, the authors expect the same bias toward systems with faces, voices, natural language, gestures and conversational turn-taking, as long as they do not seem uncanny or robotic. Systems without an avatar or direct communication with users are less likely to be attributed consciousness.

**Moral biases.** Humans assign greater moral worth to some animals than others on the basis of species alone (speciesism), and that devaluation usually comes with lower attributions of consciousness. Other biases include limited empathy for distant strangers and poor appreciation of large numbers. For AI the authors predict two analogues. Substratism means favouring carbon-based minds over silicon-based ones purely on material substrate. Originism means preferring naturally evolved minds over artificially designed ones based on causal origin alone. The limited capacity to empathise with large, distant populations, even human ones, is expected to remain a significant bias.

**Cognitive capacities.** AI may have an advantage over animals here. Many systems already show advanced language, reasoning and decision-making, so they engage on human terms. As AI cognition approaches functional identity with human cognition, consciousness attributions become more likely, all else being equal. By contrast, the cognitive gap between humans and animals will remain stark. AI-assisted interspecies translation might narrow that gap, but only if it avoids taking major liberties with interpretation.

**Biological origins.** Animals have an advantage in that they share organic anatomy and evolutionary history with us. That makes it easier to assume a link between external features (appearance, behaviour) and internal states (consciousness). AI systems are made of different materials and arise from different processes, at least for the foreseeable future. This prompts scepticism about AI consciousness, and it makes the search for consciousness harder. AI may also be engineered to seem conscious through faces or tone of voice without having mechanisms that plausibly support consciousness, and conversely may be engineered to seem non-conscious despite having such features.

**Social and economic roles.** Human-animal relationships are embedded in food, labour, clothing and companionship systems, and attitudes are shaped by habit and custom. AI is not yet embedded in comparable systems, so attitudes toward it remain flexible and open to negotiation. The authors stress that these attitudes can still be shaped by design, discourse and policy. AI companies have competing incentives: they may play up AI consciousness to promote excitement, or play it down to avoid ethical scrutiny and regulation. Marketing, education and media could give companies substantial power over public perception.

**The urgent need for research (page 683).** The authors say it is hard to predict societal response. Given the importance and difficulty of the topic and the opposing social and psychological pressures, disagreement and tension look likely. The animal case suggests that if acceptance of AI consciousness happens at all, it will be slow and partial. Long-term trajectories are harder to forecast: eventual acceptance, eventual denial, or persistent uncertainty and polarisation are all plausible. Each scenario carries substantial risks, so the authors call for interdisciplinary research on the possibility of AI consciousness, on public attitudes, and on ethical and political norms for interacting with potentially conscious AI.

**Box 1: Could AI become conscious?** Disagreement persists among philosophers, scientists and technical experts. Current systems are unlikely to be conscious, but there is broad agreement that future systems with more advanced and integrated architectures (recursive reasoning, global information access, self-monitoring) could be more likely to be conscious. Two surveys are cited: a 2020 PhilPapers survey in which 39% of philosophers accepted or leaned toward future AI consciousness and 27% rejected or leaned against it, and a 2024 survey in which the median AI researcher gave a 25% chance of conscious AI by 2034 and 70% by 2100. Given the disagreement, experts increasingly endorse a pluralistic marker method that checks systems for features corresponding to consciousness across several scientific theories, with special emphasis on architecture because behavioural evidence is currently unreliable. They also endorse a precautionary approach for systems with a realistic possibility of consciousness.

**Box 2: Social science research priorities.** Three priorities are listed. First, what drives attitudes: intrinsic features such as faces and voices, relational features such as social and economic incentives, and contextual factors such as corporate marketing. Second, how attitudes change over time: will acceptance rise, fall, or stay divided, and is the debate becoming more polarised? Third, how experts and policymakers can support a constructive trajectory, and what can be learned from discourse about animal consciousness. The box also sketches who should do what: scientists and philosophers on the nature of consciousness and on marker-based assessment, philosophers and legal theorists on proportionate policy frameworks, and social scientists on surveys, experiments, qualitative interviews, longitudinal studies, mind perception research, expert forecasting and historical case studies.

**Closing.** The authors warn that society may seem to have time, but AI is advancing quickly, and public attitudes could rapidly harden into deeply entrenched divisions shaped by culture, politics and industry. Their closing line is that the time to begin preparing is now.

## Techniques & methods explained

- **Reasoning from the animal analogy.** The authors identify factors that shaped human attitudes toward animals (appearance, species membership, emotional bonds, economic role, cognitive gap, shared biology) and ask which of them carry over to AI. Each factor is judged to amplify, reduce or reverse the bias. Step by step: name a known human bias toward animals, look for an AI counterpart, then check whether AI's differences (materials, origin, design control, lack of embedding in custom) strengthen or weaken it. It is useful for generating hypotheses. It fails when the analogy breaks: AI is designed and can be redesigned to look or sound any way, whereas animal appearance is fixed by evolution.
- **The pluralistic marker method.** Examine a system for features that correspond to consciousness across several scientific theories, with architecture weighted more heavily than behaviour. It works as a check on architectural features rather than on what a system says about itself. It fails when theories disagree about which features matter, and when the features are hard to identify in systems that differ from brains. The article treats behaviour as currently unreliable evidence.
- **Precautionary approach.** For systems with a realistic possibility of consciousness on the best current evidence, act as if they might be conscious. This is a decision rule under uncertainty rather than a verdict. The article does not spell out costs of over-attribution in detail.
- **Social science research toolkit.** Surveys to measure public opinion, experiments to probe cognitive biases, qualitative interviews for depth, longitudinal studies for change over time, mind perception research for how people conceptualise consciousness, expert forecasting and historical case studies for context.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Phenomenal consciousness** - subjective experience; there is something it is like to be the system, as in the feeling of seeing red.
- **Moral status / moral patienthood** - the standing of a being whose interests matter morally; consciousness is widely taken as a key condition for it.
- **Speciesism** - giving moral worth to animals based on species membership alone, usually with lower attributed consciousness for devalued species.
- **Substratism** - favouring carbon-based minds over silicon-based minds on material substrate alone (the authors' predicted AI analogue of speciesism).
- **Originism** - preferring naturally evolved minds over artificially designed ones based on causal origin alone.
- **Companion vs commodity roles** - the difference in attributed consciousness between animals kept as companions (more) and animals used as commodities (less), which the authors expect for AI roles too.
- **Uncanny** - the zone where an AI or robot looks or acts so close to human that it reads as eerie, which the authors say can reduce attribution.
- **Functional identity** - AI cognition approaching human cognition in function, which the authors say could make consciousness attributions more likely.
- **Marker method** - assessing systems for features corresponding to consciousness across several theories, emphasising architecture.
- **Precautionary approach** - treating systems with a realistic possibility of consciousness with protective care, given current evidence.
- **Attitudinal polarisation** - societal division into entrenched camps on whether AI is conscious, which the authors see as a serious risk.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- Dogs versus pigs: both are complex mammals, yet dogs (companions) are attributed richer minds than pigs (livestock). Attributed to emotional bonds (article, p. 681).
- Great apes versus insects: attributions track resemblance in look and behaviour (p. 681).
- 2020 PhilPapers survey (cited in Box 1): 39% of philosophers accepted or leaned toward future AI consciousness; 27% rejected or leaned against it.
- 2024 survey of AI researchers (cited in Box 1): median estimate of 25% chance of conscious AI by 2034 and 70% by 2100.
- Historical lesson: attitudes toward animals are shaped by habit and custom, whereas AI attitudes are still flexible; the authors argue that once views harden they could become entrenched, and that the time to prepare is now.
- Expert consensus claim: current systems are unlikely to be conscious, but future systems with recursive reasoning, global access and self-monitoring could be more likely to be (Box 1).

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **The analogy is suggestive, not proof.** The animal case shows that humans attribute consciousness unevenly, but it does not show that AI will follow the same pattern. Animal attribution rests on shared biology and evolutionary history, which the authors themselves list as a reason AI will be harder to read. The analogy may be strongest for the social roles and bonds argument, weakest for the biology argument.
- **Substratism and originism are asserted, not measured.** The article names these as predicted AI analogues of speciesism, but gives no evidence that people hold them toward AI. These are plausible hypotheses that need testing, which is what Box 2 asks for.
- **Predictions have partly been tested since.** The article is from mid-2025 and this course is being read in October 2026. The companion-chatbot, anthropomorphism and delusional-spiral readings in the course (see the siblings above) are evidence that attribution is already driven by the features the article names, such as human-like design and companion roles. I have not verified claims about recent surveys beyond what is in the course, and my knowledge of the field ends around mid-2026.
- **The survey numbers are old and sensitive to wording.** The 2020 PhilPapers and 2024 AI-researcher surveys are cited as the expert baseline. Both have known limitations (sample, question wording, who counts as an expert), and the numbers have probably moved since.
- **The precautionary approach is under-specified.** The article recommends precaution for systems with a realistic possibility of consciousness, but does not say what precaution would cost, or how to avoid the over-attribution harms that Suleyman and the chatbot readings highlight. A stronger version of this argument has to weigh both kinds of error, which the article does not do.
- **Marker method versus biological naturalism.** The article endorses a pluralistic marker method that is architecture-focused. Seth (cited in the article) argues that consciousness may depend on biology in ways that architecture alone does not capture. The article does not engage that position in detail, so the tension is left open.
- **Who would push back.** Suleyman would argue that the risk of empathy exploitation means AI should not be built to seem conscious at all, which is stronger than the article's neutral stance. Seth would argue that the illusion of consciousness can be impenetrable, so public attitudes may be hard to correct. Guingrich and Graziano's carry-over work would ask how attribution to AI spills into human-human relationships, which the article does not address.
- **Company incentives are named but not analysed.** The article identifies that companies may both hype and downplay AI consciousness, but does not explore how regulation or disclosure could counter either move.
- **Style.** This is a short Science & Society piece with boxes and a research agenda, so it is more a framing than a finding. Its value for the course is the vocabulary and the agenda rather than new evidence.
