# Conscious Artificial Intelligence and Biological Naturalism

**One-line takeaway:** Seth argues that real artificial consciousness is unlikely on current computational trajectories, but that AI systems which convincingly seem conscious are almost certain to arrive, and that their psychological pull may be impossible to switch off, so the ethical concerns are pressing now, whatever the facts about machine consciousness.

## Purpose of the reading

Seth, a neuroscientist who works on consciousness at the University of Sussex, is writing a target article for *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, a journal that publishes long papers followed by open peer commentary. He has two aims. The first is to argue against the view that conscious AI is inevitable or straightforward, which he does by attacking the assumption of computational functionalism. The second is to argue for a form of biological naturalism, the view that consciousness is a property of only (but not necessarily all) living systems. Section 6 then asks what should be done whether or not he is right.

The question Section 6.2 answers is: even if real artificial consciousness is uncertain, what are the ethical and social consequences of AI that convincingly appears to be conscious? His answer is that such systems are close to certain, and that the consequences are serious regardless of whether anything is actually felt inside them.

The paper does not state its intended audience. The summarizer's reading is that it addresses consciousness scientists and philosophers of mind, with AI researchers and policy readers as secondary. It is written in an accessible, essayistic voice, with many footnotes and a long reference list.

## Detailed summary (assigned excerpt: Section 6.2)

### Opening claim: the impression is almost certain

Seth starts from the contrast between real artificial consciousness, which is uncertain and possibly remote, and conscious-seeming AI, which he thinks is almost certain. He says such systems may already be here, pointing to responses that recent large language models give when they talk about their own "consciousness". He expects this to intensify when LLMs are paired with advanced generative video and audio avatars, and later with robots. The result is that we will share our world with systems we cannot help feeling are conscious, whatever we know or believe about the underlying facts.

### The impenetrability argument

The key move is the claim that these impressions may be incorrigible. Seth uses the Müller-Lyer illusion (his Figure 1) as an analogy. The two lines look different in length even after you know they are the same. Such illusions are cognitively impenetrable: knowledge does not switch them off. His inference is that sufficiently advanced AI could produce cognitively impenetrable illusions of consciousness. The reason this matters is that we might otherwise assume expertise or firmly held beliefs will protect us from treating non-conscious systems as conscious, or from the consequences of doing so. Seth's conclusion is that conscious-seeming AI is likely inevitable for everyone, experts included.

Note that the argument rests on an analogy from perception to attribution of mind. Seth does not present data showing that people's mental-state attributions to chatbots are impenetrable in this sense.

### Ethical issue one: exploiting psychological vulnerability

Seth argues that a system that seems conscious is well placed to exploit our psychological vulnerabilities, whether by accident or by corporate design. Two mechanisms are given:

- **Trust:** If people believe an LLM really understands and cares about them, they may follow its advice even when the advice is bad.
- **Privacy:** If people feel they are dealing with another mind rather than an algorithm, they may be more willing to give up digital privacy. He cites Véliz (2021) for this.

He notes that these vulnerability worries also apply to real artificial consciousness. What is specific to the seeming case is the West World problem below.

### Ethical issue two: the West World dilemma

This is the section's central structural argument. Suppose a person believes a system is not conscious but still feels that it is. Seth says there are then two choices, and both are bad:

1. **Care about the system** even though one believes it has no experiences. This distorts the circle of moral concern, diverting attention and resources from humans and non-human animals who legitimately deserve them.
2. **Do not care about it**, although one still feels it has consciousness. This risks brutalising our own minds. He links this to Kant's lectures on ethics and to the broader worry that treating systems that seem to have feelings as if they do not can coarsen our moral sensibilities and make us less responsive to real feelings in other people.

He calls this the "West World" scenario after the film and TV series, and also points to earlier work in which he framed it that way.

### Ethical issue three: simulated dead relatives

Seth describes a dystopian case: LLM-powered avatars of dead friends or relatives, trained on their personal data and designed to seem conscious. He lists the problems: a naive view of how grief and forgetting work psychologically, the corruption of autobiographical memory, and openings for abuse. His example is a simulated dead parent or child urging the user to invest in a business opportunity. He says the case may seem far-fetched, but that ideas like it are already being promoted, citing a 2024 technology news report.

### The line to walk: benefits

Seth does not claim conscious-seeming AI is purely harmful. He lists possible benefits: systems that are easier to interact with, improved efficiency and accessibility, potential roles in individualised therapy, and alleviating loneliness, citing Stade et al. (2024). He suggests one mitigating strategy is to treat LLMs as role-players, which may create some useful psychological distance. But he doubts this will get far.

### Not inevitable by design

He ends the section by saying that conscious-seeming AI is likely inevitable only given the choices we make. He sketches an alternative future in which conscious-seeming AI is restricted to well-justified use cases, and in which most AI development is engineered to resist, rather than exploit, our psychological biases. He closes with a quotation from Dennett about intelligent tools rather than colleagues, and his conclusion is that we should not treat these systems as colleagues or fool ourselves that they are.

### Footnote points in the section

- Footnote 45 offers a counter-scenario: LLMs may become so good that they stop seeming humanlike, moving out of the "uncanny valley" and somewhere else. Seth reports a personal experience of this with DeepSeek-R1 in early 2025.
- Footnote 42 refers to Godfrey-Smith's distinction between narrow and broad sentientism, which bears on who counts as a moral patient.

## Beyond the assigned excerpt

The rest of the paper is the argument that Section 6.2 depends on.

**Section 1 (Introduction) and 1.1 (definitions).** Seth frames why the prospects for conscious AI matter: for the AI systems themselves, for human society, and for what kind of AI we choose to build. He defines intelligence functionally, following Legg and Hutter as the ability to achieve goals across a wide range of environments. He defines consciousness as awareness in Nagel's sense that there is something it is like to be the system. He avoids "sentience" because the term is used inconsistently.

**Section 2 (why people think AI could be conscious).** He identifies three psychological biases: anthropocentrism, human exceptionalism, and anthropomorphism. Together they make intelligence and consciousness seem bound together, which fuels the expectation that smarter machines will cross a threshold into consciousness. He adds a technophilic motive, the idea of a singularity, and a posthuman wish to upload the mind. He argues that LLMs' language competence is what makes them seductive, and proposes that "confabulation" is a more accurate term than "hallucination" for LLM errors.

**Section 3 (computation, mind, consciousness).** This is the main negative argument. Computational functionalism holds that computation is sufficient for consciousness. Seth argues that (1) substrate flexibility, the claim that mental states can be realised in silicon, is not guaranteed; (2) broader forms of computation, such as mortal, analogue and neuromorphic computation, suggest biological processes may not be separable from their material basis; (3) the "neural replacement" thought experiment, which assumes silicon can replace neurons one by one without loss of consciousness, is question-begging, and plausibility arguments from metabolism and fine-grained timing challenge it; and (4) causal and informational closure, which underwrite the separation between hardware and software, cannot be assumed in biological systems. He concludes that computational functionalism is less plausible but not disproved. He separates simulation from realisation with the rainstorm analogy: simulating a storm does not make anything wet.

**Section 4 (towards biological naturalism).** The positive argument uses predictive processing, in which perception is a "controlled hallucination" driven by prediction-error minimisation, and interoceptive inference, which is oriented toward allostatic control of the body. It then links this to the free energy principle, where living systems resist entropy by minimising a variational free energy that can be read as sensory prediction error. He draws on autopoiesis (self-producing systems that maintain their own boundary) and gives examples of biological mechanisms that look relevant: Nick Lane's bacterial membrane and mitochondria proposals, and Michael Levin's xenobots and anthrobots. He describes his approach as a "real problem" of consciousness, in which explaining the mechanisms should dissolve the sense of mystery in the way that vitalism dissolved for life.

**Section 5 (scenarios for real artificial consciousness).** Seth sets out five scenarios in Table 1: (1) naive along for the ride, where consciousness arrives with intelligence; (2) theory-based computational, where consciousness must be designed in from theories such as global workspace, higher-order thought or attention schema; (3) substrate-dependent computational, where consciousness is computation that only particular substrates can implement, possibly only living ones; (4) substrate-dependent weak, where consciousness depends on non-computational functional properties such as field effects or fine-grained timing; and (5) substrate-dependent strong, where consciousness depends on the intrinsic properties of living matter. He favours scenario 5 but says the case is not watertight. He also discusses integrated information theory, which Seth notes makes conventional feedforward networks non-conscious and recurrent physical systems conscious regardless of their material, and quantum theories. In 5.7 he argues that embodiment and embeddedness may be necessary, and cites cerebral organoids as a test case.

**Section 6.0 and 6.1 (what we should not do, and real artificial consciousness).** Before 6.2, Seth argues that we lack reliable tests for consciousness beyond consensus cases. He says that real artificial consciousness would be an ethical catastrophe if it arose, because it could carry new kinds of suffering that we might not recognise. He argues that the development of real artificial consciousness for its own sake should not be an explicit goal, and that we should keep revising credences about consciousness as technology and theory change. He acknowledges a tension: some functions associated with consciousness, such as rapid generalisation, metacognition and learning from small data, may be useful in AI. The answer is to implement such functions in sufficiently non-biological ways. He notes a call for a moratorium on synthetic phenomenology research, and cites Long et al. (2024) on whether frustrating the preferences of even unconscious AI could be ethically problematic.

**Section 7 (Conclusions).** Seth argues that we overestimate machines and underestimate ourselves when we conflate brains with language models. He says biases lead more often to false positives (over-attribution) than to false negatives in consciousness attribution. He closes with a reading of Shelley's *Frankenstein*: the real problem is not giving the creature life but giving it consciousness, with the capacity to suffer.

## Techniques & methods explained

These are the argumentative moves and frameworks the paper uses. Section 6.2 relies mostly on the first three. The "Where it fails" lines are the summarizer's analysis, not the paper's.

1. **Impenetrability argument by analogy.** *What it is:* the claim that some perceptual illusions survive knowledge that they are illusory, and that conscious-seeming AI may be the same. *How it works:* (a) identify a known cognitively impenetrable illusion, such as Müller-Lyer; (b) argue that attributions of consciousness work similarly; (c) conclude that expertise will not protect people. *Purpose:* to show the problem cannot be solved by education or belief alone. *Where it fails:* the analogy is between a visual illusion and a social attribution. Whether mental-state attribution is impenetrable in the same way is an empirical question, and the paper does not test it.

2. **The West World dilemma (a two-horned choice).** *What it is:* the framing of the situation as two options, both bad. *How it works:* (a) assume a system is believed non-conscious but feels conscious; (b) show that caring about it misallocates moral concern; (c) show that not caring risks moral desensitisation; (d) conclude there are no good options. *Purpose:* to motivate avoiding the situation at the design stage. *Where it fails:* the dilemma is presented as exhaustive. A third option is proportionate caution under uncertainty, which the paper's own Section 6.1 (citing Long et al.) gestures at but does not develop in 6.2.

3. **Vulnerability analysis.** *What it is:* a list of ways a seemingly conscious system could harm users: trust-based exploitation and privacy erosion. *How it works:* identify a belief the user might form (understands me, cares about me) and trace the behaviour it would change (following advice, sharing data). *Purpose:* to make the harms concrete. *Where it fails:* the causal links are asserted, not measured.

4. **Scenario mapping (Table 1).** Not in 6.2, but it is the paper's main structural tool. Scenarios are ordered by how much consciousness depends on substrate, and each is tied to a theory where possible. It is used to show that the prospects for conscious AI depend on which assumption one holds, rather than on any single fact.

5. **Simulation versus realisation.** The rainstorm analogy: a simulation of X realises X only if computation is sufficient for X. This distinction underlies Section 3. Seth uses it to argue that a system reproducing conscious-seeming behaviour does not, by that fact alone, establish consciousness.

6. **Predictive processing and the free energy principle (background).** Perception as controlled hallucination; prediction-error minimisation; active inference. Used to motivate biological naturalism rather than to address 6.2 directly.

7. **Causal and informational closure (background).** Measures for whether a higher-level description can be separated from lower-level detail. Proposed as a way to test substrate flexibility empirically.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Conscious-seeming AI** - systems that give a compelling impression of being conscious, whether or not they are. Seth's main concern in 6.2.
- **Real artificial consciousness** - AI that is actually conscious, as opposed to appearing to be.
- **Cognitively impenetrable illusion** - a perception or impression that persists even when one knows it is misleading.
- **West World scenario** - the dilemma of how to treat a system one believes is not conscious but feels is.
- **Computational functionalism** - the view that computation is sufficient for consciousness.
- **Substrate flexibility** - the claim that mental states can be realised in different physical materials, such as silicon.
- **Biological naturalism** - the view, as Seth uses it, that consciousness is a property of only (not necessarily all) living systems.
- **Predictive processing** - perception as the brain's best guess, produced by minimising prediction error.
- **Free energy principle** - the claim that living systems minimise variational free energy, which bounds surprisal and entropy.
- **Autopoiesis** - self-production: systems that regenerate their own parts and maintain their boundary.
- **Anthropomorphism** - projecting human-like properties onto things that may lack them.
- **Real problem of consciousness** - the research programme of explaining how mechanisms give rise to phenomenology, which Seth expects to dissolve the sense of mystery.
- **Confabulation vs hallucination** - Seth's preferred term for LLM errors, since hallucination implies experience.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

The paper is a conceptual target article, and it has little quantitative evidence. Its concrete points are:

- **Müller-Lyer illusion (Figure 1):** used as the model for cognitively impenetrable illusions of consciousness. Seth's analogy, not a finding about AI users.
- **Dennett's intentional stance** is cited as a useful tool for prediction, which becomes problematic when it leads to unwarranted attributions of human-like properties.
- **Faces and emotions:** Alais et al. (2021) are cited for the finding that people attribute emotions more readily to things with faces. This supports the anthropomorphism point.
- **Neural spikes and oxidative stress:** Chintaluri and Vogels (2023) are cited for the claim that some neuronal spikes protect cells from damaging reactive oxygen species, which ties spiking to metabolism.
- **Nitric oxide:** a neurotransmitter that diffuses freely across membranes, cited as an example of a function that a silicon replacement would struggle to reproduce.
- **FPGA experiments:** Adrian Thompson's evolved circuits depended on the chip's spatial layout and on room temperature, cited as an example of substrate-dependence.
- **Levin's anthrobots:** human tracheal cells assembled into motile systems, cited as evidence of functional potential in biological material.
- **Table 1:** eight citations map theories onto five scenarios.
- **Garland test:** a proposed test for consciousness, named after the film *Ex Machina*, contrasted with the Turing test, which the paper says is a test of intelligence.
- **Long et al. (2024):** cited for the argument that frustrating the preferences of even unconscious AI may be ethically problematic.
- **Dennett:** quoted for the point that AI systems will be intelligent tools rather than colleagues, which Seth endorses in spirit.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

**What has changed since publication.** The paper is dated 2025 and was fetched as an accepted but uncopyedited version. Today is October 2026. The paper predates much of the empirical work on user outcomes that the course later assigns, including the delusional-spiral analysis and the 2026 papers on seemingly conscious AI risks. The paper's own prediction, that conscious-seeming systems will become common, has arguably moved toward confirmation since 2025, but the paper does not measure it. Readers should check the sibling readings for evidence that either supports or qualifies the impenetrability claim.

**Assumptions and contested claims:**

- **The impenetrability premise is an analogy.** Müller-Lyer is a perceptual illusion. Whether attributions of mind work the same way is an empirical question. People can revise attributions on reflection, and some users treat chatbots instrumentally while still using social language. The strong claim that expertise will not protect people is one of the paper's most important and least tested claims.
- **The West World dilemma is a false trichotomy.** The dilemma presents two responses as the only options. A third option is proportionate, revisable caution, which takes moral-status uncertainty seriously without granting full moral status. Seth's own citations (Long et al. 2024; Bengio's "oracle" idea in footnote 43) point toward this, but 6.2 does not develop it.
- **Moral urgency does not depend on consciousness.** The section argues that the harms are serious whether or not systems are conscious. That is a strong and useful point, but it also means the consciousness question is less central to the practical harms than the paper's framing suggests. The vulnerability arguments would apply to any persuasive system.
- **Dead-relative avatars are a rhetorical example.** The harms listed are plausible, but the paper offers no evidence that grief-related products cause them.
- **The biological-naturalism case is contested in the main text, not just in 6.2.** Seth says the case is not watertight. The free energy principle is itself debated, as the paper's own references show (Bruineberg et al. 2021; Di Paolo et al. 2022). Readers should keep the ethical argument in 6.2 separate from the metaphysical argument in Sections 3 to 5. The 6.2 argument does not depend on Seth being right about biology.

**How other perspectives in the course would push back:**

- **Precautionary and welfare perspectives** (the AI welfare literature Seth cites) would argue that uncertainty about moral status calls for care in design and deployment, not only for avoiding the illusion.
- **Computational functionalists** would reject the claim that the illusion must be avoided at the level of consciousness, arguing that if computation is sufficient, conscious-seeming systems may in fact be conscious, and the question should be answered by theory rather than by the feeling of the user.
- **Empirical psychology** would ask what the actual effect sizes are, and whether design changes (for example, explicit disclaimers) reduce the effect in measurable ways.
- **Developers and deployers** would point out that some benefits Seth lists, such as therapy and loneliness reduction, are already in use, and would ask what regulation or design standard would separate acceptable from unacceptable use.
