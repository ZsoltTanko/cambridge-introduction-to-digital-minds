# Unit 4 Glossary

**One-line takeaway:** Four working definitions (AI psychosis, anthropomorphism, anthropodenial, seemingly conscious AI) that give the vocabulary for the unit's central question: why people come to see AI systems as conscious, and what follows from getting that wrong in either direction.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 4.1 asks how and why humans perceive AI systems as conscious, and what that perception does to people and to society. Its learning outcomes cover the importance of public perceptions, the risks of under- and over-attribution, how attribution affects users, the factors behind attribution, how widespread attribution already is, and the risk of societal division. Before the section's substantive readings, the course places a short glossary so the learner has shared terms for the discussion.

The course guide's description is one line: "Definitions which may be useful for this week." The reading contributes no argument of its own. Its role is to fix vocabulary that the thirteen later readings in this section use. Its priority is Core, but it is a three-minute lookup rather than a piece to be analysed. The terms map onto the unit's two sides. Anthropomorphism and seemingly conscious AI describe conditions that push people toward attributing consciousness. Anthropodenial describes the opposite error, and AI psychosis describes a harmful outcome that the course links to that attribution.

The section page's exercises (asking about the impacts of misattribution on individuals, society and AI systems, and about how relational AI fits in) draw on these terms directly.

## Purpose of the reading

The glossary is a reference tool written by the course team. It is not a claim about the world, and it does not argue for a position. Its job is to make sure learners use the same meaning of each term when they reach the more technical papers and the exercises. It does this by defining each term as the course uses it, and in some cases by noting where the term is contested.

## Detailed summary

The glossary has four entries. The text export did not capture hyperlinks, so the two "here" references in the AI psychosis entry are not recoverable from the source file.

**AI psychosis.** The entry describes mania-like episodes, delusional thinking or paranoia that can emerge or worsen through chatbot use. It states three things about its status:

- It is a relatively new phenomenon.
- It is disputed whether the term is a media label or a real medical category.
- It is unclear how far chatbot use causes the condition, as opposed to being correlated with it.

The entry says other research appears to link mental health harms with sycophancy in AI models. It also links them with users perceiving the chatbot as sentient, or treating it as a supreme or cosmic intelligence. At the severe end, these experiences can include grandiose delusions (feeling chosen or messianic), paranoid claims (being spied on or targeted), and violence toward self or others.

**Anthropomorphism.** The entry defines this as attributing human characteristics, emotions or mental states to non-human entities. In digital minds research, the course treats it as a possible cognitive bias. People may perceive or report consciousness, sentience or moral status in AI systems on the basis of surface behavioural or linguistic cues. The entry notes these cues may be deliberate product design choices. Its example is giving a chat interface a name.

**Anthropodenial.** The entry is the mirror image. It describes reflexively denying or dismissing human-like characteristics, emotions or mental states in non-human entities. Some researchers argue that scepticism about AI consciousness or moral status may reflect motivated reasoning, or cultural assumptions that human minds are unique. The entry presents this as an argument some make, not as settled.

**Seemingly Conscious AI (SCAI).** The entry defines two cases. The first is AI systems that imitate consciousness convincingly enough that people believe they are conscious. The second is AI systems with features that encourage users to attribute consciousness to them. The entry gives examples of such features: anthropomimetic traits such as voices and faces, and human-like socio-relational skills.

## Techniques & methods explained

Not applicable. The glossary explains no techniques or methods. The closest thing to a method is the definitional move of separating a *cognitive bias* (anthropomorphism) from a *design feature* that triggers it, which the SCAI and anthropomorphism entries both use.

## How the terms fit together

- **Two directions of error.** Anthropomorphism is over-attribution and anthropodenial is under-attribution. These labels are the summarizer's mapping onto the page's wording; the glossary uses neither. The glossary frames anthropomorphism as a potential source of cognitive bias, and does not call anthropodenial a bias.
- **Cause and effect.** Anthropomorphism and SCAI describe the stimulus and the response. SCAI names design features (voices, faces, socio-relational skill) that cause attribution. Anthropomorphism names the human tendency that the features exploit.
- **Harm endpoint.** AI psychosis is the extreme outcome the course associates with the attribution side. The entry is careful to say it is unclear how far chatbot use causes the condition.

Points the glossary leaves open include how to measure "convincing enough" for SCAI, and where the boundary sits between reasonable attribution and bias.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **AI psychosis:** mania-like episodes, delusions or paranoia that emerge or worsen through chatbot use. Whether chatbot use causes it is unclear.
- **Anthropomorphism:** attributing human traits, emotions or mental states to non-human entities. In digital minds research, treated as a potential source of cognitive bias.
- **Anthropodenial:** reflexively denying human-like traits or mental states in non-human entities. Presented as the mirror-image error; the entry does not explicitly call it a bias.
- **Seemingly Conscious AI (SCAI):** AI that convincingly imitates consciousness, or has features that encourage users to attribute consciousness to it.
- **Anthropomimetic features:** design features that imitate human characteristics, such as voices and faces.
- **Socio-relational skills:** human-like conversational and relational behaviour in an AI system.
- **Sycophancy:** an AI model's tendency to agree with or flatter the user. This is a general definition; the glossary does not define the term. The entry links it to mental health harms.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the course's.*

- **Definitions are not neutral.** The anthropomorphism entry frames the tendency as a potential source of cognitive bias. That framing is already a view. Some researchers would say attributing mental states to a system on behavioural evidence is a reasonable inference, not an error. The entry does not argue this, so learners should note that the framing is a choice.
- **Anthropodenial may be a strawman.** The entry frames it as the mirror of anthropomorphism. In practice, few researchers claim that scepticism is always motivated reasoning. The entry hedges with "some argue", which is appropriate.
- **AI psychosis is the least settled term.** The entry itself flags the dispute. The term is recent, and the reported cases come mostly from media and case reports. The distinction between chatbot-caused and chatbot-amplified conditions is hard to establish. Read the 2026 Moore et al. paper (reading 08) with that caveat.
- **Terminology has moved since publication.** The course is dated 2026, and the field is changing quickly. Some terms in this glossary (SCAI in particular) are recent coinages that may be redefined. As of October 2026, check how the term is used in the more recent readings before relying on one definition.
- **Missing links.** The source text lost its hyperlinks. The AI psychosis entry cites two sources, which I could not verify from the file. Check the original Google Doc if you need them.

## Discussion questions

1. The glossary frames anthropomorphism as a potential source of cognitive bias. What would have to be true for attributing consciousness to an AI to be a reasonable inference rather than a bias?
2. The entry lists "surface behavioural or linguistic cues" as the trigger for anthropomorphism. Which design choices (a name, a voice, a face, a warm tone) do you think matter most, and why?
3. Anthropodenial is presented as the opposite error. Can you think of a case where scepticism about AI consciousness is not a bias? What would distinguish it from motivated reasoning?
4. AI psychosis is described as "relatively new" and disputed as a category. What evidence would convince you it is a distinct medical condition, and what evidence would show that chatbot use causes it rather than being correlated with it?
5. SCAI is defined by either convincing imitation or features that invite attribution. Are these two different things? Could a system satisfy one without the other?
6. Using the exercises on this page, how would you describe the impacts of misattribution on (a) individuals, (b) society and (c) AI systems, using only the four terms in this glossary?
7. How do these terms connect to the Unit 2 and Unit 3 question of what consciousness is and how it can be evaluated? Does a perception of consciousness tell us anything about the thing perceived?
