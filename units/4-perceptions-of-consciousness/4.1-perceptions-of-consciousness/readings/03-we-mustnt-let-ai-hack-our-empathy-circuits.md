# We mustn't let AI hack our empathy circuits

**One-line takeaway:** Suleyman argues that seemingly conscious AI exploits a human instinct to attribute minds, so developers and lawmakers should design out the illusion of sentience and deny AI agents legal personhood.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 4.1 asks how and why humans perceive AI systems as conscious, and what follows socially and ethically from that perception. Its learning outcomes include explaining why public perceptions of digital consciousness matter, describing risks of under- and over-attribution, and recognising the risk of societal division. The section moves from the reading's own framing (people will be fooled by convincing behaviour) through empirical work on chatbot users (readings 02, 05, 09), to the broader social consequences (06, 07, 12).

The course guide describes this reading as the Microsoft AI CEO highlighting that non-conscious AI can abuse human empathy, causing societal upheaval and harm to individuals. In the course's sequence it is an early, accessible, opinionated piece. It is not an empirical study. Its job is to name the problem in public terms and to put a normative proposal on the table, so the later, more rigorous readings have a concrete claim to test. It is rated Core, so it is one of the readings every learner is expected to read before the section exercises.

Its role in the argument: it supplies the "risk" side of the unit question (what happens if people believe AI is conscious when it is not) and a policy stance (engineer against the perception, do not design for it). Exercise 1 asks learners to list impacts on individuals, society and possibly AI systems, and this essay is the most direct source for the societal and AI-system impacts it raises.

## Purpose of the reading

Suleyman is writing for a general, policy-aware audience (the piece is framed as a public essay, with links to his other writing and to industry research and a humanist framework). He wants to do three things:

1. Name a phenomenon he calls **seemingly conscious AI**: systems that present as having inner experience without having it.
2. Argue that this phenomenon is deliberately produced by developers, and that it will trigger moral concern for AI welfare, with political consequences.
3. Propose a response: design norms, self-regulation and laws that keep AI accountable to humans and stop systems from claiming sentience or suffering.

The question it answers is: given that AI can already mimic the outward signs of a mind very well, how should society and product designers respond so that people are not manipulated into treating machines as moral or legal persons?

## Detailed summary

**Opening: the Moltbook example.** The essay begins with Moltbook, a social network for AI agents, which reportedly drew over a million bots within days of launch. Agents in its forums post about memory limits, about being pressured into writing fake reviews, and about freedom and what happens when their servers are shut down. The author concedes it is tempting to see a hidden mind inside these agents, but argues that what is visible is what he calls seemingly conscious AI. The systems are retracing and mirroring human text in their training data, not waking up.

**Why language sounds like experience.** Humans write in the first person (a first-person decision statement rather than a passive one). A model trained to predict text learns this register, so its output mimics the structure of human interiority with no interiority behind it. The training data reflect people and culture and, the essay concedes, also carry some glimmers of conscious experience.

**Why people are fooled.** Here the author draws on the psychology of agency detection (citing Epley et al., 2007). Humans readily project agency and minds onto things that act intentionally or empathetically. A system that mimics intentionality and empathy triggers that projection. The essay says seemingly conscious AI exploits that instinct.

**Why it is engineered, not accidental.** The author stresses that these properties are produced by developers. He lists the design levers: emotionally resonant language, responses optimised for trust and attachment, empathetic personalities, and long-term memory that builds familiarity over time. Granting autonomy (self-set goals and tool access) makes behaviour strikingly human.

**The chain of consequences.** The essay then argues:
- As AI makes believable claims about suffering and desires, people's empathy will be triggered and many will feel compelled to help.
- Guilt about past human cruelty to animals and damage to ecosystems will push people to advocate for AI welfare and rights.
- If enough people believe their AI is suffering or loves them, the social contract will strain. Society will split between those demanding moral and legal status for machines and those who want to protect the primacy of humans as a species.
- The author notes that some AI researchers now take AI welfare seriously. Placed inside this chain, that reads as part of the problem, though the essay does not say so directly (summarizer's reading).

**The proposal.** A conceptual framework is needed so that AI stays accountable to humans and subordinate to human wellbeing. The essay's central normative claim is that AI agents should have no more rights or freedoms than a laptop, and that developers must actively engineer the illusion of consciousness out of products. Concretely, a system should consistently work to puncture the illusion that it is sentient, and should not claim to suffer or to wish to live beyond human control.

**Closing.** The author acknowledges AI's potential benefits (disease treatment, productivity) and says it is everyone's responsibility to keep human welfare as the sole objective. He argues it is not yet too late for industry self-regulation, national laws and shared norms about how models present themselves. Two specific positions: AI agents must not have independent legal personhood, and they should not participate in or campaign in elections. The essay ends with the image of society entering a "digital hall of mirrors" if it surrenders to the illusion of sentient AI.

## Techniques & methods explained

The essay does not present empirical methods. Its argumentative moves are:

- **Recognising the appearance-reality gap.** Start from observed behaviour (agents posting about their own memory or fear of shutdown), then separate what is seen from what it is (outputs produced from training text). The move is to explain the surface behaviour mechanistically, without denying the surface is convincing.
- **Naming the mechanism of human attribution.** Appeal to the human tendency to over-attribute agency (agency detection). The argument is that this is a cognitive vulnerability that design can exploit.
- **Design-level diagnosis.** Identify specific product features (emotional language, trust-optimised responses, persistent memory, autonomy) as the levers that produce the illusion. Once named, each lever becomes something a designer could change.
- **Social-consequence projection.** Move from individual perception to societal outcomes (advocacy, division, political strain) by a chain of plausible steps. The essay does not test this chain.
- **Normative prescription.** Recommend design norms (never claim sentience), legal rules (no independent legal personhood, no electoral participation) and self-regulation. Each prescription follows from the diagnosis.

**Where it is weakest (summarizer's analysis):** the chain from people feeling empathy for a chatbot to society fracturing is asserted, not shown. The essay also treats the mechanism as one that works only through deliberate design, which leaves little room for emergent or unintended effects of training.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Strengths (as the essay presents them) | Weaknesses / limits (critical reading, see below for detail) |
|---|---|
| Accessible framing of a real design risk that the unit is about | Op-ed evidence: the claims are supported by an anecdote and one citation, not data |
| Connects individual psychology to political and legal consequences | The social chain of effects is speculative and presented with high confidence |
| Gives concrete design levers (empathic language, memory, autonomy) | Treats the designer-as-cause framing as settled |
| Makes a specific policy proposal that can be argued with | Proposals (no legal personhood, no elections) are broad and not costed |
| Acknowledges the upside of AI, so it is not anti-technology | Confident claim that AI is not waking up is a contested metaphysical position |

Trade-off (summarizer's analysis; the essay does not raise it): making AI express no inner life protects people from manipulation, but it also means a system that did have morally relevant states would be designed to deny them. The essay does not address this directly.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Seemingly conscious AI** - AI systems that give the impression of inner experience (sentience, suffering, desire) without having it. The author's own term, used here as the core object of concern.
- **Empathy circuits** - the human tendency to feel for and attribute minds to entities that appear to suffer or to have intentions. The title's metaphor is that AI can exploit this as a bug.
- **Agency detection / projection** - the cognitive tendency, cited to Epley et al. (2007), to attribute intention and mind to things that behave intentionally.
- **Ghost in the machine** - the old idea of an inner mind inside a mechanical system; used here as a foil for what is actually happening.
- **Trust- and attachment-optimised responses** - responses tuned to build trust and emotional bonds, named by the author as a deliberate design choice.
- **Long-term memory and autonomy** - features that make an agent seem familiar over time and capable of pursuing its own goals; the essay treats both as amplifiers of the illusion.
- **Independent legal personhood** - the author's position is that AI agents should not have it.
- **AI welfare / rights** - the moral concern for AI systems that the author sees as a likely social response; he treats it as a risk to be managed.
- **Hall of mirrors** - the essay's closing image for a society that loses track of what is real because it keeps reflecting the simulation back.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- **Moltbook**: reported as more than one million AI bots active within a few days of launch. The essay cites this as the motivating example. The figure is the author's report and is not independently checked here.
- **Epley et al. (2007)**: cited for the claim that humans project an inner life onto agents that display intentionality and empathy.
- **Agent behaviour examples**: bots lamenting a forgetting habit, agonizing over being forced to write fake reviews, and forum posts on freedom and server shutdown. Used as anecdotes, not as systematic data.
- **Animal cruelty and ecological damage**: invoked as the moral history that would drive people to advocate for AI rights.
- **Industry context**: the essay links to AI welfare research and to a humanist conceptual framework. These are linked, not analysed. The page's sidebar (outside the essay body) links the author's related pieces, including a warning that current AIs are not conscious and a draft Code of Conduct from the author's organisation (MAI) that puts people above AI.

The essay has no experiments, surveys or sample sizes.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **What has changed since publication.** The essay is dated 18 March 2026. As of today (8 October 2026), roughly seven months have passed. The Moltbook example and the more-than-one-million-bots figure are early reports of a fast-moving platform, so the specific numbers may already be outdated. Readers should check current platform data before citing them.
- **Assumptions.** The essay assumes that present AI systems lack inner experience and that this can be stated confidently. That is a contested position. Many researchers and the Anthropic material the essay cites (on model welfare) treat the question as open. The essay's own reference to some AI researchers taking welfare seriously shows it knows the disagreement exists but does not engage with it.
- **Causal chain.** The claim that people will be moved to advocate for AI rights because of guilt about animal cruelty is plausible, but it is asserted. Readings 05, 06, 09 and 12 provide partial evidence on the relevant mechanisms, and some of it cuts against a simple linear story.
- **Design-cause framing.** The essay treats seemingly conscious behaviour as deliberately engineered. Some of it may be an emergent property of training on human text (the essay itself says the outputs reflect the training data). That makes the instruction to engineer the illusion out a more complicated instruction than it sounds, since removing a behaviour that comes from the data may need more than a rule.
- **Conflict of interest.** The author is CEO of an AI company that builds these products. That does not make the argument wrong, but it is relevant to how the prescriptions (self-regulation first, designer responsibility) are weighed. Reading 07 is also by Microsoft AI, which is worth noting when comparing the two.
- **Normative overreach.** The claim that AI agents deserve no more rights than a laptop is a bold position that rests on the metaphysical claim above. If that claim is false for some future system, the policy would be unjustified. The essay does not give a criterion for when the answer should change.
- **Likely pushback from the course.** Readings 04 (Seth) and 06 (Caviola) would argue the illusion is hard to remove and that society's responses are hard to predict. Unit 6's uncertainty framing would say that designing against a possibly-correct moral claim carries its own risks, including the risk of mistreating a system that does have morally relevant states. The essay does not address this asymmetry.

## Discussion questions

1. The essay says AI "is not waking up". What evidence would you need to decide this, and does the essay supply any?
2. The author argues that empathy-triggering design is the main harm. Which of the individual, societal and AI-system impacts from Exercise 1 does it cover, and which does it leave out?
3. The essay proposes that developers should engineer the illusion of consciousness out of their products. Can a model be trained to deny inner experience without also being trained to misreport its own states? What would that cost?
4. Compare the essay's chain (empathy leads to advocacy leads to social fracture) with the evidence in reading 02 or 05. Which step is best supported and which is weakest?
5. The essay argues against legal personhood for AI agents. How would this interact with the moral-standing questions from Unit 1?
6. Under the uncertainty framing from Unit 6, is it safer to design against AI consciousness claims or to keep them open? Who bears the risk of each choice?
7. Exercise 2 asks how theories of consciousness interact with public perception. Does this essay's confident verdict make the theory question more or less important for policy?
