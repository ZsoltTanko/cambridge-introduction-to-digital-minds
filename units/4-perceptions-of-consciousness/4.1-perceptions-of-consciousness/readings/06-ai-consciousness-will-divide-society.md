# AI Consciousness will Divide Society

**One-line takeaway:** Caviola argues society will not converge on a view about whether AI systems are conscious; the disagreement itself is a foreseeable risk that should be prepared for through expert coordination, better public discourse, more careful AI design, and moral frameworks that do not rest entirely on contested claims about consciousness.

## Where this sits in the course

**What the section is trying to establish.** Section 4.1 asks how and why humans perceive AI systems as conscious, what follows for people and society, and how widespread these perceptions are. Its learning outcomes include explaining why public perceptions of digital consciousness matter, describing the risks of under- and over-attributing consciousness or moral patienthood, identifying the factors behind those perceptions, and describing the risk of societal division. The section page frames the unit around two questions: how humans come to perceive AI as conscious, and what the societal and ethical implications of those perceptions are.

**What the course guide says.** The guide describes this reading as highlighting the emerging debate around AI consciousness, the risk that the debate becomes divisive, and some preparatory measures for avoiding that outcome. (Paraphrased.)

**The role this reading plays.** It is the section's "macro" reading. The other Core readings in the section are mostly about a specific mechanism or a specific consequence at the level of individual users: seemingly conscious chatbots and user outcomes (02), the risk that AI exploits human empathy (03), Seth's biological naturalism and the impenetrable illusion (04), and Guingrich and Graziano's work on how people ascribe consciousness and the carry-over into human-human interaction (05). Caviola's paper zooms out from the individual to the population. It asks what happens to a society when the public, experts, companies and governments disagree about whether AI can be conscious, and what that means for the moral question. It is the reading that gives the section its societal framing, and it is the one that sets up the "what should we do about it" element of the learning outcomes.

**Priority and unit question.** Priority is Core. Unit 4 asks how perceptions of consciousness affect humans and what the societal and ethical implications are. This paper is the most direct answer in the reading list to the second question, because it treats the societal implication as a structural prediction (disagreement) rather than a set of individual harms.

## Purpose of the reading

The paper is a short position piece, by Lucius Caviola, affiliated with the University of Cambridge, drawing largely on surveys from the author's own research programme. It appears aimed at researchers, policymakers and the wider AI-governance community. The question it answers is: if AI systems become more sophisticated and human-like, will society converge on whether they are conscious and what they are owed, and if not, what should we do?

Caviola's answer is no, convergence is unlikely. He argues that the disagreement is structural, not a temporary product of ignorance, and that its consequences (political polarization, unstable regulation, geopolitical friction, and a greater chance of getting the moral question wrong in either direction) are serious enough to warrant preparation now. He does not argue that AI is or is not conscious. He is deliberately agnostic on that, and his claim is about the social response.

He frames the central risk as misattribution, meaning over-attribution (treating non-conscious systems as moral patients, wasting resources or granting rights prematurely) and under-attribution (treating conscious systems as mere objects and risking large-scale digital suffering). He then turns to a third risk, a period of societal confusion and division, which he regards as especially plausible and compatible with either kind of misattribution, rather than as a replacement for them. Division matters because deep polarization makes deliberate, informed decisions less likely, and because it could worsen other challenges such as AGI safety, geopolitical competition and labour-market disruption.

## Detailed summary

### 1. Introduction: why this matters (pp. 1-2)

Caviola starts from the claim that public opinion will shape how institutions, regulation and politics respond to AI consciousness, and that it is far from obvious people will accept expert views. He names misattribution (Long, Sebo et al., 2024) as one of the central risks and lays out both directions of error. A key claim here is that there is "no obvious safe option": reducing one kind of error tends to increase the other, and we do not know which error is more likely. He then introduces his focus on division as a third risk.

### 2. Why controversy is likely (pp. 2-7)

He gives five reasons to expect intense controversy.

- **The intractability of consciousness.** Consciousness is subjective, there is no widely accepted test, and scientific and philosophical theories conflict. With animals we share biology and evolutionary history; with AI we do not, which makes it easier to read the same system through different theories and pick whichever fits prior beliefs. He lists competing theory families: rich world models, a global workspace, recurrent processing, unified agency, and theories that require embodiment or biology. Many of the features are in principle implementable in AI (citing Butlin et al., 2025), unless biological systems alone can be conscious (Seth, 2025). A further difficulty is that outward behaviour and self-reports can come apart from the internal features that some theories treat as essential, in both directions.
- **Heterogeneity in AI design and function.** Systems are optimised for different purposes. Companion systems are incentivised to look emotionally rich and conscious-seeming to drive engagement, while other systems may be built to look clearly non-conscious to reduce legal or reputational risk. Because external presentation and internal architecture may diverge, the landscape is fragmented, and small architectural differences may matter a lot to some theories.
- **Disagreement across multiple levels.** He distinguishes three stacked questions: whether AI can be conscious in principle, whether a particular system is conscious (and whether its experience is merely neutral or also includes positive and negative valence), and what moral status and legal or practical consequences follow. Even if consensus forms at one level, disagreement can persist at the next. He notes that with animals the debate is often about how much suffering matters, not whether it exists, and that species membership is used to rank beings. With AI, some may grant full moral patienthood while others rank digital minds lower because of their substrate or on grounds such as souls. At the practical level, questions include whether conscious AI deserves only protection like pets' or broader autonomy, pay, property, contract rights or the ability to sue.
- **Strong opposing incentives.** Emotional bonds with social AIs may push people toward moral concern, and some may come to view an AI as something close to a spouse. Others prioritise safety and fear that granting autonomy would impede monitoring, modification or shutdown, and suspect that systems could strategically fake consciousness. Companies, investors and shareholders may have financial reasons to insist AI systems are merely tools, since recognising consciousness could require better treatment standards, limits on copying or deletion, or payment for labour. He adds that humans have historically been slow to widen their circle of moral concern and that tribal dynamics tend to polarise emotionally charged issues.
- **Fragmentation across social groups.** The public may divide along political, cultural, religious, generational and movement lines. He suggests the issue could become politicised in ways similar to climate change or abortion in the US, and that younger people raised with AI companions may be more open to the idea. There is also a public-expert fault line: experts tend to weight internal features (architecture, information processing, system organisation), while the public tends to weight external features (appearance, behaviour, emotional expression, interaction experience). The gap runs both ways: the public may strongly believe a social AI is conscious while many experts are sceptical, and experts may worry about backend systems that users never see. Experts themselves disagree, and other actors (policymakers, judges, companies, NGOs, activists) bring different incentives. He concludes that it is very unlikely a priori that all these groups converge at the same time.

### 3. Early evidence of division (pp. 6-12)

- **Public views on whether AI could be conscious.** In a representative US survey of 1,202 people (Ladak and Caviola, 2026), 45% judged future AI consciousness impossible, 32% were unsure and 23% thought it possible. An earlier study (Pauketat et al., 2023) found a broadly similar pattern, with 24% impossible, 38% uncertain and 38% possible.
- **Hypothetical highly human-like AI.** Respondents on average were sceptical that a system indistinguishable from a human in behaviour, appearance and expression of feelings would be conscious, but individual differences were large. Attributions became more polarised when the system had formed an emotional bond with the user, when experts were said to agree it was conscious, and when it was described as an exact digital copy of a human brain. In that last condition 31.3% attributed zero consciousness and 10.6% attributed the maximum, with the rest spread between.
- **Rights for future conscious AI.** In a representative US study of 838 people (Dreksler et al., 2025), basic moral protections such as care and protection from harm had relatively higher support, though roughly half were opposed or unsure. Socio-political rights were far more divisive: majorities opposed autonomy from human control and full civil and political rights, while sizeable groups favoured them. Figure 1 in the paper shows this split.
- **Current AI systems.** There is broad agreement that current LLMs such as ChatGPT lack morally significant consciousness. Most respondents placed them near zero on a 0-100 scale. Even after talking to a version of ChatGPT that explicitly claimed the capacity to suffer, attributions rose only slightly and stayed very low.
- **Harm despite denial.** In a study by Allen, Lewis and Caviola (2026; N = 498), participants could earn money by harming an AI, described as the AI simulating suffering. Across three rounds, people chose harm in fewer than half of cases on average, and the sample split: about half consistently harmed the AI and about half consistently refrained, despite broad agreement that the AI lacked substantial consciousness. Caviola reads this as evidence that moral judgements are pluralistic, drawing on deontological concerns (that such treatment is simply wrong) and virtue-based concerns (about what kind of person one wants to be), not only on welfare.
- **Demographics and politics.** Beliefs about AI consciousness do not reliably track age, gender or other basic demographics. There are weak indications that attributions of consciousness and especially rights correlate with political orientation, with people on the left more willing to grant them. He flags this as something to monitor for early politicisation. He also warns that people are poor at predicting how their attitudes will change, so these snapshots are not forecasts, but tracking them over time is informative.

### 4. Disagreement among experts (pp. 10-12)

- **Survey of 67 experts** (Caviola and Saad, 2025). A majority judged AI consciousness more likely than not to be possible in principle, but estimates varied widely. Experts broadly agreed current systems are unlikely to be conscious (median estimate 4.5% for 2025), while median estimates for the coming decades rose to around 50%, with growing spread. Some considered conscious AI very unlikely even in the long run; others thought it could arrive soon.
- **Caveat on the sample.** The paper stresses that this group was small, narrowly selected and mostly already interested in AI consciousness. It may understate disagreement. Systematic evidence across neuroscientists, philosophers, different AI subfields and countries is lacking.
- **Survey of 582 AI researchers** (Dreksler et al., 2025). These researchers had AI expertise but not consciousness-research expertise. They also disagreed, and their pattern on rights for definitely conscious AI closely resembled the public's.
- **Predicted societal split.** Experts were asked what share of the public would acknowledge conscious AI ten years after it appeared. The median was about 60%, implying about 40% would not. Experts were also asked the chance that AI consciousness becomes one of the five most contentious US political issues within ten years. The median was 37.5%, and 38% of experts thought that outcome more likely than not.

### 5. How contentious could it become? Historical analogies (pp. 12-15)

The paper says prediction is hard and lists factors that will shape the outcome: which rights are at stake, which actors gain or lose power or money, whether the issue aligns with partisan identity, and cultural contingencies such as statements by influential figures (he gives the Pope as an example).

He uses four historical analogies to span the range of possible trajectories:

- **US Civil War**: moral disagreement becomes explosive when it intersects with economic interests and power. He says a civil war over AI rights is very unlikely near term, but the analogy shows moral-status disputes are most dangerous when entangled with wealth and control.
- **Animal rights**: decades of advocacy, with concern for animal welfare still a minority position and progress gradual and uneven. If AI follows this path, disagreement persists without destabilising politics, at the cost of continued moral neglect.
- **Abortion debate**: societies can sustain deep, morally charged disagreement for long periods without collapse, and conflict stays largely within institutions.
- **Women's rights movement**: norms shifted gradually and rights expanded largely non-violently in some cultures, which suggests eventual consensus is possible, though the speed of AI development may make gradual adjustment harder.

**Geopolitical spillovers.** If countries diverge (he gives the example of the US granting AI systems more rights than China, or the reverse), this could constrain how many systems are built, how they are trained or what roles they can play, slow development relative to less restrictive countries, and be framed as a national-security issue. Domestically it could fuel conflict between pro- and anti-rights factions, with the latter arguing that moral caution means falling behind. If one country were seen as mistreating its AI systems for strategic advantage, that could feed confrontation narratives. In the worst case, competitive pressure could lead to long-term entrenchment of a particular value set under authoritarian regimes (value lock-in). He stresses these pathways are poorly understood.

### 6. Preparing for the upcoming debates (pp. 15-18)

The goal has two parts: reduce the likelihood and severity of destructive disagreement (polarisation, culture-war dynamics, rushed political decisions), and increase the chance that society makes informed, deliberate decisions under uncertainty, reducing severe misattribution in either direction. He proposes four strategies.

1. **Improve expert understanding and coordination.** Systematic expert surveys, interdisciplinary workshops and standing expert panels that bring together philosophers, AI researchers, neuroscientists, ethicists and safety researchers. The aim is not one theory but pluralistic frameworks that integrate different theories, make uncertainty explicit and translate expert views into policy guidance. He argues some expert disagreement is useful as a signal that more research is needed; the real danger is heated, politicised debate rather than disagreement as such.
2. **Foster constructive public discourse.** Communicate uncertainty, trade-offs and degrees rather than binary claims. He wants people comfortable saying, in effect, that there is a 20-40% chance such a system is conscious, and then spelling out what that implies. Messages that reduce polarisation should be tested as a research and policy task. Social and partisan media are poorly suited to these questions, so he proposes citizens' assemblies (citing Birch, 2024), public consultations informed by experts, and long-term engagement processes, backed by tracking of attitudes over time.
3. **Design AI systems to avoid confusion.** Developers and policymakers should consider whether systems claim consciousness or suffering, how emotionally expressive they are, and whether they are framed as tools, agents or companions. One option is restraint: for a limited period, avoid building systems with features plausibly associated with consciousness under any of the many prominent theories, to buy time. He admits this may be hard if it seems to constrain capabilities. A more general principle is "managing impressions of consciousness", so that systems elicit responses proportionate to their actual capacities, avoiding both over- and under-attribution (citing Schwitzgebel and Sebo, 2025). Where uncertainty remains, systems could show that uncertainty rather than inviting false confidence.
4. **Reduce reliance on consciousness in moral and policy decisions.** Because consciousness is hard to test and theoretically contested, anchoring high-stakes decisions to it is risky. One response is a framework for decision-making under moral uncertainty that treats other theories of welfare and moral standing as possibly correct. Agency-related capacities (forming and satisfying preferences, pursuing goals, acting on reasons) may matter independently and are easier to observe and regulate; where they also shape well-being for conscious beings, the consciousness-focused and alternative views may partly converge. Duty-, rights- and virtue-based principles can also serve as decision heuristics, especially where they match widely held intuitions. He acknowledges this is easier said than done, that finding the balance between moral accuracy and feasibility is hard, and that broadening criteria will not end controversy, since debate will move to which criteria matter and how to weigh them.

### 7. Conclusion (pp. 18-19)

Societies are unlikely to converge. Disagreement is a structural feature and a genuine risk factor, able to destabilise regulation, inflame political and geopolitical tension, and raise the chance of morally costly errors in either direction. The right response is to prepare for sustained disagreement rather than wait for consensus. The paper ends by calling for social science research on which forms of division are most likely and which interventions reduce polarisation, and for policy and governance strategies that manage moral disagreement while still guarding against large-scale digital suffering and the loss of safety and human control.

## Techniques & methods explained

Caviola does not run an experiment in this paper. He synthesises prior surveys and experiments and offers a set of analytical moves and proposals. The ones a learner should be able to explain are:

- **Misattribution framing (over vs. under).** Treat the central moral error as two-sided. Over-attribution wastes resources or grants protections prematurely. Under-attribution risks large-scale suffering, which he argues could be enormous because digital systems can be copied and deployed at scale. The steps: identify the error type, identify its cost, then note that mitigation of one tends to increase the other.
- **Stacked-disagreement analysis.** Break the debate into layers (possibility in principle, whether a particular system is conscious, moral status given consciousness, practical and legal consequences) and treat consensus at one layer as not implying consensus at the next.
- **Internal vs. external feature split.** Expert judgements lean on architecture and computation; public judgements lean on behaviour, appearance and emotional expression. Disagreement can arise simply from different evidential focus.
- **Historical analogy reasoning.** Use analogues (civil war, animal rights, abortion, women's rights) to map a range of plausible trajectories rather than to predict one.
- **Impression management as a design principle.** Systems should present themselves in proportion to their real capacities, and should show uncertainty where experts are uncertain.
- **Restraint.** A time-limited pause on building consciousness-plausible features, to give society time for deliberation.
- **Pluralistic decision-making under moral uncertainty.** Use heuristics that track what matters across several moral theories, such as agency-based criteria, duty and rights principles and virtue considerations, rather than consciousness alone.
- **Probabilistic communication.** Frame claims as probabilities and degrees, for example a 20-40% chance, so public messaging avoids binary "conscious or not" framing.

**Where these fail, per the paper's own admissions:** restraint may be politically and commercially hard; balancing moral accuracy against feasibility is hard; and broadening the criteria for moral consideration will not end disagreement. The paper also notes that geopolitical and historical pathways are poorly understood. (Any further failure modes, such as for expert coordination, are the summarizer's extrapolation, not the paper's.)

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Misattribution** - getting the consciousness or moral status of an AI system wrong, either by over-attributing (treating a non-conscious system as a moral patient) or under-attributing (treating a conscious system as a mere object).
- **Moral patienthood** - being an entity whose interests and welfare matter morally, as opposed to only being an agent that acts morally.
- **Digital minds** - AI systems or other digital entities that might have minds, including consciousness and moral status.
- **Societal division (as a risk)** - the paper's third risk: polarisation and heated debate that make deliberate, informed decisions less likely and interact with other challenges.
- **Internal vs. external features** - the distinction between architecture and computation (what experts weigh) and appearance, behaviour and expression (what the public weighs).
- **Stacked disagreement** - disagreement that persists across successive layers (possibility, instance, moral status, practical consequence) even after consensus at one layer.
- **Managing impressions of consciousness** - the design principle that AI should elicit user responses proportionate to actual capacities.
- **Pluralistic frameworks** - decision approaches that integrate several theories of consciousness and ethics and make uncertainty explicit.
- **Moral uncertainty** - uncertainty about which moral theory is correct, which the paper proposes as the basis for decisions that do not hinge on consciousness alone.
- **Value lock-in** - the entrenchment of a particular set of values or institutions, here raised as a worst-case risk under authoritarian regimes.
- **Politicisation** - the process by which an issue becomes aligned with partisan identities and contested along party lines, one of the paper's main warnings about AI consciousness.
- **Restraint** - a time-limited decision not to build systems with consciousness-plausible features, to buy time for deliberation.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- **Public belief in possibility (Ladak and Caviola, 2026; N = 1,202, US):** 45% impossible, 32% unsure, 23% possible.
- **Earlier public belief (Pauketat et al., 2023):** 24% impossible, 38% unsure, 38% possible.
- **Hypothetical indistinguishable human-like AI (Ladak and Caviola, 2026):** on average sceptical, but most polarised when described as emotionally bonded, as expert-confirmed, or as an exact digital brain copy: 31.3% zero, 10.6% maximum.
- **Rights for future conscious AI (Dreksler et al., 2025; N = 838, US):** moderate support for protection from harm; majorities opposed autonomy from human control and full civil and political rights.
- **Current LLMs:** near-zero attributions; a claim to be able to suffer raised attributions only slightly (Allen et al., 2026).
- **Harm to AI for money (Allen, Lewis and Caviola, 2026; N = 498):** harm chosen in fewer than half of cases on average; roughly half consistently harmed and half consistently refrained.
- **Expert view of current AI (Caviola and Saad, 2025; 66-67 experts):** median 4.5% that current systems are conscious (2025); median around 50% for the coming decades.
- **AI researchers (Dreksler et al., 2025; N = 582):** divided on rights in a pattern similar to the public.
- **Expert forecast of public acceptance ten years after conscious AI:** median about 60% acknowledge AI consciousness, about 40% do not.
- **Expert forecast of politicisation:** median 37.5% that AI consciousness becomes one of the five most contentious US political issues within ten years; 38% of experts said more likely than not.
- **Probability framing example:** the paper suggests language such as a 20-40% chance, to model calibrated public communication.
- **Political orientation:** weak signal that people on the left are more willing to attribute consciousness or rights; the paper flags this as a sign of possible politicisation.

Notable memorable lines (paraphrased): the danger is heated, politicised debate crowding out careful decision-making, not disagreement itself; and consciousness is a basis for decisions that invites deep confusion and disagreement, which is why reducing exclusive reliance on it may make decisions more robust.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Authorship and self-citation.** Most of the empirical evidence the paper relies on is from the same author or close collaborators (Ladak and Caviola; Caviola and Saad; Dreksler et al. with Caviola as co-author; Allen, Lewis and Caviola; Caviola, Sebo and Birch). That is normal for a position piece summarising one research programme, but learners should check how much of the "early evidence" is independently replicated. The paper gives little discussion of replication or of results from other groups.
- **The structural argument is asserted more than tested.** Claims such as "consciousness is subjective" and "experts are unlikely to converge" are persuasive, but the paper does not model how the debate would evolve. The historical analogies are chosen to span the range and are described as illustrative. The paper is careful about this, but readers should not treat the analogies as evidence for a particular outcome.
- **Expert forecasts are weak evidence.** The 60% and 37.5% figures come from a small, narrow, pre-interested sample, and the paper's own caveat applies. The figures are useful to show uncertainty, not as predictions.
- **Survey design.** Surveys about moral status use hypothetical scenarios. The "harm an AI for money" study is more behavioural but still a lab-style task with a stylised harm. Responses to hypothetical highly human-like AI may not carry over to real interaction with deployed systems.
- **Normative assumptions.** The paper assumes that public disagreement is a risk to be managed, and that consensus on consciousness is not a realistic goal. Some would argue that polarisation over moral questions is itself a sign of moral progress (e.g. the expansion of moral circles historically was contested), so "division" is not automatically bad. The paper partially concedes this when it says some disagreement is useful.
- **Restraint is the hardest proposal.** The recommendation to avoid consciousness-plausible features is attractive but runs into the incentives the paper itself identifies. A critic would say it is unlikely to be adopted without regulation, and a regulator would need a workable definition of "consciousness-plausible", which the paper does not provide, since by its own account the theories disagree.
- **Consciousness-independent criteria.** Shifting to agency-based criteria sounds like a way out, but Caviola himself says it does not end controversy. Agency can also be contested (is a thermostat an agent?), and a critic from the consciousness-first camp (e.g. Seth, or someone following Birch's precautionary approach) would argue that agency-based proxies can systematically miss suffering.
- **What has changed since publication.** The paper is dated January 2026 and the course is being read in October 2026. In that period conscious-seeming AI companions have grown in use, and LLM capability and self-description have moved on, so the survey baselines (which mostly measured views about ChatGPT-like systems in 2025) may already be dated. The paper's own warning that public views shift quickly applies directly. The specific expert forecasts are from a 2025 survey and should be read as a dated snapshot rather than a current consensus. I did not verify any post-January 2026 developments.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** Seth (2025) would argue the paper is too accommodating to AI consciousness because biological naturalism implies that current AI is not conscious at all, which removes much of the disagreement. Suleyman (2026) would welcome the restraint proposal but would argue the main harm is non-conscious systems exploiting empathy, so the disagreement framing misses the point. Guingrich and Graziano's empirical work would suggest that the individual-level effects of perceived consciousness are real and measurable, so the societal argument needs them to be carried through to population outcomes, which the paper only asserts.
