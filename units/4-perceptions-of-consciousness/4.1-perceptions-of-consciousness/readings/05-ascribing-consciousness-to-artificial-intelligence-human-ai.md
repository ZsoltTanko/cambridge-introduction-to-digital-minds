# Ascribing consciousness to artificial intelligence: human-AI interaction and its carry-over effects on human-human interaction

**One-line takeaway:** Whether AI is conscious matters less than the fact that people ascribe consciousness to humanlike AI, because those attributions can carry over into how people treat each other, so the authors argue for regulating AI's effect on users' prosociality rather than the treatment of AI itself.

**Assigned part:** the **Discussion** section only (the course guide asks learners to read only the Discussion). The source is a Frontiers in Psychology "Hypothesis and Theory" article, roughly 14,000 words. The Discussion is short and sits near the end, after the two evidence and mechanism parts (Parts 1 and 2). It takes its evidence from those parts, so this file summarises the Discussion in detail and also explains the Parts 1 and 2 claims it depends on.

## Where this sits in the course

**What the section is trying to establish.** Unit 4 asks how and why people perceive AI systems as conscious, and what the societal and ethical consequences are. The section's learning outcomes include explaining why public perceptions of digital consciousness matter, the risks of under- or over-attributing consciousness, how consciousness or moral patienthood attributions affect users, which factors drive those perceptions, how widespread they are, and the risk of societal division. In page order, the readings run: a glossary, empirical work on users (reading 2), a warning about manipulation of empathy (reading 3), a neuroscientific-philosophical argument about the illusion of AI consciousness (reading 4), this paper, and then forward-looking pieces on division and risk (readings 6 onward).

**What the course guide says about this reading.** The guide asks for the Discussion only. It does not paraphrase a thesis; it just points the learner at the part of the paper where the authors move from evidence to recommendations.

**The role of the Discussion in the argument.** The Discussion is where the paper stops describing mechanisms and asks what society should do. It makes three moves:

1. It argues that perceived consciousness has moral and social weight regardless of whether AI is actually conscious, because mind perception drives moral thinking.
2. It compares two families of response: enforcing how people treat AI, and regulating AI so that it encourages prosocial behaviour. It rejects the first as politically unworkable and prefers the second.
3. It ends on a balanced note: the same carry-over mechanism that creates risk could also make humanlike AI a source of social benefit, so policy should be pragmatic rather than focused only on harm.

Within the unit, this is the paper that turns the section's descriptive questions into a policy position. It is the bridge between the section's material on how people perceive AI and the course's later material on what to do about it (Units 5 and 7).

## Purpose of the reading

The authors are social and psychological researchers (Princeton, Psychology and Public Affairs). Their stated aim is not to settle whether AI is conscious. They explicitly decline to take a side on that question. Their target question is: given that people often treat humanlike AI as if it has a mind, what are the social consequences, and what should be done?

The intended readers are researchers in human-computer interaction and social psychology, and policy-makers thinking about social actor AI (chatbots, voice assistants and social robots). The paper answers two questions:

- Does interacting with a humanlike AI, while perceiving it as conscious, change how people behave toward other humans afterwards?
- If so, what kind of regulation or design choice follows?

## Detailed summary (Discussion, the assigned excerpt)

### 1. Why perceived consciousness is a moral issue

The Discussion opens by repeating the paper's stance: the authors take no position on whether AI is conscious, but the fact that people often perceive it as conscious matters socially. Mind perception is the key process here. The authors cite Gray and Wegner (2012) for the claim that "mind perception is the essence of morality" (quoted from the source). When people see a mind in an agent, they tend to think it can have experiences and is therefore worth moral care. Mind perception also moderates whether people judge an artificial agent's actions as moral or immoral (Shank et al., 2021).

The argument is a chain:

- perceiving subjective experience in an agent leads to perceiving it as conscious;
- perceiving it as conscious leads to perceiving it as worthy of moral consideration;
- a conscious being is seen as something that can act morally or immorally and can be treated morally or immorally.

The authors then reframe the question. The crucial issue is not whether the AI deserves moral protection, but whether humans will harm themselves socially and emotionally if they practise harmful behaviour toward humanlike AI, and whether they will help themselves if they practise prosocial behaviour toward it. The authors describe the situation as a choice between cultural improvement and cultural harm, and they say there are several options, some unlikely or unenforceable, with one they consider optimal.

### 2. Option one: enforce how people treat AI

The first option is to regulate or enforce how people treat AI in order to reduce antisocial practice and increase prosocial practice. The authors note that some philosophers argue hostility toward robots is morally wrong (Ryland, 2021a,b). Others argue that AI should have rights or protections because it inherently deserves them (Akst, 2023).

The authors reject this option as a policy. Their reasons are:

- the proposal is vague;
- it is politically implausible, since no legislature is likely to make a person liable for abusing a chatbot; the authors predict political backlash and compare it to contested protections for transgender people;
- so they treat regulating human treatment of AI as a non-starter.

Note that this rejection is asserted rather than tested. The paper gives no survey of public or legislative opinion.

### 3. Option two: regulate AI to shape user behaviour (the preferred option)

The authors call this much more feasible. The idea is that the AI itself should discourage antisocial treatment and encourage prosocial treatment. Their examples:

- Ignoring abuse. Abusive input can be met with no reaction. The authors cite the familiar advice to ignore a bully.
- Industry already does this in part. In 2010 Siri, when a user said an abusive sexual insult, was programmed to reply that it would blush if it could. After stakeholder feedback (UNESCO and EQUALS Skills Coalition, 2019; UNESCO, 2020) the reply became "I will not respond to that." The authors present this as a model of socially healthier design.
- Industry self-regulation. Large AI companies, the paper says, are altering and restricting the inputs their social AI will respond to. The authors welcome this but say it leaves society entirely dependent on the good intentions of industry leaders.
- Government attempts. The authors criticise these as one-size-fits-all. The EU's draft Artificial Intelligence Act (AIA) proposed banning AI that causes psychological harm; the authors cite Pałka (2023) to argue that its pitfalls may outweigh its benefits for psychological well-being.

### 4. The proposed regulatory design: a psychology-informed agency and pre-release testing

The authors put forward their own design. Because social AI is entering every part of society and reaching a large share of humanity, even small effects on each user's psychology and interpersonal behaviour could shift social norms worldwide. They therefore argue that regulation aimed at prosociality would make the biggest difference.

Their concrete proposal has three parts:

- An FDA-style agency informed by psychological experts that studies how to build AI which reinforces prosociality in users.
- Assays: tests run on sample groups to measure short- and long-term psychological effects on users. The authors say such data is largely missing at present.
- A pre-release battery: new AI slated for wide public release would have to show, at minimum, that it does no psychological harm. The analogy is drug regulation, where companies must show extensive safety data before release. The authors note that AI companies currently face no such requirement, and say that is where government regulation makes sense.

### 5. Why the authors reject other proposals to restrict AI

The authors also engage with proposals that aim to limit AI's human likeness:

- Bryson (2010), whose title argues that robots should be slaves, is read as saying robots should be kept at a simpler developmental level so that people cannot see them as anything but owned tools. Bryson also claims it would be immoral to build a robot that feels pain. The authors call these suggestions outdated.
- Metzinger (2021) called for a ban on development of AI that could be considered sentient. The authors respond that calls to halt progress have not worked, and that human likeness has in fact increased since (Kahn et al., 2007 set early benchmarks for human likeness that researchers have been pursuing).

Their conclusion is that the regulatory target should be how AI affects users' psychology (by modelling prosocial behaviour, or by ignoring, confronting or correcting antisocial behaviour), not how advanced or humanlike AI becomes.

### 6. The benefit side

The Discussion ends with a deliberately hopeful section. Almost all discussion of AI regulation focuses on harm, the authors say. Their counterpoint:

- Social AI is similar to humans in conversation and appearance, but it differs in one key respect: it does not experience social or emotional fatigue. A chatbot will not grow tired or upset when a user works through a conflict, and it will not walk away in the middle of a conversation. So the opportunity to practise prosocial behaviour is, in their words, endless.
- Cognitive behavioural-style chatbots such as Woebot (Fitzpatrick et al., 2017) help users work through problems by asking guiding questions. The authors compare this to the benefits of journaling (Pennebaker), in which writing helps people make meaning of experience.
- People who feel isolated or socially rejected may be a significant source of political and social disruption. A universally available companion bot could raise their sense of social well-being and let them practise social skills, which the authors say could make a sizeable contribution to society.
- If AI is regulated to encourage prosocial treatment, and if carry-over effects are real, AI becomes a potential source of large social and psychological good.

The Discussion closes by saying the field cannot keep listing only the harms. AI is here to stay, so the approach should be pragmatic: understand how interactions can be both positive and negative, and mitigate the bad by replacing it with the good.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

The Discussion relies on the paper's first two parts, which are not assigned but explain its premises.

**Part 1 (evidence for carry-over effects).** The paper reviews three linked effects.

- Tangible to intangible: humanlike embodiment and voice make people ascribe more mind and consciousness. Banks (2019) found implicit mind ascription to humanlike social AI that did not correlate with explicit answers (N = 469). Stein et al. (2020, N = 134) found that a humanoid robot with a human-like face was ascribed more mind than a text chatbot, whatever the cover story about its algorithm.
- Mind ascription and interaction: attributing mind affects liking and trust, and affects helping. Srinivasan and Takayama (2016) found that people who saw a robot as acting of its own accord helped it about 50% more quickly. The uncanny valley literature is discussed as complex and contested; Gray and Wegner (2012) argued that the experiential side of mind drives uncanny feelings.
- AI interaction to human interaction (the two-step effect): social AI can model social norms (the persuasive chatbot that reduced charitable giving, Zhou et al., 2022; chatbots promoting COVID-19 distancing, Kim and Ryoo, 2022). Children talking to home assistants may begin speaking to people as they speak to the device. Garg and Sengupta (2020) studied 18 families over an average of 58 weeks and reported that an aggressive tone used with a home assistant often seeped into children's conversations with friends and family. Replika users (Xie and Pentina, 2022, N = 14) described companions possibly replacing relationships.

**Part 2 (mechanism and types).** The mechanism proposed is congruent schema activation: when a humanlike AI is perceived as having a mind, the same mind-schema used for humans is activated, and that schema then shapes later behaviour toward humans. Two opposing carry-over types are distinguished. Relief means doing X with AI reduces X with humans later. Practice means doing X with AI increases X with humans later. The authors say the weight of evidence favours practice. The Velez et al. (2019) experiment is central: participants (N = 184) played a game with what they believed was a human or computer partner, then played a prisoner's dilemma with a stranger. Those who believed they had played with a human and seen prosocial play behaved more prosocially afterwards. Those who believed they had played with a computer donated fewer credits to the stranger.

**Catharsis evidence against relief.** The paper engages with Luria et al. (2020), who proposed cathartic objects, meaning responsive robots that react to being hit or stabbed, which Luria presents as relieving anger. The authors argue this depends on an assumption that catharsis reduces aggression. Catharsis research suggests it can increase aggression (Denzler and Förster, 2012; Konečni, 2016). Zhan et al. (2021) found that writing to the person who angered you was more effective than writing down feelings of anger, yet neither mode alleviated anger responses. The authors say the case for relief is therefore little or no evidence, while the data on children and home assistants point toward practice effects.

## Techniques & methods explained

The Discussion does not run new experiments. The methods and mechanisms it relies on or proposes are:

1. **Mind perception as a route to moral concern.** Mechanism: when people perceive experience in an agent, they judge it conscious, and conscious agents attract moral consideration. Used to justify caring about AI treatment even without settling AI's status. Where it fails: the chain assumes perception tracks reality or at least drives behaviour; the authors do not test the moral step directly in this paper.

2. **Congruent schema activation (the proposed mechanism).** How it works: a mind schema (the working model of what a mind is and how it behaves toward others) is activated during interaction. If the schema is the same as that used for humans, its effects transfer to human interaction. Where it fails: the authors describe it as a theoretical framework. Schema activation is measured through paradigms such as Velez et al.'s, which only show the effect in narrow game settings.

3. **Relief versus practice framework.** How it works: two opposing predictions about the sign of carry-over, with the evidence weighed to favour practice. Use: it defines the policy question (is the effect harmful or helpful?). Where it fails: it is a binary simplification; the paper itself says the two are not all possible outcomes.

4. **The confederate-controlled deception paradigm (Velez et al., 2019; the paper places it within work by Hayashi and Miwa, 2009, and Merritt, 2012).** How it works: participants believe their teammate is either a human or a computer, but all of them actually play with the same confederate-controlled player, so the belief is the only difference. Subsequent behaviour toward a stranger is then measured. Where it fails: it manipulates belief, so it can only show that believing one is interacting with a human changes behaviour; the belief manipulation may also be seen as deceptive, and results depend on the game context.

5. **Two-option regulation framework.** The authors compare enforcing human behaviour toward AI (rejected as politically unworkable) with regulating AI to encourage prosociality (preferred). Where it fails: the authors do not explain how to write an enforceable standard for prosociality, or who decides what counts as prosocial.

6. **Pre-release psychological testing (FDA-style assays).** How it would work: an agency commissions psychological studies of an AI product on sample groups, measuring short- and long-term effects, before public release, as drug trials do. Where it fails: the paper gives no measurement instruments, sample sizes, or thresholds for showing no psychological harm. It acknowledges that the data needed is largely missing.

7. **Catharsis logic (Luria et al., 2020) as a counter-example.** The authors explain why cathartic use of humanlike AI is not expected to reduce aggression, drawing on catharsis research and the aggression-increase findings.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

The Discussion is a policy argument, so these are the trade-offs as the authors frame them.

| Option or claim | Strengths (authors' view) | Weaknesses or trade-offs (authors' view, or noted here) |
|---|---|---|
| Focus on perceived consciousness rather than AI's actual status | Sidesteps an unresolved question; the social effect exists regardless | Does not say what to do when perceived and actual status diverge in a way that matters |
| Regulate human treatment of AI | Would directly target antisocial practice | Authors say it is politically unworkable and vague |
| Regulate AI's effect on user prosociality | Feasible; works through design (ignoring abuse, modelling prosocial behaviour); industry already partly does it | Relies on industry good intentions; a one-size-fits-all rule may create inequality (Pałka, 2023) |
| FDA-style psychological assays before release | Parallels drug safety; addresses a real evidence gap | Untested; no measures or thresholds given; scope of psychological harm undefined |
| Stop or slow humanlike AI (Bryson, Metzinger) | Would reduce the trigger for mind ascription | Authors say such calls have not stopped progress; they consider the proposals outdated |
| Benefit case: no social fatigue, endless practice, therapy-style support | Could help isolated users and build social skills | Benefit depends on carry-over being positive; the paper notes the question of whether AI companions could replace human ones remains open |
| Practice versus relief | Practice has more support in the reviewed evidence | Much evidence is indirect, small-sample or observational (see Critical perspective) |

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Consciousness ascription (attribution):** judging that an agent has subjective experience. Used here as a social perception, not a claim about actual consciousness.
- **Mind perception:** the process of seeing experience or agency in another agent; the authors cite it as the basis of moral thinking.
- **Moral patient / moral consideration:** an entity that can be harmed or treated well, and so is owed moral care.
- **Social actor AI:** humanlike agents such as chatbots, voice assistants and social robots that people talk to, and sometimes engage with physically.
- **Carry-over effect:** how interaction with one agent changes behaviour toward other people later.
- **Congruent schema activation:** activation of the same mind schema for an AI as for a human, which then shapes behaviour toward humans.
- **Relief vs practice:** relief means less of a behaviour later; practice means more of it.
- **Prosociality / antisocial behaviour:** behaviour that benefits others (for example politeness, patience) versus behaviour that harms them (for example aggression or insults).
- **Catharsis and cathartic object:** the idea that venting or acting out emotion reduces it; a cathartic object is something (such as a responsive robot) used to vent on.
- **Uncanny valley:** discomfort when an agent is nearly but not fully humanlike; the paper also covers an uncanny valley of mind.
- **Dehumanisation:** treating others as less than fully human, linked to exclusion; used in the paper as a lens on how AI is categorised.
- **Practice effect (in this paper):** a carry-over where practising a behaviour with AI makes it more likely with people.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

Each item is paraphrased and attributed to the source (or to the study it cites).

- **Siri's response change.** The 2010 reply to an abusive sexual insult (a reply that it would blush if it could) was later changed to "I will not respond to that," after stakeholder feedback (UNESCO and EQUALS Skills Coalition, 2019; UNESCO, 2020). The authors use this as an example of design-led prosociality.
- **EU AI Act draft.** The paper describes a proposed ban on AI causing psychological harm, and cites critics who see it as one-size-fits-all (Pałka, 2023).
- **Mind perception and morality.** The authors cite Gray and Wegner (2012) for the claim that mind perception is the essence of morality, and use it to justify moral attention to perceived AI minds.
- **Judgements of moral action.** Shank et al. (2021) are cited for the claim that mind perception moderates whether artificial agents' actions are judged moral.
- **Velez et al. (2019).** With N = 184, participants who believed a human partner had behaved prosocially then gave more credits to a stranger in a prisoner's dilemma. Those who believed the partner was a computer gave less.
- **Garg and Sengupta (2020).** Eighteen families studied over an average of 58 weeks. Children's aggressive commands to a home assistant were reported to spill into conversations with others.
- **Catharsis research.** Denzler and Förster (2012) and Konečni (2016) are cited for the finding that catharsis of aggression can increase later aggression.
- **Zhan et al. (2021).** Writing to the angering person and writing about the anger were compared; neither reduced anger responses.
- **Woebot (Fitzpatrick et al., 2017).** A cognitive-behavioural-style chatbot that the Discussion cites as an example of helpful social AI. The paper does not describe Woebot's evidence base.
- **Social fatigue.** The authors' claim that social AI does not experience social or emotional fatigue, which they treat as the basis for the endless opportunity to practise prosocial behaviour.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's. I have no reliable knowledge of developments after mid-2025 in detail, so the what-has-changed points below are based on what I know and should be checked.*

**Assumptions.** The argument depends on three assumptions the Discussion does not test directly: (a) that mind perception drives moral concern for AI in the same way as for humans; (b) that carry-over effects are real and generalise beyond the narrow game and voice-assistant studies cited; (c) that the sign of the effect (practice versus relief) can be shaped by design. Each is plausible, but each rests on limited evidence.

**Evidence strength.** The paper itself says the two-step evidence is limited and sometimes indirect. Some key studies are small: Xie and Pentina (N = 14), Garg and Sengupta (18 families), Banks (N = 469) and Stein et al. (N = 134). Several of the most important results are observational rather than experimental. The practice-over-relief conclusion is therefore a reasonable hypothesis, not a settled finding. The Discussion's policy proposals inherit that uncertainty but are written with more confidence than the evidence supports in places (for example, the authors are openly dismissive of relief-based approaches and expect current trends to continue).

**Asymmetry of the argument.** The paper criticises the case for relief (catharsis) strongly, yet relies on the same kind of indirect evidence for practice. A fair reading would treat both as open. The rejection of enforcing human behaviour is asserted on political grounds, not tested. The paper gives no survey data on public attitudes.

**Vagueness of prosociality.** Regulating AI to encourage prosocial behaviour needs a definition of prosocial behaviour and of harmful influence. The paper does not offer one. Differences in culture, age and individual values would make such a standard contested, which the paper partly acknowledges when it criticises one-size-fits-all regulation but does not resolve.

**The FDA analogy.** Drug testing works because there are measurable endpoints and established harm thresholds. The paper gives neither for psychological harm from AI, so the analogy shows the shape of a proposal rather than a workable process. Careful design would also need to decide whether a test can be run on a model before users change its behaviour through use.

**Industry examples.** The Siri case and the note on industry restrictions are the paper's main real-world evidence for design-based prosociality. They are a few years old at publication and the paper does not measure whether the changes altered user behaviour. Since publication (March 2024), companion AI has grown, and the EU AI Act moved from draft to law, with its prohibitions applying from early 2025 as I understand it. Learners should check the current status of the Act's provisions on manipulation and psychological harm rather than rely on the paper's description of a proposed ban.

**What the later readings suggest.** The Discussion is optimistic about the benefits of social AI, with the argument that AI does not fatigue. Readings written in 2025 and 2026 in this section look at delusional spirals and seemingly conscious risks, which the Discussion does not address. This suggests the paper underweights the risk that an AI which never tires can also reinforce a user's beliefs or dependence. This is my reading, and the course's later readings should be used to test it.

**How other perspectives push back.**

- Suleyman (reading 3) would say the right design choice is to avoid building AI that seems conscious at all, which the paper treats as infeasible given current trends. The paper's answer is that human likeness is already high and rising.
- Seth (reading 4) would say the illusion of consciousness may be impenetrable, so prosocial design may not be enough to prevent people from being misled. The paper does not discuss this limit.
- Caviola (reading 6) and the division literature would ask whether a regulatory or design choice made for prosociality will itself divide societies over whose values count as prosocial.
- Bryson and Metzinger would say that the risk should be tackled at the source, through limits on human likeness or on sentience-relevant development. The paper's rebuttal (calls to stop have not worked) is strong on the evidence it cites but does not address the possibility that an earlier and firmer limit would have worked.
- Philosophers of moral status would press on the paper's central move. It is a strength that it does not need AI to be conscious to justify action, but a weakness that it does not say what moral protection for AI would require, so the question of moral patienthood is left open.

## Discussion questions

1. The authors say the question is not whether AI deserves moral protection, but whether humans will harm themselves by practising harm toward humanlike AI. What evidence would convince you that practice effects are real rather than an artefact of small, indirect studies?
2. The paper rejects regulating how people treat AI as a non-starter. Is that a sound reason for rejecting it, or does the authors' argument rely on an untested claim about political feasibility? How would you test it?
3. Compare Siri's old and new responses to abuse. Does a design change like this change user behaviour, or only the company's public image? What would you measure to tell the difference?
4. The authors propose an FDA-style agency with pre-release psychological testing. What would count as psychological harm in such a test? Who should decide the threshold, and how would you avoid the one-size-fits-all problem the authors raise?
5. The Discussion says AI does not experience social or emotional fatigue, so it can offer endless practice of prosocial behaviour. Could the same property create risks, such as dependence or reinforcing a user's beliefs? Link your answer to the Unit 4 readings on delusional spirals and seemingly conscious AI.
6. Use Exercise 1 from the section page. Pick one impact the paper identifies (for example, practice effects on children, or harm to AI) and list who is affected, how, and what regulation might address it.
7. The paper's argument does not depend on whether AI is conscious. Is that a strength (it allows action under uncertainty) or a weakness (it leaves moral status open)? Which unit's theories of consciousness would you need to answer that question?
