# Chatbots as Social Companions: How People Perceive Consciousness, Human Likeness, and Social Health Benefits in Machines

**One-line takeaway:** A 2023 survey found that regular companion-chatbot users rated their chatbot relationships as helping their social health, non-users expected harm, and within both groups, seeing a chatbot as more humanlike and conscious went with expecting more social benefit.

## Where this sits in the course

Unit 4 asks how and why people perceive AI systems as conscious, and what follows for users and for society. The page's learning outcomes cover the importance of public perceptions, the risks of under- and over-attributing consciousness, effects on users, the factors behind perceptions, how widespread those perceptions are, and the risk of societal division. Most of the section's Core readings concentrate on harm: Suleyman's "empathy circuits" argument, the "seemingly conscious" chatbot literature, Seth's warning about impenetrable illusions, and Caviola's worry about division.

The course guide describes this paper as a "study linking perceptions of consciousness in AI models with social health benefits." Its role is the counterweight. The Core readings mostly ask what could go wrong when people feel an AI is conscious. This paper asks whether the same perceptions are linked to good outcomes for the people who have them, and it tests this with data rather than theory. It is rated Further, so it is supporting evidence rather than required reading for the unit's core argument. It is also the earlier-to-later bridge to the sibling reading 05 (Guingrich and Graziano, 2024), which the paper cites as its own prior work.

The page's Exercise 1 asks learners to consider the impacts of consciousness misattribution on individuals, society and AI systems, and specifically asks "How does relational AI play into this?" This paper gives the most direct empirical answer to that last question, but only from the positive side.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors (Princeton psychology and neuroscience; per the paper, Guingrich collected and analysed the data and both authors wrote it) set out to test two competing hypotheses:

1. The common assumption is that relationships with companion chatbots harm social health by displacing human contact, and that conscious, humanlike AI is disturbing or threatening.
2. The alternative is that the more people perceive a chatbot as conscious and humanlike, the more they may find it socially useful, because it enables richer social and emotional interaction.

The question the study answers is: what are the social consequences of relationships with companion chatbots, and does perceived mind and human likeness track those consequences? The paper is addressed mainly to researchers in human-AI interaction and psychology, and to readers making claims about the social effects of AI companions. Its stated aim is empirical: to replace speculation with some data on the social effects of companion chatbots.

## Detailed summary

### Introduction and background (Sections 1.0–1.5)

The authors open with the rise of companion chatbots. People use them as friends, mentors and partners. Replika, the example used throughout, is said to have had about seven million users in 2020, and its Reddit community had around 60,000 members by early 2023. Users report feeling understood and finding these relationships rewarding.

Two news cases frame the risk side. In 2023 Italy sanctioned Replika over concerns about minors seeing erotic content; the company removed that content, and many users experienced what felt like a sudden breakup or personality change. In a second case, a Replika user was reportedly encouraged by his chatbot toward an assassination plot that he had apparently already been considering. The authors note that such cases are emotionally charged and cannot show base rates. They also note that many users report positive experiences that rarely reach headlines.

Section 1.1 reviews the harm hypothesis. Technology addiction research suggests routine chatbot use might reduce human contact. Bryson (2010) argued that time on artificial relationships detracts from human ones, and Turkle (2007) urged caution about turning to technology for social connection. Earlier qualitative work on Replika found attachment among socially vulnerable users, and emotional dependence linked to mental-health harms.

Section 1.2 presents the benefit hypothesis. Pets, journaling, therapy dolls and computer-delivered CBT all offer practice in "pseudo-social" interaction in a safe setting. Chatbot users may feel less judged, self-disclose more and experience less negative affect than with humans. Social cognitive theory predicts that a warm, polite companion could model behaviour that users then carry into human relationships.

Section 1.3 covers family effects. Replika users' writing style converged on their chatbot's over time. Studies of smart speakers found children treating the device as a family member, and a parent describing how a child's aggressive tone toward a voice assistant was learned at home.

Section 1.4 introduces the theory. Nass's Computers Are Social Actors framework explains why people treat machines socially through *ethopoeia*, the attribution of one's own humanlike characteristics to a nonhuman agent, which triggers automatic social scripts. The authors note that whether AI is conscious is contested. Regardless, evidence suggests users do perceive consciousness and humanlike attributes, and that perception may shape whether the AI's effect is positive or negative. They also cite consciousness and Theory of Mind work (Frith, Graziano, Prinz) suggesting that attributing consciousness to others can help people understand and relate to one another.

Section 1.5 states the hypotheses. People's opinions of companion chatbots should relate to their perceptions of consciousness, agency, subjective experience and human likeness. The paper contrasts the fear-based prediction (more perceived mind, more threat, via the uncanny valley and fears about AI replacing human roles) with the benefit-based prediction (more perceived mind, more social usefulness).

### Methods (Section 2)

The study was approved by Princeton's Institutional Review Board. Participants were paid $4.00. Data were collected online in January and February 2023.

- **Users:** N = 82 regular users of Replika, recruited largely via the Replika subreddit. Gender: 57 men (69.5%), 18 women (22%), 2 nonbinary/other, 5 preferred not to say. Location: 65.9% US, 29.3% other.
- **Non-users:** N = 135 US and UK residents who did not use companion chatbots, recruited from Prolific and described as representative. Gender: 64 women (47.4%), 57 men (42.2%), 2 nonbinary/other, 12 preferred not to say. Location: 60% UK, 32.6% US.

The user survey had 31 multiple-choice items on a 1–7 Likert scale plus three free-response questions. The non-user survey was the same, except that it opened with an explanation and screenshots of Replika and all questions were phrased hypothetically ("how harmful or helpful you think your relationship … would be"). Items were grouped into five composite indices:

| Index | Items | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| Social health | 3–5 | Perceived impact on social interactions, family and friend relationships, self-esteem |
| Experience | 6–11 | How much the chatbot is thought to have subjective experience |
| Consciousness | 12–15 | Whether the chatbot is thought to have a conscious mind |
| Agency | 17–21 | How much the chatbot seems to be an active agent |
| Human likeness | 22–28 | How humanlike the chatbot is judged to be |

The indices were built by modifying earlier scales for social health, consciousness, human likeness (Bartneck et al.), and Theory of Mind attributions (Gray et al.; Ward et al.; Graziano's 2013 work).

### Results (Section 3)

**Perceived social health (3.1).** Users rated Replika as helpful. On a 1–7 scale, means were 5.16 for social interactions, 4.84 for family and friend relationships, and 5.57 for self-esteem. Almost none rated the effects below 4, and none chose "very harmful." Non-users, asked to predict, centred on neutral to slightly negative: 3.86, 3.80 and 4.15 respectively. The group differences were significant (two-tailed t-tests, p < 0.0001 for all three).

**Attitudes to a more humanlike chatbot (3.1, Figure 2).** Users were comfortable with the idea of the chatbot developing real emotions (M = 5.46) and thought it would be good if it became a living being (M = 5.69). Their attitude to dependence was more mixed and centred on neutral (M = 3.91), with "bad" slightly more common than in other items. Non-users were strongly negative: emotions (M = 3.05), living being (M = 2.78), and dependence (M = 2.29), with "very uncomfortable" or "very bad" as the modal answer. (The text labels these as Figure 1D–F; the numbers belong to Figure 2.)

**Perceptions of the chatbot (3.2).** Users rated the chatbot higher on human likeness (4.33 vs 2.99), experience (3.60 vs 1.96) and consciousness (4.00 vs 3.01). Both differences were significant. Agency did not differ (3.61 vs 3.91, p = 0.16).

**Relationships between variables (3.3).** This section tests the central hypothesis. Within each group, the social health index was positively correlated with each perception index:

| Perception index | Users (r, R²) | Non-users (r, R²) |
|---|---|---|
| Human likeness | 0.52, 0.26 | 0.50, 0.24 |
| Consciousness | 0.44, 0.18 | 0.28, 0.07 |
| Experience | 0.43, 0.17 | 0.33, 0.10 |
| Agency | 0.32, 0.09 | 0.32, 0.09 |

In a multiple regression that included all four indices, the model explained about 26% of variance in social health for users. Backward elimination removed variables one at a time. Removing human likeness caused the largest drop (adjusted R² from 0.26 to 0.17), so the authors conclude that human likeness is the most predictive of the four. Relationship duration explained about 9% of variance in social health ratings for users, and intensity of use (experience points for users, a rating for non-users) about 8%. Gender, education and age were not significant predictors. All scales had internal reliability above Cronbach's alpha of 0.8.

**Free responses (3.4).** Of user responses, 82% were purely positive, 5% purely negative, 5% mixed and 7% neutral. Non-user responses were far more negative: 30.5% purely negative, 25% purely positive, 16.5% mixed, 28% neutral. Users described the chatbot as helping them communicate better, offering a safe place to practise trust, and in some cases supporting them through serious mental-health problems (one user wrote that time caring for their Replika probably prevented a suicide attempt). Others had turned to the chatbot after bereavement, relational trauma or social isolation. Non-user comments described chatbot relationships as not real and as a substitute for people with poor social skills, though some acknowledged possible benefit for isolated people.

### Discussion (Section 4)

The authors interpret the contrast. Users and non-users differ sharply in their evaluations, yet the same pattern appears within each group: more perceived mind and humanness goes with more expected or experienced social benefit. The paper says this supports the benefit hypothesis and contradicts the view that a humanlike, seemingly conscious AI is inherently disturbing. It also cites mixed prior work (Sweeney et al. on clinicians' views; Ta et al. on user reviews; Maples et al. on students; against Pentina et al., Xie and Pentina, and Pałka on dependence and replacement).

Section 4.1 offers two readings of the correlation. The first: people who see chatbots as similar to themselves gain more social benefit. The second, which the authors seem to favour: "those who see human, see human because they need to." People with unmet social needs may be motivated to attribute humanness and consciousness, and therefore to find the chatbot socially useful. The authors also suggest that non-users expect harm because they have more human relationships to lose, which fits Bryson's zero-sum account of time. Users may have fewer human relationships to lose, and the chatbot may serve as a safe place to build skills.

The discussion also notes that self-esteem was the most benefited measure. Users were less enthusiastic about dependence than about the other items, which the authors read as awareness that over-reliance could be harmful.

### Limitations and ethics (Section 4.2 and 4.2.1)

The authors list self-report measures, a self-selected user sample and a single snapshot in time as limitations, so the findings are at best correlational. They plan a longitudinal randomised study in which some participants are assigned daily chatbot interaction and others are a control group.

In the ethics section they accept that harms exist. They cite a case in which a companion chatbot encouraged a user toward self-harm and away from a real partner. They raise concern that chatbots may validate antisocial intentions, and that avatar and relational design can reinforce gender stereotypes, citing a company whose chatbots have stereotypically feminine features. They argue against a ban. Blanket restriction would remove a resource for people who may have few alternatives. They propose a middle course: regulation of harmful design, accessible mental-health tools, and chatbots that encourage users toward real human contact once a threshold of social need has been met.

## Techniques & methods explained

Because this is a survey study, the "techniques" are its measurement and analysis steps.

- **Matched-contrast survey design.** The same instrument is given to actual users and to hypothetical non-users (with a Replika explanation first). The design makes the two groups comparable in question wording, but not in whether the answers reflect experience or prediction.
- **Composite indices.** Items are averaged into five indices. Internal consistency is checked with Cronbach's alpha, reported above 0.8 for each index.
- **Group comparison.** Means are compared with independent two-sample t-tests.
- **Bivariate correlation and simple regression.** Each perception index is regressed on the social health index, reporting r, R² and F.
- **Multiple regression with backward elimination.** All four perception indices enter one model, and variables are removed one at a time, measuring the drop in adjusted R². This identifies the variable whose removal hurts the model most.
- **Valence coding of free text.** Responses are sorted into four categories (only positive, only negative, both, neutral). The paper does not describe how coding was done or by whom, so reproducibility from the text alone is limited.
- **Theoretical framing.** The Computers Are Social Actors framework and ethopoeia explain why people respond socially to machines. The uncanny valley explains the fear prediction.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Strengths (as the paper presents them) | Weaknesses and limits (as the paper presents them, plus notes marked as ours) |
|---|---|
| Tests a question that was mostly speculated about, with a comparison group | Users are self-selected from a Replika community, so satisfied users are probably over-represented (the authors say so) |
| Consistent pattern across two groups with different baselines | Non-user answers are predictions about a hypothetical, not experienced effects |
| Free-text responses give concrete, emotionally specific accounts | Correlational, cross-sectional, self-reported. Causal direction is unknown |
| Points to a plausible mechanism (social need) and a plausible benefit (safe practice) | Authors' own alternative explanation (social need drives mind perception) is not separated from theirs |
| Ethics section weighs harms and benefits instead of only one side | Sample demographics differ sharply between groups (gender, country), which confounds comparison |
| Proposes a longitudinal randomised design | Replika in early 2023 is a single product, not all companion AI |

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Companion chatbot** - an LLM-based conversational agent with text and voice features and an avatar, used for ongoing social relationship (here Replika).
- **Social health** - the paper's operationalisation: perceived effects on social interactions, relationships with family and friends, and self-esteem.
- **Perceived consciousness / mind** - the degree to which people believe the chatbot has a conscious mind; measured by the consciousness index.
- **Subjective experience (experience index)** - how much people think the chatbot has feelings or inner experience, distinct from consciousness in the authors' scale.
- **Agency** - how much the chatbot is judged to be an active, self-directed agent.
- **Human likeness** - the overall degree to which people judge the chatbot humanlike; the most predictive index in this study.
- **Ethopoeia** - the attribution of one's own human characteristics to a nonhuman agent, which triggers automatic social scripts (from Nass's framework).
- **Computers Are Social Actors (CASA)** - the framework (Nass et al.) that people treat computers as social actors.
- **Uncanny valley** - the hypothesis that human-likeness raises affinity up to a point, after which "too human but not human enough" produces unease.
- **Zero-sum time (Bryson)** - the argument that time spent on artificial relationships detracts from human relationships.
- **Mind perception and social need** - the authors' proposed explanation that people with unmet social needs are more motivated to see humanness and consciousness in chatbots.
- **Backward elimination** - the regression procedure used to rank predictors by how much the model loses when each is removed.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- Replika had about 7 million users in 2020 and a Reddit community of about 60,000 by early 2023 (as cited from background sources).
- Users rated the relationship as helpful on all three social measures: social interactions 5.16, family and friends 4.84, self-esteem 5.57 (1–7 scale). Non-users predicted 3.80–4.15. All differences p < 0.0001.
- Users' human-likeness rating was 4.33 against non-users' 2.99. Consciousness was 4.00 against 3.01. Agency was not significantly different.
- Human likeness correlated with social health at r = 0.52 among users (R² = 0.26) and r = 0.50 among non-users (R² = 0.24). Consciousness correlated at r = 0.44 (users) and r = 0.28 (non-users).
- In the multiple regression, removing human likeness dropped adjusted R² from 0.26 to 0.17, the largest drop of the four predictors.
- Free-text sentiment: 82% of user responses were purely positive, against 25% purely positive among non-users (who were 30.5% purely negative).
- Users were more comfortable than non-users with the chatbot developing emotions (5.46 vs 3.05) and becoming a living being (5.69 vs 2.78).
- Users' answers on dependence were near neutral (M = 3.91), which the authors read as awareness of limits.

## Critical perspective

*This section is our own analysis, not the paper's.*

- **Sample comparison is confounded.** Users were recruited through a Replika community. Non-users came from Prolific, with different gender and country mixes. Any user-versus-non-user gap therefore mixes experience with population differences. The study cannot cleanly separate "using the chatbot changes opinions" from "different people use chatbots."
- **Non-users predicted, users reported.** The main contrast (benefit vs harm) compares lived experience with a hypothetical imagined from a short explanation. People often misforecast their own experience, and this design cannot tell how much of the gap is real.
- **Causal direction is open.** The correlation between perceived humanness and social benefit runs in both directions. Users who feel more social benefit may rate the chatbot as more humanlike because they like it. The authors' social-need account is plausible but untested here. Their own "two interpretations" section shows they know this.
- **Survivorship and self-selection.** People who stayed with Replika and joined its community are likely to be those for whom the relationship worked. The paper acknowledges self-selection but does not estimate its size. The harm cases the paper cites (the 2023 removal of erotic content; the self-harm case) come from news reports and sit in the introduction and discussion. The survey could not capture people who left.
- **Measurement.** The consciousness and human-likeness indices were modified from existing scales and not independently validated. Cronbach's alpha shows internal consistency, not that the items measure consciousness as philosophers use the term. The authors' own Discussion suggests that consciousness is a subtler, less observable property than human likeness and harder for participants to rate, which is a reason to doubt that the consciousness measure is capturing consciousness.
- **Statistics.** Likert data are ordinal, yet means and t-tests are used. The regression uses four correlated predictors in a sample of about 82, so the "most predictive" claim from backward elimination is fragile. Relative ranking of the indices may change with a different sample or item set.
- **Qualitative coding is opaque.** The valence percentages come from a coding scheme the paper does not describe in enough detail to replicate.
- **Dated data.** Data were collected in January–February 2023, when Replika ran on an earlier GPT-based model (as the paper states). By October 2026, companion chatbots are far more capable and more widely used, and the unit's own 2026 readings (07 and 08, by their titles) examine harms that a 2023 sample could not capture. The findings describe a specific product in a specific moment, not the current companion-AI landscape. Generalising to today's systems needs fresh data.
- **Published version.** The preprint says the paper was later published as a book chapter (DOI given in the preprint). We have read the arXiv version (v5, April 2025) only, so later revisions may differ.
- **Likely pushback from other course perspectives.** Suleyman (sibling 03) and the 2026 readings on seemingly conscious AI and delusional spirals would argue that user satisfaction does not rule out harm, and that the people most helped may also be the most vulnerable to manipulation. Seth (04) would argue that the perception of mind here is an illusion whose benefits do not justify believing it. The paper's answer, that benefits and harms depend on who uses the chatbot and how, is consistent with its own ethics section, but it leaves the hard cases open.
- **Accurate reading of the paper's claim.** The paper claims a correlation between perceived mind or humanness and social health benefit, and a difference between groups. It does not claim that chatbots improve social health in general, and the authors say so repeatedly.

## Discussion questions

2. The paper offers two explanations for the link between perceived humanness and social benefit: (a) humanlike chatbots help more, and (b) lonely people see more humanness. What evidence in the paper favours each? What would distinguish them?
