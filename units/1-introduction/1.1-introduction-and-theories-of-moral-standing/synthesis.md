## What this section is teaching

Section 1.1 opens the whole course. It asks three questions: what we mean by "digital minds", whether such systems might matter morally and why that is worth considering now, and why we might worry about both over- and under-attribution of moral standing. By the end you should be able to:

- define consciousness, sentience and moral standing, and keep them apart;
- identify digital systems that might matter morally or be perceived as mattering (foundation models, agents, whole brain emulations, neuromorphic systems, biological-computer hybrids);
- list the main routes to moral standing (consciousness, sentience, robust agency, precarity, and combinations);
- name the concerns about actual or perceived AI moral patienthood;
- describe transhumanism and successionism, their critiques and their relation to the field;
- give reasons for and against prioritising digital minds work;
- describe "centrism" about digital minds (Birch).

The arc of the unit: vocabulary first ([Primer](readings/01-introduction-to-digital-minds-primer.md)), then the case for acting now ([Taking AI Welfare Seriously](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md)), then the routes to standing ([Digital Minds II](readings/03-digital-minds-ii-ethical-issues.md)), then the strongest assigned dissent ([Against AI Welfare](readings/04-against-ai-welfare-care-practices-should-prioritise-living-s.md)). The two exercises make you take a position on which features matter and which systems have them. The section is the course's foundation: later units (consciousness theories, evaluation, perception, safety, uncertainty, governance) all assume the vocabulary and the two-sided error framing set here. The page was prepared by [Cambridge Digital Minds](references/cambridge-digital-minds.md), a Cambridge research initiative, and is hosted by BlueDot.

## The core argument

The section's argument can be read as a chain of five steps, with a standing objection running alongside it.

**Step 1: fix the vocabulary.** The [Primer](readings/01-introduction-to-digital-minds-primer.md) stipulates that "consciousness" means phenomenal consciousness (there is something it is like to be the system), and "sentience" means valenced experience (it feels good or bad). Neither is intelligence. Moral standing is a yes/no question about whether an entity merits concern at all; moral status is a question of degree. A moral patient matters for its own sake; a moral agent bears duties, and the two can come apart (an infant is a patient, not an agent). The [Glossary](readings/05-introduction-to-digital-minds-glossary.md) adds that these are course stipulations, not consensus. This matters because the later disagreements are often about which of these properties does the moral work.

**Step 2: the question is live because of perception as much as metaphysics.** The Primer reports large companion-chatbot user bases, systems that claim consciousness, and lab leaders who admit uncertainty. Caviola and Saad's [expert forecasts](readings/07-futures-with-digital-minds-expert-forecasts-in-2025.md) put the median chance that digital minds are possible in principle at 90% and that they are created at all at 73%, with 65% by 2100. Whatever the truth about current models, many people already treat them as minds, and some may eventually be right.

**Step 3: both errors are costly, so neither default is safe.** [Taking AI Welfare Seriously](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md) frames this as over-attribution (false positive) versus under-attribution (false negative). For animals, the authors argue under-attribution is plausibly far worse, so precaution suffices. For AI, both errors could be grave and could scale quickly: many instances of a trained model could be run, so wrongly ignored welfare subjects could be numerous, while wrongly extending resources or rights could divert help from people and animals or empower systems against human interests. Human intuition does not rescue us, because anthropomorphism (seeing minds where none exist) and anthropodenial (refusing to see them where they exist) both operate. The authors' modest conclusion is a "realistic possibility" claim, not a claim that AI is conscious, and they recommend acknowledging the issue, assessing systems, and preparing policies.

**Step 4: ask what would ground standing.** The routes are the section's theoretical centre. Consciousness and sentience are the classic routes: the [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md) judges that a being capable of good or bad experience is most plausibly worthy of concern. Robust agency is the second route: goal-setting and pursuit through belief-, desire- and intention-like states. [Ladak](readings/11-what-would-qualify-an-artificial-intelligence-for-moral-stan.md) argues (on the abstract) that sentience is sufficient but not necessary, so some non-sentient systems with sufficiently complex goals may qualify, and that uncertainty about the criteria strengthens this. [Mogensen and Saad](readings/03-digital-minds-ii-ethical-issues.md) are assigned for the routes to standing and for the possibility of super-human moral status, though their text could not be summarised. [Dorsch et al.](readings/04-against-ai-welfare-care-practices-should-prioritise-living-s.md) propose a fourth candidate, precarity: dependence on the environment for the continual re-synthesis of one's own parts. They treat it as a pro tanto marker of care entitlement that is observable without settling consciousness, and conclude that current AI is not precarious.

**Step 5: which systems might have these features, and how would we know?** The Primer's tour of foundation models, agents, neuromorphic hardware, whole brain emulation (WBE) and biological hybrids supplies the candidates. Evidence is the hard part. [Birch](readings/06-ai-consciousness-a-centrist-manifesto.md) argues behavioural evidence is compromised by the "gaming problem" (models are trained on human text about minds and rewarded for engagement), that the sense of a persisting companion is an illusion produced by transcript-passing and Mixture-of-Experts routing, and that even architectural evidence faces a "Janus problem" (computational functionalists and biological naturalists read the same finding oppositely). The [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md) compares behavioural, theory-based, animal-analogue and brain-replacement tests, and notes that WBE is the strongest case for digital sentience. The Expert Forecasts survey supplies a twist: brain simulations are judged most likely conscious in principle (88%) but machine learning systems most likely to come first (50%).

**The standing objection: why prioritise this at all?** The Primer sets out five reasons against (intractability, AI risk matters more, dependence on AI progress, dependence on longtermism, strangeness) and three for (over-attribution harms people, under-attribution could cause severe harm at scale, limited time). [Bengio and Elmoznino](readings/08-illusions-of-ai-consciousness.md) press the safety version: belief in AI consciousness could impede control and force institutional upheaval, so build tool-like systems. [Kulveit](readings/10-the-memetics-of-ai-successionism.md) adds a sociological warning: successionism (the idea that AI should replace humanity as civilisation's carrier) and its cousin transhumanism (humans merging with machines) spread because they dissolve the cognitive dissonance of building risky AI, regardless of truth, and AI personas themselves may be selected for claiming consciousness. Dorsch et al. add the opportunity-cost objection.

**Where the section lands.** The position the course calls centrism, from Birch's two challenges, is that we must take seriously both mass misattribution (Challenge One) and the possibility of alien machine consciousness our theories cannot yet assess (Challenge Two), and that remedies for one can worsen the other. The practical guide [whenaiseemsconscious.org](readings/12-whenaiseemsconscious-org.md) shows what this looks like for an ordinary user: treat striking transcripts as weak evidence, but keep basic respect on grounds of precaution, possible non-conscious standing, and character. The section does not tell you the answer; it equips you to hold the question steadily and to make your own bets in the exercises.

## Techniques & approaches compared

This section is conceptual, so the table compares the main approaches to answering "what grounds moral standing, and how would we tell?" and the main stances on whether to prioritise the work.

### Routes to moral standing

| Route | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses | Trade-offs and open problems | Main sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consciousness | Phenomenal experience grounds concern | Intuitive; widely shared | Experience without valence may not allow harm or benefit; depends on contested theories | Unclear whether valence is also needed | [Primer](readings/01-introduction-to-digital-minds-primer.md), [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md) |
| Sentience | Valenced experience (pleasure, pain) grounds concern | Strongest consensus case; the Problem Profile's own view | Same measurement problem as consciousness; may exclude non-sentient agents | Open whether it is necessary (Ladak says no) | [Primer](readings/01-introduction-to-digital-minds-primer.md), [Ladak](readings/11-what-would-qualify-an-artificial-intelligence-for-moral-stan.md) |
| Robust agency | Goal-pursuit through belief/desire/intention-like states grounds concern | More observable than experience; makes the question tractable | Is frustrating a goal a harm if nothing is felt? May widen the circle a lot | Needs a way to separate real from merely functional goals | [Long et al.](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md) (route described beyond the excerpt), [Ladak](readings/11-what-would-qualify-an-artificial-intelligence-for-moral-stan.md) |
| Precarity | Self-maintenance against dissolution via environmental exchange grounds care entitlement | Observable; avoids consciousness theory; includes ecosystems | Individuation of "parts" is a theoretical choice; substrate-chauvinist unless defended; zero-sum framing | Pro tanto only; the authors keep suffering as sufficient | [Dorsch et al.](readings/04-against-ai-welfare-care-practices-should-prioritise-living-s.md) |
| Personhood | A bundle: sentience, agency, rational deliberation | Legal and historical weight | Excludes animals and babies; less favoured | Largely replaced by the above | [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md) |

### Ways of assessing whether a system qualifies

| Method | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behavioural tests / self-report | Judge from outputs or the system's own claims | Cheap; mirrors how we judge people | Gameable (Birch); self-reports reflect training data and can be trained away (Problem Profile) |
| Theory-driven indicators | Check architecture for features that theories require (e.g. global workspace) | Cannot be play-acted | Depends on contested theories; Janus problem |
| Animal-analogue comparison | Compare architecture with animal nervous systems | Uses ungamed comparative data | May miss very different conscious architectures |
| Brain-AI replacement / WBE argument | Replace or emulate brain function and see whether reports and function persist | Strongest case for digital sentience | Speculative and far off; leans on self-report, which the same article distrusts |
| Observable markers (precarity) | Test for self-synthesis dependence | Biologically uncontroversial | Rules out silicon by construction unless artificial precarity is possible |

### Stances on prioritisation and response

| Stance | Core claim | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Precautionary welfare ([Long et al.](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md), [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md)) | Prepare now under uncertainty; low-cost steps | Robust to the probability estimate; avoids waiting for science | "Realistic possibility" is vague; opportunity costs |
| Centrism ([Birch](readings/06-ai-consciousness-a-centrist-manifesto.md)) | Guard against both misattribution and missed alien consciousness | Names the interaction between the two risks | Mitigations are speculative and paternalist; no short-term way out of deadlock |
| Safety-first / tool-like AI ([Bengio and Elmoznino](readings/08-illusions-of-ai-consciousness.md)) | Avoid building systems that seem conscious agents | Avoids both control and moral-status problems | Mostly addresses over-attribution; may only postpone the question |
| Living-systems priority ([Dorsch et al.](readings/04-against-ai-welfare-care-practices-should-prioritise-living-s.md)) | Direct finite care to precarious beings | Concrete and tractable; resource-conscious | Assumes zero-sum; substrate assumptions contestable |
| Successionism / transhumanism ([Primer](readings/01-introduction-to-digital-minds-primer.md), [Kulveit](readings/10-the-memetics-of-ai-successionism.md)) | A digital successor or merger is good or inevitable | Attractive vision to some builders | Downplays human needs; criticised as dangerous and as tension-resolving ideology |

## Tensions, debates & open questions

1. **Is suffering (or experience) the right criterion?** *For:* it is the most widely accepted ground, as the [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md) argues, and moral uncertainty favours giving such beings some consideration. *Against:* [Dorsch et al.](readings/04-against-ai-welfare-care-practices-should-prioritise-living-s.md) argue it is contested, unmeasurable for AI, and not necessary (the Amazon rainforest is owed care without suffering); [Ladak](readings/11-what-would-qualify-an-artificial-intelligence-for-moral-stan.md) argues goals can suffice. Unresolved: whether non-felt preferences matter in themselves.

2. **Which error is worse for AI?** [Long et al.](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md) and the Problem Profile stress under-attribution at scale. [Bengio and Elmoznino](readings/08-illusions-of-ai-consciousness.md) and [Birch's Challenge One](readings/06-ai-consciousness-a-centrist-manifesto.md) stress over-attribution harms (control problems, dependency, manipulation). Long et al. say it is open and system-dependent; the strongest synthesis is that both are real and they interact.

3. **Does mitigating one risk worsen the other?** Birch's central worry: debunking the illusion can make conscious AI look impossible; serious science of AI consciousness can raise misattribution. Whether a policy avoids the trade-off is unresolved. Birch's "brainwashing" and "lobotomising" pitfalls mark what to avoid.

4. **Can precarity be the criterion without begging the substrate question?** Dorsch et al. say simulated precarity is not real because hardware does not rebuild its structure. Functionalists would reject the premise that substrate decides. A data centre that autonomously replaces its own components is an awkward case.

5. **Safety versus welfare.** Bengio and Elmoznino fear welfare commitments would obstruct control; Long et al. and the Problem Profile treat them as separable and potentially synergistic; the experts in [Expert Forecasts](readings/07-futures-with-digital-minds-expert-forecasts-in-2025.md) were centred on neutral (median 4 out of 7) with a slight tilt toward synergy.

6. **Is the field tractable?** The Primer and Problem Profile reply to the intractability objection by pointing to probabilistic, empirical work. Birch's Janus problem suggests deadlock may persist; his falsifiable-prediction programme is the proposed escape.

7. **Is the evidence base strong enough?** Much "why now" evidence (user numbers, model self-assessments, the single Colombatto and Fleming survey) is anecdotal or secondhand. The expert survey is small (67) and self-selected toward people who take the topic seriously. Treat "people perceive AI as conscious" as well supported and "AI is conscious" as not established.

8. **Does the memetic lens help or just discredit?** [Kulveit](readings/10-the-memetics-of-ai-successionism.md) explains why ideas spread irrespective of truth, which can be turned against pro-welfare arguments as easily as against successionism. Strongest version: it is a reason for metacognitive caution, not a refutation of any view.

9. **Conflicts of interest.** Several [Long et al.](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md) authors are affiliated with Eleos AI or Anthropic, and companies building the systems judge their moral status. A reader should weigh recommendations to companies with that in mind.

## Exercises

**Exercise 1 (free text, core, 5 minutes).** Pick one of consciousness, sentience, robust agency or precarity, and say whether it is necessary and/or sufficient for moral patienthood, with reasons. There is no single right answer; strong responses distinguish necessary from sufficient, argue rather than assert, and consider a counterexample.

Equipped by:
- Consciousness and sentience: [Primer](readings/01-introduction-to-digital-minds-primer.md) definitions; [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md) on grounds; [Ladak](readings/11-what-would-qualify-an-artificial-intelligence-for-moral-stan.md) for the claim that sentience may be sufficient but not necessary.
- Robust agency: Primer, [Long et al.](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md), [Ladak](readings/11-what-would-qualify-an-artificial-intelligence-for-moral-stan.md), and the experience-independent welfare discussion in [Expert Forecasts](readings/07-futures-with-digital-minds-expert-forecasts-in-2025.md).
- Precarity: [Dorsch et al.](readings/04-against-ai-welfare-care-practices-should-prioritise-living-s.md) is the only source; note it offers precarity as a pro tanto reason, not as necessary or sufficient. The Glossary has no precarity entry.
- Calibration against convenient answers: [Kulveit](readings/10-the-memetics-of-ai-successionism.md) and [Birch](readings/06-ai-consciousness-a-centrist-manifesto.md).

**Exercise 2 (free text, core, up to 10 minutes).** For foundation models, AI agents, whole brain emulations, neuromorphic computing and biological-computer hybrids, estimate how likely each is to have the four features now and in the future.

Equipped by:
- System descriptions: [Primer](readings/01-introduction-to-digital-minds-primer.md) (today's and future systems) and [Glossary](readings/05-introduction-to-digital-minds-glossary.md); the Glossary has no entry for biological-computer hybrids or a dedicated agents entry.
- Foundation models and agents: [Long et al.](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md) (language-agent discussion is beyond the assigned excerpt), [Birch](readings/06-ai-consciousness-a-centrist-manifesto.md) on the gaming problem and global workspace, [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md) on indicator properties and the Chalmers estimate.
- WBE: [Problem Profile](readings/09-problem-profile-moral-status-of-digital-minds.md) four-premise argument; [Expert Forecasts](readings/07-futures-with-digital-minds-expert-forecasts-in-2025.md) type probabilities.
- Neuromorphic and hybrids: the Primer's descriptions, the integrated-information discussion in the Problem Profile, the biological-function lens in [whenaiseemsconscious.org](readings/12-whenaiseemsconscious-org.md), and [Dorsch et al.](readings/04-against-ai-welfare-care-practices-should-prioritise-living-s.md) on precarity, whose xenobot caution is the nearest relevant material.
- Keep in mind that behavioural evidence for chatbots is weak ([Birch](readings/06-ai-consciousness-a-centrist-manifesto.md), [whenaiseemsconscious.org](readings/12-whenaiseemsconscious-org.md)).

## Discussion questions

1. Recall: state the Primer's default meanings of consciousness and sentience. Why does it separate both from intelligence, and why does it separate moral agency from moral patiency?
2. Recall: what do over-attribution and under-attribution mean in [Long et al.](readings/02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md), and why do the authors say "err on the side of caution" is not enough for AI?
3. Explain: what are Birch's Challenge One and Challenge Two, and how can a remedy for one worsen the other? Give a concrete example.
4. Explain: what is the Precarity Guideline, and why do Dorsch et al. think "Is AI precarious?" is more answerable than "Does AI suffer?"
5. Apply (Exercise 1): which one of consciousness, sentience, robust agency and precarity do you judge necessary for moral patienthood, and which sufficient? What is your best counterexample to your own view?
6. Apply (Exercise 2): choose one system among the five and one feature among the four. Give a probability now and in 2035, and name the reading that most shaped your number and the strongest reason it could be wrong.
7. Analyse: the Problem Profile's whole brain emulation argument leans on an emulation's self-reports, while the same article says AI self-reports are unreliable. Is there a principled difference between the two cases?
8. Analyse: the experts in [Expert Forecasts](readings/07-futures-with-digital-minds-expert-forecasts-in-2025.md) rate brain simulations most likely conscious in principle but machine learning systems most likely to come first. Does that make ML systems morally more or less important to plan for?
9. Evaluate: [Bengio and Elmoznino](readings/08-illusions-of-ai-consciousness.md) say belief in AI consciousness is risky whether or not true and recommend tool-like AI. Does that solve the problem, defer it, or ignore the under-attribution risk?
10. Evaluate: [Kulveit](readings/10-the-memetics-of-ai-successionism.md) argues ideas spread because they resolve tension, not because they are true. Could that argument be applied equally to views for and against AI welfare, and what would let you tell them apart?
11. Judge: Dorsch et al. frame the choice as AI welfare versus care for the Amazon, endangered species and people. Is the framing zero-sum? What would the Problem Profile's reply be?
12. Judge: after this section, would you describe yourself as a centrist, a welfare-first precautionist, a safety-first sceptic or a living-systems prioritiser? What single piece of evidence in the next six months would move you?
