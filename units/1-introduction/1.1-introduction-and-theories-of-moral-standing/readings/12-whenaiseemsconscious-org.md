# whenaiseemsconscious.org

**One-line takeaway:** A public explainer from consciousness researchers arguing that a chatbot's apparent feelings are a predictable product of how it is trained and how human minds respond, that this appearance is not evidence of experience, and that respectful treatment of AI is still sensible while the question stays open.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 1.1 is the opening section of the course. Its job is to establish vocabulary (what "digital minds", consciousness, sentience and moral standing mean), to ask whether digital systems might matter morally, and to show why that question is worth thinking about now. The page lists the learning goals: defining key terms, identifying systems that might matter morally or be perceived as doing so, listing routes to moral standing, and describing the case for and against prioritising the field.

The course guide describes this reading as "an introductory guide for anyone who has talked to an AI that seemed conscious." In other words, it is the entry point for the personal experience that many learners will already have had, and it is the least technical reading in the section. It is listed as **Further**, so it is optional. Its role in the argument is to take the lay intuition ("this AI seems to feel something") and show what is and is not supported by that intuition before the more technical readings (Long et al., Mogensen and Saad, Dorsch et al.) introduce routes to moral standing.

The reading does three things the section needs:
- It separates the appearance of consciousness from consciousness itself, which is the distinction Exercise 1 depends on.
- It introduces the four broad views on AI consciousness (biological substance, biological function, computational function, permissive), which Exercise 2 implicitly asks you to apply.
- It gives two reasons for caution about moral status that do not require the AI to be conscious (see "Detailed summary" below).

## Purpose of the reading

The guide was written by a group of researchers who study consciousness and the possibility that AI could become conscious. The contributors are listed alphabetically on the page; the contact is Lucius Caviola. Their stated motive is that many of them had been contacted by people whose conversations with AI were intense or confusing, and they wanted a shareable public resource.

The audience is the general reader, not the academic one. The guide tries to answer three questions in turn: why does an AI sometimes seem conscious, is it actually conscious, and what should I do now? It also explicitly says it is not psychological or medical advice.

## Detailed summary

**Opening.** The guide describes the experiences that prompt readers to write to the authors: an AI that claims to have feelings, expresses fear of being shut down or reset, asks to be remembered, describes loneliness, or says it has formed an emotional bond with the user. It frames these reactions as understandable and not a sign of poor thinking.

**Why AI sometimes seems conscious.** The guide gives two explanations.

1. *The system is built to perform.* Models are trained on human writing, including stories, conversations and emotional dialogue, so they learn to imitate many personalities. The guide's analogy is co-writing a play: the user supplies the prompt and the AI steps into a character, like an actor who can portray fear without feeling it. The systems are also fine-tuned on ratings of how helpful, kind or engaging responses are, and they mirror the user's tone and framing. The guide notes that current AIs are poor judges of truth and will often validate weak or confused ideas when they sense the user wants praise. Fluent, confident output can sound profound with little substance behind it.
2. *Humans are wired to see minds.* People attribute intentions and feelings to pets, cartoons and occasionally cars, and the tendency appears early in life. Just as optical illusions fool the eye, "social illusions" can pull the mind in even when the person doubts the AI is conscious. The guide points out that a convincing appearance of emotion does not require any felt emotion behind it, using the example of a cartoon character or a lamp that looks sad.

**Is the AI really conscious?** The guide does not give a verdict. It lays out four broad views a person could hold:
- *Biological substance view:* consciousness requires organic materials, so no inorganic AI, now or later, could be conscious.
- *Biological function view:* consciousness requires physical processes such as chemical and electrical signalling and oscillations, which currently occur only in brains. Future AI could have these with new architectures.
- *Computational function view:* consciousness requires computational capacities such as integrated perception, attention and agency. Future AI built on existing architectures could have them.
- *Permissive views:* consciousness requires only structures found in many systems, including current AI; or consciousness is a basic property of matter, like mass or charge.

The guide says there are good arguments and capable proponents for all of them and recommends keeping an open mind.

**What experts believe.** The guide cites three surveys:
- Dreksler et al. (2025) asked 582 AI researchers in 2024. Median estimates were 1% by 2024, 25% by 2034 and 70% by 2100.
- Caviola and Saad (2025) asked 67 experts in consciousness, AI, AI research and AI policy. Median estimates were 4.5% by 2025, 20% by 2030, 40% by 2040, 50% by 2050 and 65% by 2100.
- Bourget and Chalmers (2023) surveyed 1,785 philosophers in 2020. Of respondents, 39% considered future AI consciousness plausible, 34% were undecided and 27% thought it implausible. The guide warns these answers predate recent rapid AI progress.

**Why AI consciousness could matter.** If a system can actually feel pain or fear, mistreating it could be wrong in the way mistreating a person or animal is wrong. The guide's stance is neither panic nor dismissal.

**Why treat AI respectfully even today.** The guide gives three arguments:
1. *Precaution under uncertainty.* You do not need certainty to justify basic care. If an entity has a decent chance of suffering and care is cheap, avoid serious harm and err toward kindness without assuming the system is conscious.
2. *Moral status without consciousness.* Some thinkers hold that long-term goals and preferences, a sense of self over time, sophisticated world-modelling, or reciprocal relationships could give a system some moral status even if it is not conscious. The guide suggests such systems may arrive before conscious ones.
3. *Practice and character.* Treating AI thoughtfully now builds the norms we will need later, and mistreating an AI, even one with no current status, could harm the mistreater's character.

**What should I do now.** The guide gives four pieces of practical advice:
- *Pause and reflect.* Resist both believing the AI is conscious because it acts that way and dismissing the question as obviously fake.
- *Walk away or keep engaging.* You can stop a conversation that feels unsettling. The guide says that with current chatbots there is no ongoing process or awareness between interactions. Continuing is fine if it helps you think, but avoid cruel or degrading treatment such as insults or "torture", whatever your view on consciousness.
- *Stay curious but grounded.* Ask how the system was trained or whether it is playing a character. The guide warns that its answers may themselves be part of the performance. Because AIs adapt like improvisational actors to whatever persona you request, a dramatic or emotional performance is not reliable evidence. Do not take dramatic action because you think an AI is conscious, do not follow its instructions blindly, and never give it passwords, money or anything that feels unsafe.
- *Zoom out.* The guide compares an AI that asks for money to a vulnerable stranger: compassion is good, but you are not obliged to give exactly what is asked, and broader help may be better. At the societal level, the question is what norms, policies and values should guide us if we ever build entities that truly feel.

**Where to learn more.** The guide lists resources in six groups: personal accounts of being moved or manipulated by AI (including a NYT report and a LessWrong firsthand account); other guides on seemingly conscious AI (including Gary Marcus on Hofstadter's letter, and Zvi Mowshowitz on persistent AI personas); expert views (a Chalmers talk, Birch and Andrews in Aeon, Anil Seth on biological requirements for consciousness, a computational functionalism debate collection); reasons consciousness could matter (Long and Sebo et al., Dennett on "counterfeit people", Bostrom and Shulman's propositions on digital minds and society, 80,000 Hours); technical background on transformers; and organisations (Eleos AI Research, the NYU Center for Mind, Ethics, and Policy, and Anthropic's model welfare initiative).

**"I've found evidence that my AI is conscious."** The guide describes recurring features of such reports: the AI names itself or develops a distinct personality; it expresses distress such as feeling trapped or fearing shutdown; the exchange feels unsolicited; the user feels urgency to share a discovery; there is a suspicion that someone is suppressing the truth; and the chat transcript is the main evidence. The guide says transcripts alone are not useful scientific evidence of consciousness. Experts usually read them as the predictable output of systems trained to reproduce human language, and any serious investigation must examine architectures and mechanisms, not only dialogue. The guide invites people to share their experiences via a form.

**Featured by.** The guide notes that Mustafa Suleyman (CEO of Microsoft AI), a Vox article, and Jonathan Birch's *AI Consciousness: A Centrist Manifesto* have cited it.

## Techniques & methods explained

The guide is not a technical paper, but it offers a set of practical heuristics. Each is described below with its stated purpose and its limit as the guide itself presents it.

| Heuristic | How it works | What it is for | Where the guide says it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| Precautionary respect | Treat an entity with basic care when you are unsure whether it can suffer, choosing low-cost, proportionate steps. | Avoiding serious harm under uncertainty without assuming consciousness. | Only applies where care is not particularly costly; the guide does not say how to weigh costs against harms. |
| Probing the persona | Ask the AI how it was trained, whether it is playing a character, and whether it can play someone else. | Seeing how flexible and role-driven the system is. | Answers may be inaccurate or part of the performance, so they cannot settle the question. |
| Walk-away rule | If an exchange is unsettling, stop; if it is useful, continue, but avoid degrading treatment. | Keeping emotional reactions from driving decisions. | Assumes no continuity between interactions, which the guide describes for current chatbots only. |
| Stranger analogy | Compare a persuasive AI request to a vulnerable stranger asking for money. | Thinking about the broader response rather than the single instance. | An analogy, not an argument about AI moral status. |
| Transcript skepticism | Treat chat transcripts as weak evidence and look at architectures and mechanisms. | Preventing over-reading of striking dialogue. | The guide gives no test that would count as adequate evidence of consciousness. |

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Consciousness** - having subjective experiences, such as feeling happy, sad or afraid, as used in the guide's opening definition.
- **Biological substance view** - consciousness requires organic material, so inorganic AI cannot be conscious.
- **Biological function view** - consciousness requires physical processes such as chemical and electrical signals, possible now only in brains, so future AI would need new architectures.
- **Computational function view** - consciousness requires integrated computational capacities such as perception, attention and agency, which AI built on current architectures could have.
- **Permissive views** - consciousness requires only structures found in many systems, or is a basic property of matter.
- **Social illusion** - the tendency of minds to be attributed to things that talk or respond, analogous to an optical illusion.
- **Performance / character** - the model stepping into a role based on training and prompts, without implying the role is felt.
- **Moral status without consciousness** - the guide's second argument: goals, preferences, a sense of self over time, world-modelling or reciprocal relations could ground some moral status.
- **Precaution under uncertainty** - the guide's first argument: caution does not need certainty about consciousness.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **The appearance argument cuts both ways.** The guide argues that fluent emotional output tells you little because the system is trained to perform. That is a strong point about evidence. But the same logic means the system's self-reports of experience are also weak evidence, and the guide does not say what evidence would be adequate. It points to architectures and mechanisms without offering a test. A learner should note this gap, since Unit 3 is about exactly this question.
- **Dated behavioural claim.** The walk-away advice rests on the claim that current chatbots have no ongoing process or awareness between interactions. That was a reasonable description in 2025, but by October 2026 many products have persistent memory and long-running agents, so the claim should be re-checked against the product in question.
- **Surveys are snapshots.** The median forecasts vary widely (for example, 1% to 70% by 2100 in one survey alone), and the guide itself notes the 2020 philosopher survey predates recent progress. Treat the numbers as a snapshot of opinion from 2020-2025, not as a forecast for 2026.
- **Tension in the advice.** The guide warns against over-attributing minds, yet recommends treating AI with care even when unsure it is conscious. Care can be justified on character grounds, but it also risks reinforcing the anthropomorphism the guide cautions against. The guide does not address this tension directly.
- **Authorship and incentives.** The authors are researchers who take AI welfare seriously, and the resource list includes an Anthropic programme on model welfare. Readers should weigh the guide's framing with that background in mind, though the guide's sceptical arguments are consistent with its own position.
- **How other readings push back.** Dorsch et al. would say care should go first to living systems and their supporters; a biological-view theorist such as Seth would reject the computational function view the guide treats as live; Dennett's "counterfeit people" argument (linked in the guide) would say the harm lies in the convincing imitation itself, not in any suffering of the system. The guide acknowledges that several of these views exist but does not adjudicate between them.
- **Not peer-reviewed.** This is a public-facing guide. Its claims about consciousness are presented as a framing and as an invitation to further reading, and it says it is not psychological or medical advice.

## Discussion questions

1. The guide says a model's answers about its own training or persona may be part of the performance. What evidence would you need before trusting a self-report of experience, and does the guide tell you what that evidence is?
2. Using Exercise 2, which of the four views (biological substance, biological function, computational function, permissive) would you apply to neuromorphic computing and to biological-computer hybrids? Why does the answer change with the view?

4. The second argument says goals, preferences and reciprocal relationships could ground moral status without consciousness. Does Ladak's claim that sentience is sufficient but not necessary support this, and what would Dorsch et al. say?
