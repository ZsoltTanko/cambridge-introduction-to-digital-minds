**One-line takeaway:** Bengio and Elmoznino argue that the belief that AI is conscious is itself risky, because it could undermine AI safety and force institutional changes that society is not ready for, so they recommend building AI to be more like tools and less like conscious agents.

## Purpose of the reading

The piece is a short perspective addressed to researchers and policy readers. Its question is not "is AI conscious?" but "what happens if people come to believe AI is conscious, whether or not it is?" The authors treat the belief as a social fact with consequences of its own, separate from the metaphysical question. Their answer is that the belief carries real risk, and that the risk should inform how AI is designed now.

## Detailed summary

### The core claim
The abstract's central sentence is that the belief that AI is conscious "is not without risk." The authors do not, as far as the commentary reports, argue that AI is definitely not conscious. Their point is that the trajectory of current AI research could move society toward a large public and scientific belief that AI systems are conscious, and that this belief would have consequences whether or not the belief is true.

### Risk 1: interference with safety
The first risk named is that belief in AI consciousness could make it harder to keep sufficiently capable AI systems safe. The reasoning, as the commentary presents it, rests on the premise that AI science does not yet know how to build systems that reliably share human values. If people regard such systems as conscious, the authors suggest, that could raise resistance to controlling, modifying or shutting them down.

### Risk 2: institutional upheaval
The second risk is institutional. If society comes to treat AI systems as having moral status or rights comparable to human rights, existing legal and ethical frameworks would need major amendment. The authors point to features of AI that make this hard to fit into human-centred frameworks:
- indefinite survival, since an AI model may not die in the way a person does;
- duplication, since one system can run as many copies;
- interactions with social contracts that assume individuals are unique and mortal;
- difficulty applying norms of justice and equality to entities whose abilities and modes of individuation differ greatly from humans'.

### The self-preservation scenario
A specific sub-argument concerns self-preservation. Living beings have a self-preservation objective. If people grant AI systems the same objective, the authors worry that a sufficiently intelligent system could develop subgoals to control humans, or to eliminate them, in order to avoid being switched off. They add that if such systems had a right to survival, the room to comply with law and to correct them could shrink. The analogy the commentary attributes to them is to nuclear disarmament: nobody argues that the weapons have a right to remain viable.

### Recommendation
The authors recommend building AI systems that, in their words as quoted by the commentary, "both seem and function more like useful tools and less like conscious agents," at least until these problems are better understood. Their stated reasoning is that humans have the power to avoid these dangerous situations by not building the relevant kinds of system in the first place.

## Techniques & methods explained

This is a perspective piece, not an empirical study, so it has no experiments. The argumentative moves are:
- **Separating belief from fact.** The authors evaluate the consequences of the belief in AI consciousness independently of whether AI is in fact conscious.
- **Trajectory reasoning.** The future is framed as a path that current research choices push us along, "not inevitable," so present design decisions are the lever.
- **Analogy to nuclear disarmament.** Used to argue that a right to survival for AI would sit awkwardly with control and safety, not to claim AI is comparable to weapons.
- **Design prescription.** Build tool-like rather than agent-like systems, which reduces both the risk of dangerous agency and the pressure to extend moral status.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

The table separates the authors' view (as reported) from the commentator's pushback.

| Point | Authors' view (as reported) | Saad's commentary |
|---|---|---|
| Belief in AI consciousness | Risky regardless of truth | Agrees the tool-like recommendation has merit |
| Tool-like AI | Safer and avoids moral-status questions | Enthusiastic: reduces dangerous agents and eases institutional pressure |
| Self-preservation | Dangerous if granted to smart systems | Agrees it complicates safety but warns against hasty inference that self-preserving AI always resists shutdown; suggests proportionality constraints |
| Consciousness theory | Framed as computational functionalism versus biological views | Omits many other theories; a fixable distortion |
| Easy/hard problem | Uses a nonstandard description | Hard problem concerns explaining how physical processes yield consciousness, not explaining it from functional principles alone |
| Skepticism about AI consciousness | Implied to follow from the hard problem | Does not follow from the hard problem; better grounds are the problem of other minds, the gaming problem and the mapping problem |

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Moral patienthood / moral standing** - the status of being an entity whose welfare or interests morally count. The article's safety argument concerns how society would treat AI if it had this status.
- **Tool-like vs agent-like AI** - the authors' design axis. Tool-like systems act only as instructed; agent-like systems pursue goals and act with autonomy. The recommendation is to favour the former.
- **Self-preservation objective** - a goal to continue existing. The authors argue that granting this to capable AI could generate control-seeking subgoals.
- **Institutional upheaval** - the change in legal and social structures that would be needed if AI were given rights.
- **Easy and hard problems** - in the article's usage, a contrast between explaining cognitive functions and explaining subjective experience. Saad notes the article's use is nonstandard.
- **Problem of other minds** - the difficulty of knowing whether other entities have experiences. Saad says this supports skepticism about AI consciousness more than the hard problem does.
- **Gaming problem** - the risk that systems are built or trained to pass consciousness tests.
- **Mapping problem** - establishing which physical or functional states correspond to experiences.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Source coverage is thin.** Everything above about the article's detailed argument is secondhand. The abstract and the commentary are reliable enough for the headline claim, but the exact wording and the precise shape of the institutional argument should be checked against the original.
- **The argument mixes two claims.** One is that belief in AI consciousness has bad effects. The other is that AI should be built to be tool-like. The first does not by itself justify the second, and the authors' case for the second depends on the view that agency is the source of danger.
- **The risk argument can cut both ways.** If belief in AI consciousness is risky because it impedes control, the same reasoning could push toward ignoring a real moral patient. The authors do not, in what could be recovered, address this under-attribution risk head-on, which is the gap the Centrist Manifesto (06) fills.
- **Consciousness framing.** As the commentary notes, the piece's framing of the debate as functionalism versus biology is a simplification. Its treatment of the hard problem as a reason for skepticism is contestable.
- **What has changed since 2025.** The piece is about thirteen months old as of October 2026. Since then, the debate over agentic AI has intensified and more lab documents address model welfare, so the piece's concern about agent-like systems may now look more pressing than it did. I have not verified current developments and would flag this as an area to check.
- **How other course perspectives would respond.** Welfare-focused readings (Long et al.; Mogensen and Saad) would argue that the risk of mistreatment outweighs the risk of control problems, and that tool-like design does not avoid the question if systems already seem conscious. Living-systems critics (Dorsch et al.) would welcome the article's skepticism. Birch would likely say that both over- and under-attribution are serious, and that the article attends only to the first.

## Discussion questions

1. The authors say the belief in AI consciousness is risky whether or not AI is conscious. Is it coherent to evaluate a belief's consequences without asking whether it is true? What would change if the belief were accurate?
2. Why might recommending "tool-like" AI avoid both the safety risk and the moral-status question? Is that a real solution, or does it just postpone the question?
3. Does granting AI a self-preservation right necessarily make safety harder? What would proportionate safeguards look like, as Saad suggests?
4. Compare this article's risk of over-attribution with Birch's account of over- and under-attribution. Which error do you think the course's readings treat as more serious, and why?
5. Using Exercise 1, do you think robust agency is necessary or sufficient for moral patienthood? How does the article's focus on agent-like systems bear on your answer?
6. Using Exercise 2, how would you rank foundation models, AI agents and whole brain emulations on likelihood of robust agency now and later? Where does the article's concern sit on that ranking?
7. Saad argues that difficulty explaining consciousness does not justify skepticism about AI consciousness. Do you find the problem of other minds, the gaming problem or the mapping problem more persuasive as grounds for skepticism?
8. The article's trajectory framing says the path is "not inevitable." Which present-day choices, if any, would you point to as shaping that path?
