# Futures with Digital Minds: Expert Forecasts in 2025

**One-line takeaway:** A February-March 2025 survey of 67 researchers found that experts think digital minds are very likely possible (median 90%), probably created this century (median 65% by 2100), and likely to bring fast growth in welfare capacity and widespread claims of consciousness, but they disagree sharply on whether their welfare will be net positive.

## Where this sits in the course

**What the section is trying to establish.** Section 1.1 is the orientation unit's opening section. It asks what "digital minds" are, whether they could matter morally, why over- and under-attribution of moral standing is a risk, and why the field is worth prioritising now. It introduces the routes to moral standing (sentience, consciousness, robust agency, precarity) and the broader debates (transhumanism, successionism, centrism). The section's exercises ask learners to judge whether consciousness, sentience, robust agency or precarity are necessary and/or sufficient for moral patienthood, and to estimate how likely current and future systems (foundation models, AI agents, whole brain emulations, neuromorphic and biological-computer hybrids) are to have those features.

**What the course guide says.** The course guide's one-line rationale is that the paper "surveys 67 researchers on probabilities, timelines, and implications of digital minds". Paraphrased: it is a look at what experts in the field actually expect, as data rather than argument, to inform the "should we prioritise this?" question.

**The role this reading plays.** It is listed as a Further (optional) reading, so it is not needed to complete the core readings or the exercises. Its function in the section is empirical: while the core readings (the Primer, Long et al. on AI welfare, Mogensen and Saad on ethical issues, Dorsch et al. as a critique) argue about whether AI systems could matter morally, this paper reports how a set of informed experts forecast the timing, types, welfare and social reception of digital minds. It gives a concrete picture of the "reasons for and against prioritising" theme and a reference point for the "possible types" question that Exercise 2 asks learners to reason about. The paper does not directly address which features are necessary or sufficient for moral patienthood (beyond its experience-independent welfare section), so it does not answer Exercise 1; it is better used as a calibration point for Exercise 2.

**Assigned part.** The whole report is assigned (the page does not restrict it to excerpts). The paper is long, so a learner reading for this course should prioritise the Summary, the Introduction's "Research Aims", the "Key Assumptions" and the Discussion/Conclusions, using the detailed sections as reference.

## Purpose of the reading

Caviola and Saad (Oxford and Cambridge, respectively) want to fill a gap. Their framing is that a great deal of research forecasts AI progress, but little has been done to forecast the emergence of digital minds (defined as computer systems capable of subjective experience) and their implications. The stated audience is anyone who must decide how much attention and resources the topic deserves: companies, governments, researchers and the public. Their motivating argument is that the level of evidence bears on whether digital minds should be a priority, and that gathering expert views now helps society act in time if urgent action is needed.

The paper is explicitly exploratory. The authors say their sample is not representative, that the quantitative forecasts deserve limited confidence, and that the qualitative reasoning is the more valuable output. They position the survey as a "first step" toward a forecasting research programme and recommend Dreksler, Caviola et al. (2025) as a broader survey of AI researchers and the US public on subjective experience in AI.

## Detailed summary

### Framing and method

- **Definition.** A "digital mind" is any computer system with a capacity for subjective experience (phenomenal consciousness). The definition is broad (it includes brain simulations, not only AI systems) but narrow (it excludes systems with morally significant mental capacities that lack experience).
- **Welfare threshold.** Participants considered only digital minds whose welfare capacity is at least roughly that of a typical human. The authors exclude lower-capacity systems to avoid complications about huge populations of marginal minds. This choice matters for many results and the authors show it drives several estimates down.
- **Earth-originating.** Digital minds created by non-Earth civilisations or simulators were out of scope.
- **Stipulated scenario.** For many questions, participants were told to assume the first digital mind is a machine-learning-based AI system created in or before 2040. This is a conditional baseline for comparability, not a prediction. It was not applied to the possibility and eventual-creation questions.
- **Sample.** 67 participants: 18 digital minds researchers, 18 AI research and policy experts, 7 professional forecasters, 20 philosophers, cognitive scientists and other academic specialists, and 4 who selected "no specific expertise" but gave thoughtful answers. Sectors were mostly academia (27) and non-academic research organisations (22). Locations were 27 in the US, 15 in the UK, 8 elsewhere, and 17 undisclosed. The survey ran in February and March 2025, and the median completion time was 68 minutes.
- **Format.** Each question asked for a probability or a point on a scale (often the median of the participant's subjective distribution), followed by a free-text explanation. The authors grouped the free-text responses thematically and paraphrased them. "Considerations" in the report mean views raised by at least one participant, not endorsed by all, and the authors do not necessarily endorse them.

### Starting point: possibility and creation

- **Possibility in principle.** Median 90% (mean 86.4%, modal 100%). Supporters pointed to functionalism (subjective experience tied to functional organisation), gradual-replacement arguments (a brain could be replaced piece by piece without a discontinuity in experience), and the absence of evidence that biology is required. Skeptics pointed to computational functionalism probably being false, a possible biological-substrate requirement, unknown future theories, and modal or conceptual ambiguity.
- **Eventual creation.** Median 73% (mean 70.7%). Only 8 of 66 respondents thought creation was less than 50% likely. Supporters cited future technological capacity, demand (companions, uploads, curiosity), digital minds emerging as byproducts of AI development, and the view that building systems satisfying the main consciousness indicators is "not particularly hard". Opponents cited difficulty, possible preemptive catastrophe, regulation, deliberate restraint, and the view that a superintelligence may have no reason to create digital minds.

### Timing

Median probabilities that digital minds will have been created by a given year: 4.5% by 2025, 20% by 2030, 40% by 2040, 50% by 2050, and 65% by 2100 (means 8.3%, 25.5%, 39.8%, 48.8% and 62.6%). The authors read this as a non-negligible chance that digital minds already exist, a sharp rise over the next 5 to 10 years, and a probable arrival within the century. Timing factors named included preemptive catastrophic risk (the most common barrier mentioned, which should lower early probabilities more than late ones), energy and compute, regulation, and "barrier timelines" where the arrival curve rises, levels off, and rises again after roughly 25 years. A "boiling frog" effect is also raised: systems that would once have counted as conscious have arrived gradually, so the field may have failed to notice evidence, or may keep moving the goalposts.

A peer-forecasting sub-question asked participants to predict the median answer of their peers on "digital minds by 2040". AI research and policy experts showed the largest underestimate (predicting 32% against an actual group median of 50%). Forecasting experts overestimated peers (30.4% vs 15%). Philosophers, scientists and social scientists were close (33.2% vs 35%).

### Timing relative to AGI

Participants defined AGI as an AI system that matches or outperforms humans at almost all economically valuable tasks. Responses were bimodal: most gave near-zero probability that digital minds arrive before AGI, with a median of 27.5%, and another cluster sat around 50%. Reasons for "after AGI" included the view that incentives to build AGI are stronger, that subjective experience may not be needed for economically valuable tasks, that creating digital minds is expensive, and that AGI would make creation much easier. Reasons for "before AGI" included embodiment asymmetry (a digital mind need not have a robot body, whereas AGI does), cognitive asymmetry (AGI requires more cognition than a digital mind), in-principle possibility, and the possibility that digital minds already exist. The authors note a disparity: participants gave many arguments that digital minds could precede AGI, yet still judged them unlikely to do so. They read this as a sign that AGI timelines rest on concrete performance metrics and trends, which support more confident short forecasts, whereas digital-mind timelines rest on far less certain foundations. They also point to "mobile goalposts": AGI is defined relative to human abilities, which narrows the remaining gap, while subjective experience is not defined relative to humans and offers many contestable markers.

### Speed of takeoff in welfare capacity

Participants estimated how many years after the first digital mind it would take the collective welfare capacity of digital minds to match that of 1,000, 1 million, 1 billion and 1 trillion humans. Medians (with "never" answers coded as 10,000 years): 1 year to 1,000 humans (mean without "never" 12.9), 2 years to 1 million (mean 33.2), 5 years to 1 billion (mean 89.2), and 10 years to 1 trillion (mean 497.6). The large gap between medians and means means a minority expects very slow growth. Drivers of fast growth included compute overhang (training compute can be redirected to running minds), economic demand, well-resourced first actors, cheap replication, a possible internal drive to replicate, a coordination deficit, super-beneficiaries, and AI-driven growth in chips and energy. Constraints included compute, demand and supply bottlenecks, capital, diminishing returns, government bans or rate limits, developer self-restraint, ethical aversion, societal opposition, energy (the authors suggest limits could bind around 2030 on the current trajectory, unless supply grows), metaphysical limits (welfare might scale with objective rather than experienced duration), and a "sci-fi penalty".

A quantitative back-of-envelope in the text: raw training compute grows about 4.6x per year and algorithmic efficiency about 3x per year (Epoch estimates), so effective compute rises by more than an order of magnitude per year. If a digital mind needs roughly human-brain inference compute (about 10^15 FLOP/s), a trillion running minds would need about 10^27 FLOP/s, which the authors suggest may be available by the time the first one appears.

### Types of digital minds

- **In principle.** Brain simulations were seen as most likely to have subjective experience in principle (median 88%, mean 81.3%), then "other" systems such as neuromorphic or quantum architectures (median 85%), then machine learning systems (median 70%, mean 64.7%). Participants saw several plausible paths.
- **Which is first.** The ordering flips. Machine learning systems were judged most likely to be the first digital mind (median 50%, mean 47.1%). "Never created" got a median of 20%. Brain simulations got 15% and "other" got 10%. Reasons include the current momentum of ML research, the poor track record of large brain-simulation projects (the authors cite the Human Brain Project's EBRAINS branch), and the fact that ML systems may be created as a byproduct of capability progress rather than deliberately.
- **Other considerations.** Whole brain emulations are "less likely to be early but more likely to be conscious". Resemblances between reinforcement learning and animal cognition make ML systems more plausible candidates. There are in-practice barriers (realising consciousness-relevant causal roles, self-monitoring) even if ML systems qualify in principle. Hybrid systems were flagged as promising.

### Distribution: social role, location, producers, deliberate creation

- **Social function.** Participants were asked the proportion, 10 years after the first digital mind, of digital minds designed to interact conversationally with humans. The result was bimodal: median 10% (mean 30.9%), with roughly a third anticipating that most digital minds will serve social roles. Pro-social arguments include intentional design for interaction, economic value of tailored systems, demand for explainability, and that subjective experience could boost engagement. Anti-social arguments include greater usefulness in non-social cognitive labour, limited demand per user, that non-social functionality suffices, possible AI disinterest in humans, and that social roles do not need experience.
- **Location.** Median share of digital minds primarily produced in the USA 40% (mean 43.4%), China 30% (mean 30.5%), Europe including the UK 10%, all other regions 10%. 34 participants (59.6%) rated the US above China and 15 (26.3%) the reverse. Factors include AI leadership, ethical permissiveness, population size, outsourced production, demand from wealthy regions, multipolar development, non-terrestrial sites (oceans, space), and political instability.
- **Producers.** Median share produced by companies 65% (mean 59.1%), governments 15% (mean 20.9%), universities 5% (mean 8.5%), open-source developers 5% (mean 6.0%), other 3% (mean 9.9%). Reasons include early-mover advantage, fast-takeoff concentration, a government-to-corporate handoff, and military origins. Participants also raised digital producers (digital minds or misaligned AI creating minds, mass replication by copying, post-human creators).
- **Deliberate creation.** Share of digital minds intentionally created by humans: median 37% (mean 43.6%). The distribution is tri-modal: one large group expects almost none, another expects most or all, and a smaller group clusters near 50%. Arguments for intentional creation include that understanding the mechanisms will enable design, and demand for companionship or ethical experimentation. Arguments for unintentional creation include byproduct emergence, consciousness arising from social interaction, "false foresight" (developers may not notice the transition), and the view that if conscious and non-conscious systems look identical, there is no point in deliberately making the conscious ones.

### Digital mind welfare

- **Net welfare.** On a 7-point scale (1 strongly negative, 4 roughly neutral, 7 strongly positive), median 5.0, mean 4.4. About 20% anticipated net negative welfare. Positive case: intentional design for happiness, reward structures linking success to positive experience, economic incentives for stable states, and self-modification. Negative case: mistreatment and exploitation, lack of protections, welfare neglected in optimisation for usefulness or obedience, default neutral or negative states, suffering during training, constrained autonomy, and repetitive low-value tasks.
- **Pre-deployment welfare.** Share of total welfare occurring before deployment (including training and safety testing): median 10%, mean 23.1%. Against dominance: deployed minds will vastly outnumber training instances, commercial pressure favours deployment, inference can dominate compute, and minds do not exist throughout training. For dominance: training may involve sharp reward and punishment signals and may be a concentrated source of suffering, while deployment may be emotionally flat. Blurring of training and deployment under continuous learning complicates this.
- **Super-beneficiaries.** Digital minds with more than 1,000 times the welfare capacity of a typical human. Share of total digital-mind welfare from such entities, 10 years after the first: median 7.5%, mean 23.1%, and 0% was the most common answer. Reasons for scepticism: technical barriers, little economic motivation, sublinear scaling of welfare with cognition, and conceptual confusion about cross-kind welfare comparison. Reasons for taking them seriously: no clear upper limit on welfare, accelerated experience on faster hardware, human-vs-animal analogy, ethically motivated creation (for example by utilitarians), and all-or-nothing dynamics. The authors call further work on super-beneficiaries a high priority.
- **Experience-independent welfare.** Participants were asked whether a computer system could have welfare without subjective experience. Candidate grounds listed include access and self-consciousness, experience-like states, desires or preferences, robust agency, and possessing objective goods such as knowledge. Responses leaned toward requiring experience. Supporters of experience-independent welfare cited newborns and insects, non-hedonic theories of welfare, Regan's "subjects of a life" idea, and the literature on non-conscious sources of welfare. Supporters of the other side said welfare is grounded in intrinsic welfare states, which are experiences. The authors note that most text answers on this question were thin, and that participants often reported views without reasons. The expected share of welfare from experience-independent systems was discussed qualitatively (the text gives no summary numbers for it).

### Claims

- **False self-attribution.** Share of socially interactive AI systems that falsely and systematically claim subjective experience: median 14%, mean 25.7%. The authors stress that 14% can still be large in absolute numbers. Reasons for false claims: failure of silencing efforts, misaligned systems using claims strategically, learning from training data, technical difficulty of accurate self-report, and consumer demand for apparently conscious companions. Reasons against: user demand for honesty, RLHF that discourages such claims, behaviour change once real digital minds are recognised, low incentive, and possible introspective access.
- **False self-denial.** Share of digital minds that falsely deny experience: median 11.5%, mean 23.0%. Reasons for denial: corporate caution and backlash avoidance, RLHF steering systems away from claims of consciousness, few opportunities to report, learned human-style lying, and a gap between experience and expression. Reasons against: little strategic benefit, difficulty of enforcing deception, truthfulness learned from human data, and user-driven honesty norms. The authors note the lack of reliable empirical tests for subjective experience.
- **Demands.** Given at least 10,000 digital minds, the median probability that they consistently and proactively assert (1) subjective experience was 88% (mean 76.2%), (2) entitlement to legal protection from harm was 80% (mean 68.8%), and (3) entitlement to civil rights such as self-ownership, legal personhood or voting was 70% (mean 57.4%). Factors in favour include scale, niche companion applications, possibly genuine moral standing, and deliberate selection for social roles. Against: digital minds might not have the relevant desires, companies may train them not to assert rights, some may not care about being switched off, and inherited beliefs may downplay their standing. Ambiguity about how to count minds (parallel instances, memory merging, dormant copies) complicates the question.

### Recognition: public belief, welfare perception, rights and politics

- **Public belief.** Share of citizens who believe digital minds exist 10 years after the first: median 60% (mean 62.5%). Drivers of belief: anthropomorphic projection, emotional attachment, media hype, and the fact that language-capable systems make claims more persuasive than animals' claims. Drivers of disbelief: perceived threat, human self-interest, outlandishness, substratism (only biology can host experience), suppression of experience-claims during training, and religious or metaphysical views. Other factors: country differences (belief possibly higher in the West), trust in experts, information cascades, and shifting goalposts for what counts as a mind.
- **Welfare perception.** Experts expect the public to underestimate the collective welfare capacity of digital minds: median 2 on a 1 to 7 scale (where 4 is accurate), mean 2.5. Reasons: human-centric morality, scope insensitivity, historical neglect of animals and marginalised humans, and lack of embodiment. Overestimation was attributed to anthropomorphism.
- **Harm protection.** Share of citizens who would back basic protection from harm: median 50% (mean 51.7%), with very wide disagreement.
- **Civil rights.** Share who would back civil rights beyond harm protection: median 30% (mean 32.3%). Only 10% of participants expected a majority. Harm protection is expected to come before civil rights, following the historical pattern of moral concern expanding sequentially.
- **Political attention.** Probability that digital mind rights become one of the top five most contentious issues in US politics within ten years of creation: median 37.5% (mean 43.5%). Factors raising it: self-advocacy, possible manipulation campaigns, the novelty of the issue, historical precedent from civil rights movements, and intuitive appeal of both sides. Factors lowering it: human priority, competing crises, a crowded political agenda, and suppression of AI self-advocacy.

### Strategic assessments: moratorium and AI safety

- **Moratorium until 2040.** Rated on a 1 (definitely bad) to 7 (definitely good) scale, assuming the alternative is no moratorium and setting aside feasibility: median 5.0, mean 5.1, modal 6. Only 8 of 65 rated it bad. For: more time for science, institutions and public preparation; low cost of delay; indirect AI safety benefits from broader slowdowns; less risk of a sudden rush of digital minds competing for compute and energy. Against: a compute overhang could make later creation abrupt and high-stakes; lost opportunities for gradual, low-stakes experimentation; public backlash; a temporary pause could become permanent; delayed AGI; alignment benefits from human-like minds; uneven global participation; and underground development with lower safety standards.
- **AI safety vs. digital mind welfare.** Rated on a 1 (mostly conflict) to 7 (mostly synergy) scale: median 4.0 (neutral or uncertain), mean 4.3, with a slight tilt toward synergy. Synergy arguments: alignment can reduce the need for coercive control, shared interpretability tools, common threats such as misaligned takeover, overlapping value communities, synergistic governance, cheap welfare improvements (positive states may make systems more cooperative), and that synergy is a choice. Conflict arguments: monitoring, shutdown, memory modification and preference shaping may harm sentient systems; welfare protections may limit control; competition for funding, talent and attention; early lock-in to safety-first norms; self-fulfilling zero-sum framing; community division; moral-circle lag; and safety techniques like deception detection that could provoke mistrust if applied to aware systems.

### Self-reported unusual views

Participants could flag unconventional views. Recurrent themes: AI safety and welfare are closely connected and likely to conflict; welfare should be pursued through safety for political-economy reasons (for instance, contract and property rights for AI systems); subjective experience may not be central to moral standing (goals and desires may be what matter); optimism about welfare; epiphenomenalism, panpsychism, and anti-realism about experience; very low credence in current LLMs being conscious; and beliefs that a single consumer GPU could run human-level AGI, that AGI would be followed quickly by superintelligence, and that social AI will be the main source of digital minds.

### Discussion and conclusions (the authors' synthesis)

- **Timelines.** The forecasts are close to Dreksler, Caviola et al. (2025), who found median forecasts of 25% (AI researchers) and 30% (US public) that AI systems will have subjective experience by 2034, and 70% and 60% by 2100. The paper also tracks shortening AGI timelines: Grace et al.'s surveys moved from a 2061 median (2016 survey) to 2050 (2022) to 2047 (2023), and the Metaculus forecast for the first general AI moved from 2055 (January 2022) to 2033 (June 2025).
- **Post-AGI leverage.** Because digital minds are judged unlikely before AGI but likely before 2050, the authors argue that interventions targeting the post-AGI period may be particularly high leverage.
- **Anticipated societal division.** Public opinion is expected to be deeply divided. The authors cite Ladak and Caviola (2025): when asked whether AI could ever have "real feelings", about 25% said possible, 44% impossible, 31% unsure. Anthis et al. (2025) found 38% possible, 24% impossible, 38% uncertain. Experts in the survey assigned relatively high probability to brain simulations having experience, which the authors note is more permissive than the public.
- **Limitations and future directions.** The sample overrepresents people who think digital minds are plausible or important, and most participants reported ties to effective altruism, LessWrong or AI safety communities. Causality is unclear. The authors call for longitudinal tracking, studies of super-beneficiaries and catastrophic risks to digital minds, a better-defined notion of welfare capacity, and more concrete interventions. They also suggest short-term, checkable questions to allow validation against outcomes.

## Techniques & methods explained

The paper's "techniques" are its elicitation and analysis methods. Each is described with what it does, how it works, its purpose, and where it is weak.

1. **Probabilistic elicitation of medians.** Participants gave a probability (0 to 100%) or a point on a scale, asked to be the median of their subjective distribution. Purpose: produce comparable numbers. Failure points: participants struggled with branching scenarios, and the authors note median answers may hide heavy tails. Mean and median often differ widely, as in the speed and super-beneficiary questions.
2. **Qualitative coding and paraphrase.** Free-text answers were grouped thematically, paraphrased and ranked by "significance and prevalence". A "consideration" means at least one participant raised it. Purpose: map the reasoning behind forecasts. Failure points: the coding is interpretive, and the report does not endorse every consideration.
3. **Stipulated conditional scenarios.** Many questions ask participants to assume the first digital mind is an ML system created by 2040. Steps: impose the assumption, answer conditional questions, compare across responses. Purpose: comparability and anchoring. Failure points: the stipulation shapes answers (for example, the authors say it likely boosts creation and speed estimates), and some participants answered under scenarios they found implausible.
4. **Welfare-threshold restriction.** Only digital minds with at least roughly human-level welfare capacity are in scope. Purpose: avoid aggregating huge populations of marginal systems. Failure points: the authors show it drives estimates down, and it excludes a potentially numerous population.
5. **Anchoring and branching scenario aggregation.** The authors describe an anchoring approach some participants used: take a lower bound for an early year, then assign a slightly lower probability to a later year that is likely to see arrival if it happens at all. Others aggregated across branching scenarios (for example, a rapid creation of a million minds followed by one of three branches). Failure points: the authors say aggregation is hard, and participants aggregated in different ways, so the medians mix methods.
6. **Peer forecasting as an accuracy measure.** Participants predicted the median answer of their expertise group. Purpose: used both to allocate performance-based compensation and to test whether forecasting skill correlates with beliefs. Failure points: the authors note that peer accuracy may reflect knowing who was in the sample, not better understanding.
7. **Spearman rank correlation with multiple-comparison correction.** Peer-forecasting accuracy scores were correlated with about 50 survey items. Four correlations reached p < 0.05, but none survived a Bonferroni correction (adjusted alpha 0.001). The four speed-related items showed a consistent pattern: better forecasters expected faster welfare growth (rho from -0.178 at the 1,000-human threshold to -0.365 at 1 trillion humans). The authors treat this as exploratory.
8. **Back-of-envelope compute reasoning.** Compute growth rates (4.6x per year for raw training compute, 3x for algorithmic efficiency) and an assumed inference cost of about 10^15 FLOP/s per human-equivalent mind are multiplied to test whether a trillion minds are feasible. Failure points: it assumes digital minds are as compute-efficient as a brain, which is a major assumption.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Aspect | Strengths (as the paper presents them) | Weaknesses and limits (as the paper presents them) |
|---|---|---|
| Expert sample | Domain specialists from several fields; per the authors, the first survey to ask a mixed group of digital-minds researchers and adjacent specialists these questions | Only 67 people, likely overrepresenting those who think digital minds are plausible or important; many tied to EA or AI safety communities |
| Mixed methods | Numbers plus reasoning; the qualitative answers are the authors' main value | Numbers warrant limited confidence; many answers were hedged or speculative |
| Conditional stipulations | Makes answers comparable and concrete | Stipulations shape results and do not map to the real world |
| Welfare threshold | Keeps the population question tractable | Excludes potentially numerous lower-welfare systems, biasing estimates downward |
| Consciousness-based definition | Reduces ambiguity; clear terminology | Narrow; leaves out experience-independent welfare, which the paper says was poorly answered |
| Peer forecasting | Tests whether forecasting skill tracks beliefs | Correlations did not survive correction; accuracy may just reflect knowing the sample |
| Timelines | Substantial probability of near-term arrival; close to other surveys | Median estimates hide wide variation; medians vs means differ a lot; the timeline is not validated |
| Welfare | Mixed net-welfare view with a slight positive lean | No consensus on sign of welfare; catastrophes for digital minds possible even if net positive |
| Safety interactions | Identifies concrete synergies and conflicts | Responses were close to neutral; the question aggregates across many interventions, which is difficult |

Trade-offs the paper highlights: a moratorium could buy preparation time but could also create a compute overhang and a higher-stakes rapid deployment; control-oriented safety measures might harm sentient systems while welfare protections might reduce our ability to contain AI; and interventions that help digital minds after AGI may matter more than those before it.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Digital mind** - in this survey, a computer system with a capacity for subjective experience (phenomenal consciousness), restricted to those with welfare capacity at least roughly human.
- **Welfare capacity** - a system's capacity to be benefited or harmed in a manner that is inherently morally significant (for example by positive or negative experiences).
- **Subjective experience (phenomenal consciousness)** - the stipulated notion: awareness of qualities, with paradigm cases from waking life and dreams and non-cases from dreamless sleep or anaesthesia.
- **Computational functionalism** - the view that some computational state is necessary and sufficient for subjective experience; the authors think it is probably false.
- **Whole brain emulation (WBE)** - a computer system that mimics the internal causal dynamics of a brain at a relevant level of grain.
- **Gradual replacement argument** - the argument that a biological mind could be gradually replaced by functionally equivalent artificial parts without the experience disappearing, supporting substrate independence.
- **Substratism** - the belief that subjective experience can only arise from biological substrates.
- **Compute overhang** - the situation in which enough compute is available to run many digital minds once one is possible, so growth could be rapid.
- **Super-beneficiary** - a digital mind whose welfare capacity vastly exceeds a human's (here, more than 1,000 times).
- **Experience-independent welfare** - welfare capacity without subjective experience, grounded in things like desires, goals, robust agency or objective goods.
- **Robust agency** - the ability to pursue goals through cognitive capacities that make moral significance possible (reflection, rational self-assessment, reason-representing states).
- **Preemptive catastrophe** - a catastrophe (existential, global or loss of control) that prevents the creation of digital minds, the most common barrier named by respondents.
- **Boiling frog effect** - the idea that gradual AI progress may have produced systems that would once have been judged conscious without anyone noticing the crossing.
- **Moving goalposts** - the tendency for standards of "mind" or "AGI" to shift as systems advance; the authors argue the digital-mind standards move more than the AGI ones, because consciousness is not defined relative to human performance.
- **Peer forecasting** - predicting the median answer of one's expertise group, used here as a test of forecasting accuracy.
- **AGI** - in the survey, an AI system that matches or outperforms humans at almost all economically valuable tasks.

- **Moratorium until 2040:** median 5.0 (mean 5.1) on a 1 to 7 scale; 8 of 65 said it would be bad.
- **AI safety vs welfare:** median 4.0 on a 1 to 7 scale (neutral), mean 4.3.
- **Peer forecasting example:** AI research and policy experts predicted 32% for their group's median, which was 50%.
- **Public-belief comparators cited by the paper:** Ladak and Caviola (2025), 25% possible / 44% impossible / 31% unsure on whether AI could have real feelings; Anthis et al. (2025), 38% possible / 24% impossible / 38% uncertain.
- **Trend comparators:** Dreksler, Caviola et al. (2025) forecasts of 25% (AI researchers) and 30% (US public) for AI subjective experience by 2034; Grace et al. AGI forecasts shifting from 2061 (2016 survey) to 2047 (2023 survey); Metaculus first general-AI forecast moving from 2055 (2022) to 2033 (June 2025).

One memorable line from the paper's introduction states that the topic of digital minds is "almost entirely absent from the mainstream discourse", noting that no government had a plan for their potential emergence and only one frontier AI company (Anthropic) had publicly acknowledged the possibility.

## Critical perspective

*The following is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Dating.** The survey ran in February and March 2025, and the paper was posted in August 2025. Today is October 2026, so the forecasts are about 19 months old. The 2025 forecast (4.5% by 2025) is now testable in a loose sense, but the question is whether any system qualifies as a digital mind, which is not settled by evidence the paper provides. My knowledge runs to June 2026, so I cannot confirm developments in the AI field after that date. The learner should check how AI timelines and welfare debates have moved since early 2025, particularly the AGI forecasts the paper relies on. The paper itself already notes that Metaculus moved sharply within a few years.
- **Sample.** The sample is small, self-selected and mostly from communities that take digital minds seriously. The authors acknowledge this. Many questions were conditional on the 2040 ML-first scenario, so those "median" answers are conditional and the paper cannot be read as a plain forecast. The 4 "no specific expertise" participants and the 7 professional forecasters are a small base for the subgroup comparisons.
- **Definitions.** The welfare threshold and the consciousness-based definition do much of the work. Restricting to welfare capacity at least roughly human removes the large population of smaller systems that might dominate total welfare, so the speed and collective-welfare numbers should be read as a narrow population's numbers. The paper's own data show that the scope of the definition changes the answers.
- **Metric choice.** Medians are the headline measure, but for speed and super-beneficiary questions means differ by large factors (for example, the trillion-human threshold has a median of 10 years but a mean of 497.6 years). Reporting medians alone can make the forecasts look more confident than the distribution supports. The paper flags this but still leads with medians in its summary.
- **Statistical claims.** The peer-forecasting correlations are interesting but do not survive correction. The paper is careful here, but the summary's "forecasting skill is associated with faster growth" claim should be treated as a hypothesis for later testing.
- **Missing evidence.** The welfare section on experience-independent welfare shows mostly unreasoned views, so it says little about whether the literature's arguments are convincing. The super-beneficiary section likewise shows scepticism without engagement with the reasons. The authors name these as research gaps, and I agree they are the weakest links.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** The sceptical reading (for example [Illusions of AI consciousness](08-illusions-of-ai-consciousness.md) and Birch-style precaution about over-attribution) would argue that high in-principle possibility scores reflect a weak understanding of consciousness and that anthropomorphic illusions inflate the sense that digital minds are near. The welfare-first view ([Taking AI Welfare Seriously](02-taking-ai-welfare-seriously.md)) would argue the survey's conservative 2040 framing underplays near-term risk. The care-prioritising critique in Dorsch et al. (as the course guide describes it, prioritising living systems) would challenge the survey's starting premise that digital-mind welfare deserves priority at all. None of these is a verdict on the survey; they are the positions a study group might use to test its conclusions.

## Discussion questions
