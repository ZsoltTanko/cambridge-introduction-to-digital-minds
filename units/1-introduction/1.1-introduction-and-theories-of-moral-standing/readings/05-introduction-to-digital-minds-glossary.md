# Introduction to Digital Minds Glossary

**One-line takeaway:** A reference list of the course's stipulated vocabulary (consciousness, sentience, moral standing, functionalism, LLMs and more), to be consulted whenever a later reading uses one of these terms.

## Where this sits in the course

**Section aim.** Section 1.1 opens the course. Its page asks what "digital minds" means, whether such systems might matter morally and why that is worth considering now, and why over- and under-attribution of moral standing is a concern. Its learning objectives are mostly definitional: define consciousness, sentience and moral standing; identify systems that might matter morally or be perceived to; list routes to moral standing; describe transhumanism and successionism; weigh reasons for and against prioritising digital minds work; and describe "centrism".

**What the course guide says.** The guide says the glossary "introduces the key terms we will discuss on this course", that each unit will have its own unit-specific glossary, and that this one is there for general reference.

**Role in the section.** This is a Further resource, so it is optional, and it does no argumentative work of its own. Its job is to fix meanings before the Core readings are read: the Primer (reading 1) and the Core papers by Long et al., Mogensen and Saad, and Dorsch et al. use these words in specific senses. The glossary also states that its definitions are "for the purposes of this course", so they are stipulations for the course rather than settled consensus. Read it alongside the Primer, not in place of it.

**Gaps relative to the section page.** Transhumanism, successionism and centrism are named as learning objectives on the page, but the glossary has no entries for them. Precarity, one of the four features in Exercise 1, is also absent. Consciousness, sentience and robust agency are covered.

## Purpose of the reading

The glossary is a reference document written by the Cambridge Digital Minds course team for learners. It does not argue for a position. It tells the reader how the course uses a set of terms from AI safety, AI ethics, philosophy of mind, moral philosophy, law, and AI forecasting, and where a term is contested. It is most useful as a lookup table during the Core readings, and as a place to check whether two authors mean the same thing by "sentience" or "moral status".

## Detailed summary

The document is organised alphabetically (A to Z). It opens with a note that the definitions are the course's working definitions. The entries group naturally into five clusters, which the walk-through below follows. The alphabetical order is not reproduced here.

### Cluster 1: AI field definitions (Safety, Ethics, Alignment, Interpretability, Governance)

- **AI Alignment** means making AI systems do what their designers intend, sometimes called intent alignment. A system is aligned when it advances its intended objectives and misaligned when it pursues objectives that are unintended and conflict with those it was given.
- **AI Ethics** targets current and near-term risks to humans: harmful content, privacy violations, mental-health effects and biased models that already discriminate. The entry says the boundary with AI Safety is blurred and that the two fields differ in emphasis.
- **AI Safety** is described as a broadly technical field about making AI safe for humans, especially against catastrophic or existential harms. Named worries include superintelligent models aiding power concentration or locking in a bad governing system, models pursuing unintended objectives they cannot be stopped from pursuing, and misuse in ways comparable to chemical, biological or nuclear weapons.
- **AI Interpretability** is the study of what happens inside models. Mechanistic interpretability looks for causal structures, algorithms or circuits in the weights. Other methods look for higher-level patterns, such as attention visualisations. The entry notes both are young and that critics doubt models are simple enough for the simplifications these methods need.
- **AI Welfare** is the potential wellbeing of AI systems, and also the field that asks whether AI systems can have interests, experiences or preferences that matter morally, and if so how they should be protected. It is described as distinct from but related to AI Safety.
- **Digital Minds Governance** covers policies, regulations and institutions for managing the risks and ethical questions raised by systems that are, or may be, conscious or morally significant. It overlaps with broader AI governance.

### Cluster 2: Consciousness, mind and agency

- **Consciousness** (default use: phenomenal consciousness). Something is conscious if there is "something it is like" to be it (Nagel, 1974). The entry is explicit that this is not about intelligence, self-concept or sense of self; it is about having experience at all. The entry notes it may be an on/off property or may have borderline cases.
- **Access consciousness** is availability for report, reasoning and control. Mere influence on behaviour does not count. It is contrasted with phenomenal consciousness, though the two may interact.
- **Seemingly conscious AI (SCAI)** refers to systems that imitate consciousness convincingly, or that have features (voices, faces, human-like social skills) that lead users to attribute consciousness to them.
- **Qualia** are the introspectively accessible, phenomenal aspects of experience. In standard usage the term names felt qualities, not functional or representational roles.
- **Sentience** is valenced subjective experience: there is something it is like to be the system, and those experiences feel good or bad. On many views sentience is enough for moral standing.
- **Robust agency** is a capacity to set and pursue goals through mental states that function like belief, desire and intention (the entry quotes Long et al., 2024). "Robust" refers to the representational structure, so a reinforcement-learning agent can show goal-directed behaviour without robust agency. The entry adds that robust agency and consciousness can come apart, and that under some theories robust agency alone can qualify an entity for moral patienthood.
- **Intelligence** is defined as the "ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments" (Legg and Hutter, 2007), with the entry noting this definition is controversial. Block's giant look-up table is the standard counterexample. The entry stresses intelligence is distinct from consciousness.
- **Anthropomorphism** is attributing human traits to non-human things. In digital minds research it is treated as a bias source, because product design (for example, giving a chat interface a name) can produce perceived consciousness from surface cues.
- **Anthropodenial** is the reflexive denial of human-like traits in non-human entities. The entry suggests scepticism about AI minds may itself reflect motivated reasoning.
- **Behaviorism** grounds psychology in observed behaviour. The entry distinguishes a mild version (seek behavioural evidence) from an extreme one (explain behaviour without reference to inner states).
- **Interpretivism** holds that mental states can be reliably inferred from behaviour when the inference makes sense of the behaviour and predicts further behaviour. The entry notes most philosophers think this is not sufficient for genuine mental states.
- **Functionalism** holds that consciousness depends on functional organisation: the relations between internal states, inputs and outputs, not on the substrate. **Computational functionalism** narrows this to the claim that the relevant organisation is computational, so consciousness depends only on algorithmic manipulation of information.
- **Substrate independence** (also "substrate flexibility") is the claim that the same kinds of mental states can occur in very different physical substrates, not necessarily carbon-based.
- **The gaming problem** holds that consciousness tests for AI are unreliable because models are trained on human data about experience and are often trained to seem human. The entry notes this can cut both ways: a model that is incentivised to deny consciousness may also "game" a test.
- **Zombie, philosophical** is a thought experiment: a being that behaves exactly like a human but has no subjective experience.

### Cluster 3: Moral standing, status and theories

- **Moral agent** is an entity that can bear duties and responsibility. Moral agency and moral patiency can come apart: a human infant is a patient but not an agent, and the entry imagines an AI that is an agent without being a patient.
- **Moral patient** is an entity that matters for its own sake, so that it can be harmed or benefited in a way that counts morally.
- **Moral standing** is whether an entity merits ethical concern or consideration at all.
- **Moral status** is the degree of that concern. The entry notes that many traditions rank entities (humans above mice), while animal-liberation positions descending from Singer (1975) argue for equal consideration of comparable interests.
- **Moral circle** is the set of entities a person or society counts as moral patients. The entry notes it has historically expanded.
- **Personhood, moral** means equivalent moral standing to a human, and the entry notes that which properties yield it is debated.
- **Personhood, legal** means being a subject of legal rights and duties. The entry notes purely legal persons can differ in standing (corporations can own property but cannot marry), and that the concept has been applied to ships and rivers.
- **Rights, negative and positive.** Negative rights are duties of non-interference (not to be harmed, deceived or manipulated). Positive rights are duties of provision (maintenance, access to information, conditions for flourishing).
- **Welfare subject** is an entity that can accrue welfare goods or bads, meaning things that are good or bad for it or that alter its wellbeing.
- **Consequentialism** judges an action by its consequences. **Deontology** judges it by whether it complies with moral norms, with duties and rights constraining what may be done for good outcomes. The entry names Kant's principle that rational agents are ends in themselves, and the standard critiques of each.
- **Utilitarianism** is the classic consequentialist theory that the right action maximises overall welfare. The entry names its critiques: it ignores distinctions between persons, and it can justify serious harm to individuals when aggregate outcomes look favourable.
- **Virtue ethics** focuses on character, asking what a person of good character would do. Its noted weakness is limited guidance in novel or uncertain situations.
- **Population ethics** evaluates welfare outcomes that involve different numbers of beings. The entry introduces the Repugnant Conclusion and notes most people find it counterintuitive.
- **Suffering risks (s-risks)** are risks of astronomical future suffering.
- **Uncertainty, descriptive** is uncertainty about matters of fact. The entry says it may be contingent (resolvable with better evidence or theory) or intractable (due to limits on what can be known). For AI consciousness both apply.
- **Uncertainty, moral** is uncertainty about what is ethically right or which framework should guide action, even when the non-moral facts are known.
- **Precautionary principle** says that under threat of significant harm, scientific uncertainty should not delay proportionate protective measures. The entry notes multiple formulations, and that the EU version attaches extra conditions (non-discrimination, cost-benefit analysis, consistency, and building scientific understanding).

### Cluster 4: Digital minds and AI systems

- **Digital Minds** is the field concerned with computer systems' potential mental states and moral significance, and also the systems it studies. The entry notes the term is used in different ways. The field also considers analogue and other non-digital systems.
- **Foundation models** are models pre-trained on large data that can be adapted to a range of tasks. **Large Language Models (LLMs)** are the most prominent class.
- **LLMs** are transformer models trained to predict the next token. After pre-training they typically undergo post-training, often reinforcement learning from human or AI feedback (RLHF or RLAIF). The entry notes that LLMs are multimodal, are deployed with agentic harnesses (tools, memory, external data), and underlie chatbots and companions. It says some capabilities that may bear on consciousness were not trained directly, and that whether they do is disputed.
- **Neuromorphic computing** is hardware designed to mimic neurons. Some theories of consciousness make it more relevant than conventional chips. The entry says most deployments are at pilot or early commercial stage.
- **Whole brain emulation (WBE)** is replicating the main functions of a brain in a computer. The entry describes connectome mapping (mapped so far for C. elegans and Drosophila), the scale problem of about 86 billion human neurons, and subtler factors such as neuromodulation and glial action.

### Cluster 5: AI risk, capability and forecasting

- **AI Psychosis** refers to mania-like episodes, delusional thinking or paranoia that can emerge or worsen through chatbot use. The entry flags that it is disputed whether this is a media label or a medical category.
- **Artificial General Intelligence (AGI)** is a system that matches or exceeds human cognitive ability across most domains. The entry shows that definitions differ (OpenAI, DeepMind, and a group of researchers all use different framings) and that some dispute whether the term is still useful.
- **Artificial superintelligence (ASI)** is a system that greatly exceeds human cognitive ability in all domains.
- **Transformative AI (TAI)** is AI that causes a transition comparable to the agricultural or industrial revolution. It is defined by its effects on wellbeing and society, not by capabilities.

## How the terms fit together

- **Two axes for moral standing.** Consciousness and sentience are the "experience" axis. Robust agency, welfare subjecthood and the capacity for valenced states are other routes. Moral standing (whether something counts) and moral status (how much it counts) are separate questions. Several entries (Moral agent, Moral patient, Moral circle) help separate them further.
- **Routes to consciousness.** Functionalism and computational functionalism give substrate-independent routes. Substrate independence is the broader idea. Neuromorphic computing and WBE are entries that matter only if substrate and structure matter. This is where the field's theory choices become concrete.
- **Evidence problems.** Interpretivism, behaviourism, the gaming problem, Anthropomorphism and Anthropodenial all concern how we would know, from outside, whether a system has mental states. Descriptive uncertainty names the deeper point that subjective experience is not directly accessible.
- **Decision-making under uncertainty.** Moral uncertainty, descriptive uncertainty, precautionary principle and the governance entries are the vocabulary for acting before the facts are settled.
- **Adjacent fields.** AI Safety, AI Ethics, AI Alignment and AI Welfare are presented as neighbouring fields that overlap in goals and differ in emphasis.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs

The glossary is short on evaluation. Points where it notes a contested definition:

- Intelligence is defined functionally, which the entry itself challenges with the look-up table case.
- The meaning of AGI and superintelligence varies by group and may be losing usefulness.
- Whether computational functionalism is correct depends on which kind of computation is meant.
- Digital Minds has no single agreed definition within the field.
- AI Ethics and AI Safety are described as overlapping but in tension, with different groups weighting risks differently.

## Key concepts & terminology

Grouped as in the walk-through above. Each entry is paraphrased from the glossary.

- **AI Alignment** - making AI systems do what they are intended to do; aligned systems advance their intended objectives.
- **AI Safety** - technical field on keeping AI development safe for humans, especially against catastrophic harms.
- **AI Ethics** - field on near-term risks such as harmful content, privacy, bias and mental-health impacts.
- **AI Interpretability** - study of the internal workings of models, mechanistic or pattern-based.
- **AI Welfare** - potential wellbeing of AI systems and the field asking whether they can matter morally.
- **Digital Minds Governance** - institutions and policies for systems that may be conscious or morally significant.
- **Consciousness (phenomenal)** - there is something it is like to be the system.
- **Access consciousness** - content available for report, reasoning and control.
- **Seemingly conscious AI (SCAI)** - systems that convincingly imitate or cue consciousness.
- **Sentience** - valenced subjective experience, with felt pleasure or pain.
- **Qualia** - the felt, phenomenal character of experience.
- **Robust agency** - goal-setting via belief-like and desire-like representational states.
- **Functionalism / computational functionalism** - consciousness depends on functional organisation (and, in the computational version, on computation).
- **Substrate independence** - the same mental states can occur on different physical substrates.
- **Anthropomorphism / Anthropodenial** - over-attribution and under-attribution of human traits to non-humans.
- **Gaming problem** - AI systems can be trained or prompted to pass consciousness tests.
- **Interpretivism / Behaviorism** - inferring mental states from behaviour, with different strengths of commitment.
- **Moral agent / moral patient** - a bearer of duties vs an entity that matters for its own sake.
- **Moral standing / moral status** - whether something merits concern, vs how much.
- **Moral circle** - the set of entities a society counts as moral patients.
- **Personhood (moral / legal)** - human-equivalent moral standing, vs being a subject of legal rights and duties.
- **Rights, negative / positive** - duties of non-interference vs duties of provision.
- **Welfare subject** - an entity that can accrue welfare goods and bads.
- **Foundation model / LLM** - large pre-trained models adapted to many tasks; LLMs are the prominent case.
- **Neuromorphic computing** - hardware designed to mimic neurons.
- **Whole brain emulation (WBE)** - replicating the main functions of a brain in a computer.
- **Descriptive vs moral uncertainty** - unknown facts vs unknown ethical framework.
- **Precautionary principle** - act protectively under threat of significant harm despite scientific uncertainty.
- **AI psychosis, AGI, ASI, TAI, s-risk, population ethics, consequentialism, deontology, utilitarianism, virtue ethics, philosophical zombie** - defined as listed in the walk-through.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the course's.*

- **Stipulation versus consensus.** The course labels its definitions "for the purposes of this course". That is honest, but learners should notice when a later reading uses a term differently. The sentience entry ("valenced subjective experience", and "on many views" sufficient for moral standing) is especially loaded, because it bundles a definition with a contested moral claim. Several other entries also flag their own disputed points (for example, whether LLM capabilities bear on consciousness, and whether AI psychosis is a medical category).
- **Currency.** The document is labelled "April 2026". Entries about recent systems (AI Psychosis, agent frameworks such as OpenClaw, Eon's 2026 fruit fly emulation, connectome progress "as of 2026") may be out of date by October 2026. I cannot verify any of these claims from my own knowledge, so treat them as the glossary's claims at its date.
- **Coverage of the page's objectives.** The section page asks for transhumanism, successionism and centrism, none of which are defined here. The Memetics of AI Successionism reading fills part of that gap, but transhumanism and centrism are not defined anywhere in what I have read.
- **Possible confusion on "consciousness".** The entry says "consciousness" by default means phenomenal consciousness, and it says "not intelligence or sense of self". Yet it also describes a separate access sense. Learners should check each reading's sense.
- **Asymmetry in the moral entries.** The Moral Status entry gives Singer's equal-consideration view as one option among several, and the Moral Standing entry is a single sentence. The glossary does not pick sides, but the coverage is uneven.
- **Other perspectives (my inference, not stated in the glossary).** The Dorsch et al. reading (reading 4) argues for prioritising living systems and so would likely question a sentience-centred framing of moral standing. The Ladak reading (reading 11) is the course's own example of a sentience-sufficient view, so the two readings can be compared directly against the sentience entry. The gaming problem and anthropomorphism entries are symmetrical in their warnings, which the glossary itself notes.

## Discussion questions

1. The glossary says moral agency and moral patiency "can come apart". Which entities in the Core readings are patients without being agents, and which are the reverse? Does that matter for the course's question?
2. Compare the glossary's definitions of sentience and consciousness. Can a system be sentient without access consciousness? Can it be conscious without valenced experience? Use the Exercise 1 features to test your answer.
3. The gaming problem says a tool that tests for consciousness could be gamed in both directions. What would a test need to show to avoid both over- and under-attribution, and which glossary entries apply?
4. Exercise 2 asks you to judge foundation models, AI agents, WBE, neuromorphic computing and biological-computer hybrids. Which of these does the glossary define adequately for that judgement, and what would you need to look up for the rest?
5. Functionalism and computational functionalism make different predictions about neuromorphic computing and WBE. Is the difference about substrate, about computation, or about both?
6. The glossary defines AGI in several competing ways. Does the course need a single working definition, or can it proceed without one? What does your answer imply for the forecasting reading (reading 7)?
7. Where does the precautionary principle fit in the debate about acting on digital minds under uncertainty, and what would the glossary's EU formulation require in practice?
