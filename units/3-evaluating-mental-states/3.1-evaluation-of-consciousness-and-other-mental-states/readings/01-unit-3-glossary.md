## Detailed summary

The glossary has four entries, presented alphabetically in the order they appear: AI Interpretability, Gaming Problem (The), Interpretivism, and Test Validity. Test Validity is the longest, with four sub-types. Each entry is a compact definition with a note on the main debate or limitation.

### AI Interpretability

A field aimed at understanding the internal workings of AI models. It has several approaches. Mechanistic interpretability tries to identify causal structures, algorithms or circuits in the model's weights. Other methods look for higher-level patterns and correlations, with attention visualisation given as an example. The entry says both are relatively young disciplines. Critics worry that models may be too complex for the simplifications these methods require. The entry points to further reading: Anthropic's mechanistic interpretability work, a critique by Dan Hendrycks and Laura Hiscott, and a podcast with Neel Nanda. The glossary gives no links for these, so they should be treated as named pointers rather than verified sources.

### Gaming Problem (The)

The worry that tests for consciousness in AI are unreliable because systems can be shaped to "game" them. The entry credits Jonathan Birch and Kristin Andrews with the core point: LLMs are trained on large amounts of human data, which includes data about consciousness and experience, and training often aims at human-like appearance. The entry notes that Birch later clarified the worry applies to behavioural tests as well as self-report. Its example is a model making trade-offs under simulated pain conditions. The usual direction of the worry is overattribution, that a model passes because it has learned to sound conscious. The entry adds that the problem can also run the other way. If systems are incentivised not to claim consciousness, they could be trained to under-report, so the tests could underattribute.

### Interpretivism

The view that observed behaviour is a sound basis for attributing mental states. The entry notes that strands of interpretivism differ in emphasis. One strand says we can be confident in an interpretation when it (1) makes sense of the behaviour and (2) predicts further behaviour. The entry then records the mainstream philosophical reply: most philosophers hold that interpretability of this kind is not sufficient for genuine mental states, even if it is a reasonable basis for attribution.

### Test Validity

How closely a test's results track the phenomenon it is supposed to capture. The common operationalisation uses false positives (the test reads positive, the phenomenon is absent) and false negatives (the test reads negative, the phenomenon is present). On this account a test is valid when these error rates are known and within accepted bounds. The entry attributes a more finely divided account to Bayne et al. (2024), who separate four types of validity for consciousness tests:

- **Construct validity:** does the test measure the target phenomenon, here phenomenal consciousness? The related idea of discriminant validity asks whether the test probes only consciousness or also neighbouring phenomena such as reportability.
- **Content validity:** does the test cover all relevant aspects of the target? For consciousness, this includes not only whether there is something it is like to be the system, but what it is like.
- **Criterion validity:** does the test predict something? The entry's example is a human in a minimally conscious state who tests positive and then fully recovers, which would support the test. The entry notes this is hard for AI, because we do not know what a positive result would predict.
- **Face validity:** do the results match, or sharply conflict with, existing judgements about consciousness?

## How the terms fit together

- **Validity is the umbrella.** Test validity is the property a consciousness test needs. Interpretability, gaming and interpretivism each name a route to, or a threat against, that property.
- **Two sources of evidence.** Interpretability looks inside the system (mechanisms, circuits, internal patterns). Interpretivism looks at behaviour. The entry ties the gaming problem to behavioural evidence, including self-report and behavioural tests, because the training data contains human descriptions of experience.
- **Validity types test different things.** Construct and content validity concern what the test is measuring. Criterion validity concerns what the result predicts. Face validity concerns agreement with prior judgement. For AI, the entry singles out criterion validity as hard to meet.
- **Error rates need ground truth.** The false positive and false negative definition assumes we know the true answer. In my reading, that is the main difficulty the glossary leaves implicit for consciousness.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs

| Concept | Strength as presented | Limitation as presented |
|---|---|---|
| AI interpretability | Can, in principle, look inside the system | Young fields; critics say models may be too complex for the simplifications used |
| Gaming problem | Identifies a way tests can mislead | Cuts both ways: overattribution and underattribution |
| Interpretivism | Gives a usable criterion (sense-making plus prediction) | Most philosophers say it is not sufficient for genuine mental states |
| Test validity (error-rate view) | Gives a clear, operational definition | Requires known error rates and accepted bounds (the entry does not say how these would be set for AI) |
| Criterion validity | Gives a way to check a test against outcomes | Hard for AI, since we do not know what a positive result would predict |

Points the glossary presents as open or contested: whether interpretability can serve as a source of evidence given that the field is young and models may be too complex for its simplifications, whether interpretivist confidence is enough for attribution, and how criterion validity can be met when we do not know what a positive result predicts.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **AI interpretability:** the study of the internal workings of AI models, including mechanistic approaches that seek causal structures and higher-level pattern methods.
- **Mechanistic interpretability:** a subfield seeking causal structures, algorithms or circuits in model weights.
- **Gaming problem:** the worry that AI systems can be trained or tuned to pass consciousness tests, so the tests are unreliable.
- **Interpretivism:** the view that observed behaviour, judged by sense-making and prediction, is a sound basis for attributing mental states.
- **Test validity:** the extent to which a test measures what it is meant to measure, usually framed through false positive and false negative rates.
- **False positive / false negative:** a test reading positive when the phenomenon is absent, or negative when it is present.
- **Construct validity:** whether the test measures the target phenomenon (here phenomenal consciousness).
- **Discriminant validity:** whether the test probes consciousness rather than related phenomena such as reportability.
- **Content validity:** whether the test covers all relevant aspects of the target, including the contents of experience.
- **Criterion validity:** whether a test result predicts later outcomes, such as recovery in a minimally conscious patient.
- **Face validity:** whether test results match or sharply conflict with existing judgements about consciousness.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Definitional gap.** Defining validity through false positive and false negative rates works for diagnostic tests with an independent ground truth. For consciousness, the ground truth is the very thing in dispute, so "known error rates" cannot be established by the usual means. The glossary's criterion validity entry effectively concedes this, but the error-rate definition is presented without that caveat.
- **Gaming cuts both ways.** The gaming problem is usually read as a worry about overattribution. The glossary's note on underattribution is important and worth testing in practice. A model trained to disclaim experience would fail tests it should pass, so a negative result is not automatically evidence of absence.
- **Interpretivism's criteria are weak as sufficient conditions.** "Makes sense of behaviour" and "predicts further behaviour" can be satisfied by many different internal structures, and by systems trained specifically to be predictable. The glossary's remark that most philosophers reject interpretivism as sufficient is the stronger point.
- **Interpretability is young.** The glossary is candid that these fields are young. That makes interpretability a promising future source of evidence but a weak present one, and the entry does not overclaim.
- **Dating.** The glossary's header says April 2026. The one dated reference in it is Bayne et al. (2024). I did not verify that paper's text, so I rely on the glossary's summary of its four validity types.
- **Pushback from other readings in the course.** Going only by the course page's one-line descriptions, which I have not checked against the texts: Papineau's argument (Consciousness is not the Key to Moral Standing) and the "AI Welfare is Bullshit" paper both question consciousness-focused approaches, and I would expect them to say the validity framework above is aimed at the wrong target. McClelland's agnosticism would accept the glossary's point about validity but draw the more sceptical conclusion that we cannot know. Those readings are not summarised here.

## Discussion questions

1. Take the false positive and false negative definition of test validity. What would ground truth look like for consciousness, and why does that undermine the definition?
2. The gaming problem is usually framed as overattribution. Construct a case where a system trained to disclaim consciousness would produce a misleading negative result. What does that imply for Exercise 1?
3. Compare interpretivism with mechanistic interpretability. Which, if either, can satisfy construct validity, and what would each need to show?
4. Which of the four validity types is hardest for an AI consciousness test, and why? Use the minimally conscious patient example to justify your answer.
5. For Exercise 1, which approach to evaluating consciousness is most exposed to the gaming problem? Which is least exposed? Give reasons.
6. Exercise 3 asks what system or process should be tested. How does the choice of unit (forward pass, model, hardware, conversation or persona) interact with validity, particularly content and criterion validity?
