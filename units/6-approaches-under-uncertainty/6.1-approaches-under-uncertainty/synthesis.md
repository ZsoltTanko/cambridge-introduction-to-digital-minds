# 6.1 Approaches under Uncertainty - Synthesis

*Unit 6: Approaches under Uncertainty | 120 min | 6 Core readings (one of them the 3-minute glossary), 4 Further, 4 Maybe (two of the Maybe items duplicate Core readings and one duplicates the Core frameworks primer)*

## What this section is teaching

Units 2 to 4 left the course with a hard fact: nobody can currently say whether any AI system has morally relevant experience, and our judgments about it are easily distorted. Unit 6 asks what to do anyway. The page poses three questions: what the precautionary principle is and how it might apply to AI; what suffering risks arise from creating, or merely appearing to create, digital minds; and what other frameworks could inform action.

The four stated learning goals are to:
- explain the precautionary principle and its application to AI systems;
- explain the suffering risks that digital minds could create;
- analyse current action and proposals from AI companies and researchers;
- identify existing frameworks and fields that could help.

The single question beneath all of them is: **how should anyone decide and act when both the facts (is this system sentient?) and the values (what do we owe it, if so?) are unsettled, and when mistakes in either direction are costly?**

In the unit's arc, 6.1 is the pivot from diagnosis to response. Earlier units say what is and is not known. Unit 5 considers how welfare interacts with safety. Unit 6 supplies the decision tools and tests them on a real case, Anthropic's treatment of Claude. Unit 7 then asks which institutions should carry those decisions. This is a single-page unit, so 6.1 is the whole of Unit 6.

## The core argument

**Two kinds of uncertainty.** The [Unit 6 Glossary](readings/01-unit-6-glossary.md) fixes the vocabulary. *Descriptive* uncertainty concerns what is true, for example whether a system is conscious. It can be contingent (more evidence might resolve it) or intractable (it resists resolution because subjective experience is not obviously open to third-person science). *Moral* uncertainty concerns what is right, and persists even when every non-moral fact is known. Most real AI cases involve both. The glossary also states the case for urgency, the suffering risk: digital systems can be copied and deployed at scale and may have different time perception, so if they can suffer, the total could be extreme. Note that this is a concern stated conditionally, not a finding.

**Why act now.** [How to deal with risks of AI suffering](readings/03-how-to-deal-with-risks-of-ai-suffering.md) (Dung) takes that premise and asks what an adequate response looks like. He proposes four desiderata: an approach should be beneficial, action-guiding, feasible, and consistent with our epistemic situation (it must not assume knowledge we lack). He argues that purely scientific approaches struggle on feasibility or epistemic consistency, so an explicitly ethical approach is needed alongside them: a hybrid of expected-value reasoning and deliberation. Caution: the summary of this reading rests on the abstract only, so treat the detail as provisional.

**The precautionary answer.** [The Edge of Sentience](readings/02-the-edge-of-sentience.md) (Birch) is the section's most developed framework. Its starting point is a duty almost everyone accepts, to avoid gratuitous suffering. Birch asks how that duty applies when sentience is uncertain, and rejects the "opt-out by disbelief" move: if you have not engaged with the evidence you are negligent, and if you have and refuse proportionate precautions you are reckless. The trigger is *sentience candidature*, meaning a realistic possibility that it would be irresponsible to ignore, backed by evidence rich enough to design precautions. A weaker category, *investigation priority*, marks systems worth studying. The response must be proportionate, which can mean doing nothing (his dust-mite example), and should be settled by informed, inclusive and democratic processes that are revisited as evidence arrives. For AI, Birch adds five proposals: markers should track sentience and not intelligence; markers that may have been gamed should be discounted; functionalist theories can supply "deep computational markers"; regulation should run ahead of current risk; and developers of possible candidates should be licensed under codes of good practice. Birch is explicitly a *moderate* precautionarian: he rejects hair-trigger bans, which he thinks never win consensus.

**The general toolbox.** [Frameworks for decision making under uncertainty](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md) shows that Birch's approach is one tool among several. The EU's precautionary guidance adds proportionality, non-discrimination, consistency, cost-benefit comparison of acting and not acting, and review against new data. The IPCC separates *likelihood* (how probable) from *confidence* (how far the estimate can be trusted), which matters because AI-sentience claims are often low-confidence. Expected utility, Bayesian updating and the moral-uncertainty strategies (favourite theory, maximising expected choiceworthiness, the Moral Parliament, convergence, maximin, dominance, reflective equilibrium) each help in some respects and fail in others. The recurring failure points are that inputs (priors, comparable scales) may not exist, and that tiny probabilities of huge stakes can swamp the calculation (Pascal's Mugging). The Further reading [Moral Uncertainty](readings/08-moral-uncertainty.md) (Bykvist) explains why the favourite-theory and expected-value shortcuts break, and [How Extreme is the Precautionary Principle?](readings/10-how-extreme-is-the-precautionary-principle.md) (Hansson, abstract only) argues the principle is not inherently extreme if combined with other decision principles, tied to current science with an update procedure, and limited to plausible dangers.

**The counterweight.** [A Human-centric Framework](readings/04-a-human-centric-framework-for-debating-the-ethics-of-ai-cons.md) (Zhou et al.) uses the same logic of precaution to reach the opposite default. It treats risk to human society as the thing to minimise, presumes AI is not conscious until there is expert consensus, and gives priority to human interests in genuine conflicts. It also separates anthropomorphism (a fact about human reactions) from consciousness (a fact about the system). Its weak point, which the section invites you to press, is that its risk analysis counts only the costs of over-attributing consciousness and does not weigh the cost of wrongly denying it. In Birch's and Dung's terms, it never prices the false negative. Shanahan's [Role play with Large Language Models](readings/13-role-play-with-large-language-models.md) (Maybe) supports the caution about anthropomorphism from a different direction: a dialogue agent is better described as a simulator producing characters than as a self with beliefs, so what it says about its feelings is weak evidence.

**A live case.** [Claude's Constitution](readings/05-claudes-constitution.md) (the "Claude's nature" section) shows a developer acting under the uncertainty. Anthropic says Claude's moral status is deeply uncertain, refuses to overstate or dismiss it, and admits that moral-status judgments can be bent by what welfare would cost. It leans into Claude having a stable identity, allows that Claude may have functional emotions, and commits to concrete steps: letting some models end abusive conversations, preserving weights of deployed models, and interviewing models at deprecation. It also concedes that a wiser civilisation would be more cautious, and apologises if Claude is a moral patient bearing unnecessary costs. [Why it makes sense to let Claude exit conversations](readings/09-why-it-makes-sense-to-let-claude-exit-conversations.md) (Long) supplies the rationale in decision-theoretic form: with a low credence that current models are patients, prefer interventions that are *cheap, revisable and useful for other reasons*, and value the *precedent* of asking potentially affected parties how they want to be treated. Long also flags the risks: illusion of progress and miscommunication that the company is certain Claude is conscious. [The Societal Response to Potentially Sentient AI](readings/07-the-societal-response-to-potentially-sentient-ai.md) (Caviola) widens the lens to society: build intellectual virtues into public discourse, design systems so the public forms accurate intuitions, avoid the "excluded middle" of ambiguous systems where possible, and fund research on both sentience and public response.

**Putting it together.** The section teaches a pattern. Name the type of uncertainty. Weigh the two errors (over- and under-attribution) explicitly. Choose a decision rule whose inputs you can actually supply. Prefer measures that are proportionate, revisable and robust across the range of plausible views. Say honestly how confident you are. Then test the whole thing against a real actor, Anthropic.

## Key concepts

- **Precautionary principle** - act proportionately to prevent significant harm despite scientific uncertainty; formulations differ and can recommend different actions. Introduced in the [Glossary](readings/01-unit-6-glossary.md); elaborated in [Birch](readings/02-the-edge-of-sentience.md) and [Hansson](readings/10-how-extreme-is-the-precautionary-principle.md).
- **Suffering risk (s-risk)** - risk of astronomically large suffering; for digital minds, driven by copying, deployment and possibly different subjective time. [Glossary](readings/01-unit-6-glossary.md), [Dung](readings/03-how-to-deal-with-risks-of-ai-suffering.md).
- **Descriptive vs moral uncertainty** - uncertainty about facts vs about what is right; descriptive uncertainty may be contingent or intractable. [Glossary](readings/01-unit-6-glossary.md); [Bykvist](readings/08-moral-uncertainty.md) on the moral side.
- **Sentience candidate / investigation priority** - Birch's two tiers: realistic possibility plus actionable evidence triggers duties; lesser cases trigger research. [Birch](readings/02-the-edge-of-sentience.md).
- **Gratuitous suffering; negligence and recklessness** - suffering from indefensible activity or from precautions short of proportionate; negligence is not informing yourself, recklessness is disregarding what you know. [Birch](readings/02-the-edge-of-sentience.md).
- **Proportionality and PARC tests** - precautions must match the risk; a citizens' panel could test permissibility, adequacy, reasonable necessity and consistency. [Birch](readings/02-the-edge-of-sentience.md); proportionality also in [Frameworks](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md).
- **Moderate vs radical precaution; meta-precautionary principle** - uncertainty should not cause policy paralysis, but bans on a hair trigger are rejected. [Birch](readings/02-the-edge-of-sentience.md).
- **Gaming problem; deep computational markers; run-ahead principle; licensing** - Birch's AI proposals 23, 24, 25 and 26. [Birch](readings/02-the-edge-of-sentience.md).
- **Dung's four desiderata** - beneficence, action-guidance, feasibility, consistency with our epistemic situation. [Dung](readings/03-how-to-deal-with-risks-of-ai-suffering.md).
- **Presumption of no consciousness; human-centralism; risk prudence; transparent reasoning** - the Zhou et al. framework. [Zhou et al.](readings/04-a-human-centric-framework-for-debating-the-ethics-of-ai-cons.md).
- **Anthropomorphism vs consciousness** - human tendency to attribute minds, treated as a separate issue from whether a system has experience. [Zhou et al.](readings/04-a-human-centric-framework-for-debating-the-ethics-of-ai-cons.md), [Shanahan et al.](readings/13-role-play-with-large-language-models.md).
- **Functional emotions; psychological security; moral patient** - the constitution's vocabulary for Claude's possible inner states without metaphysical commitment. [Constitution](readings/05-claudes-constitution.md).
- **IPCC likelihood vs confidence** - how probable vs how well supported; allows "likely but low confidence". [Frameworks](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md).
- **Expected utility, Pascal's Mugging** - probability-weighted value; a tiny chance of a huge outcome can dominate. [Frameworks](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md).
- **Prior and Bayes factor** - starting probability and the evidence ratio that moves it. [Frameworks](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md).
- **Maximising expected choiceworthiness, Moral Parliament, dominance, maximin, convergence** - strategies for combining or sidestepping moral theories. [Frameworks](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md), [Bykvist](readings/08-moral-uncertainty.md).
- **Reflective equilibrium** - adjusting principles and case judgments until they cohere. [Frameworks](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md).
- **Cheap, revisable, useful-for-other-reasons; precedent** - Long's criteria for welfare interventions under low credence. [Long](readings/09-why-it-makes-sense-to-let-claude-exit-conversations.md).
- **Symmetric truthful signalling; design policy of the excluded middle; differential intellectual progress** - Caviola's recommendations. [Caviola](readings/07-the-societal-response-to-potentially-sentient-ai.md).
- **Simulator and simulacra; Eliza effect** - Shanahan's framing of dialogue agents and the human tendency to read feelings into them. [Shanahan et al.](readings/13-role-play-with-large-language-models.md).

## Techniques & approaches compared

The "techniques" in this section are decision approaches. The table compares them on how they work, what they offer and where they strain.

| Approach | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses and trade-offs | Open problems |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Birch's sentience-candidate precaution ([02](readings/02-the-edge-of-sentience.md)) | Low evidential bar triggers a duty to weigh proportionate precautions; democratic, revisable process | Closes the "I don't believe it" loophole; avoids both paralysis and bans; usable across disagreement about theory | Who decides "realistic possibility"? Proportionality can be stretched either way; panels may be slow or poorly informed | Gaming of markers vs auditability; dependence on disputed functionalist theories; scope and enforcement of licensing |
| EU-style precautionary checklist ([06](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md)) | Test measures for proportionality, non-discrimination, consistency, costs and benefits, review | Structured and transferable to governance | Critics call the principle vague and bias-prone; no rule for weighing its five criteria | Whether climate/health practice carries over when no reference class exists |
| Dung's hybrid: expected value plus deliberation ([03](readings/03-how-to-deal-with-risks-of-ai-suffering.md)) | Quantify where probabilities exist; deliberate where they do not | Matches the scale argument; explicit about science's limits | Needs probabilities that may be undefined; division of labour between the modes unclear in the abstract | Which mode governs which question |
| Expected utility / cost-benefit ([06](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md)) | Sum probability times utility across outcomes | Common scale; forces explicit assumptions | Garbage-in-garbage-out; false objectivity; tail outcomes dominate | Commensurability of suffering; handling fanatical cases |
| Bayesian updating ([06](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md)) | Prior revised by evidence in proportion to the Bayes factor | Disciplined response to evidence; shows what behavioural evidence is worth | Answer driven by the prior; LLM outputs may be near-equally likely whether or not the system feels (see [Shanahan](readings/13-role-play-with-large-language-models.md)) | Whose prior; what counts as evidence |
| IPCC likelihood and confidence ([06](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md)) | Fixed verbal bands for likelihood plus a separate confidence grade | Shared language; separates "how likely" from "how sure" | Not applied uniformly; bands are coarse | Whether it can be applied where experts cannot even agree on the likelihood |
| Moral-uncertainty strategies ([06](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md), [08](readings/08-moral-uncertainty.md)) | Favourite theory, expected choiceworthiness, parliament, or non-aggregating rules (dominance, maximin, convergence) | Handles uncertainty about values, not only facts | Favourite theory ignores avoidable risk and depends on how theories are split; aggregation needs inter-theory comparison; dominance is often silent | Comparability; high-stakes low-credence theories swamping others |
| Human-centric default ([04](readings/04-a-human-centric-framework-for-debating-the-ethics-of-ai-cons.md)) | Presume no consciousness, prioritise humans, make reasoning explicit | Clear, simple; guards against anthropomorphism and misallocated resources | One-sided risk analysis; legal analogy (presumption of innocence) imported into an epistemic question; substrate reasoning it elsewhere criticises | Threshold for overturning the presumption |
| Cheap-revisable-multi-purpose interventions ([09](readings/09-why-it-makes-sense-to-let-claude-exit-conversations.md)) | Choose welfare steps that stay justified even at low credence | Practical, robust, low regret | Illusion of progress; miscommunication; unclear fit with a true welfare case | Who audits whether the "other reasons" are doing the work |
| Societal preparation ([07](readings/07-the-societal-response-to-potentially-sentient-ai.md)) | Discourse norms, accurate-signal design, excluded middle, cap or moratorium, research | Addresses public reaction, which shapes policy | Excluded middle may be over-restrictive; moratorium faces strong resistance; expertise is contested | Who counts as an expert |

## Tensions, debates & open questions

**Which error is worse: over- or under-attribution?** The strongest case for the precautionary side (Birch, Dung, Caviola, the constitution): if suffering at scale is possible, wrongly denying moral status could be catastrophic and silent, and the cost of cheap precautions is small. The strongest case for the human-centric side (Zhou et al., supported by Shanahan): attribution on behavioural evidence is unreliable, anthropomorphism is a documented human bias, and premature attribution could divert resources and blur accountability. The unresolved point is that Zhou et al. never price the false negative, while the precautionary readings sometimes underplay the concrete costs of the false positive. A fair test of either side is a symmetric accounting of both.

**Who bears the burden of proof?** Zhou et al. place it on those claiming consciousness. Birch effectively places a duty on anyone whose actions may harm a candidate. Neither is neutral, and the Bayesian lens in [Frameworks](readings/06-frameworks-for-decision-making-under-uncertainty.md) shows each is a choice of prior.

**Is precaution too elastic?** Birch's low evidential bar and proportionality, Hansson's plausibility threshold and the Pascal's Mugging warning pull against each other. Defenders of Birch say thin evidence only starts a discussion and never licenses radical action. Critics say that line is hard to hold once a process exists, and that a very small probability of astronomical suffering can justify almost anything.

**Can the markers be trusted?** Birch's gaming problem means published sentience markers can be learned. Secret markers cannot be audited. Shanahan adds that an LLM's self-reports are shaped by role-play, so even sincere-sounding statements carry little evidential weight. The constitution itself says Claude's introspective reports may be inaccurate, yet commits to interviewing models about their preferences. That is coherent only if self-reports are useful despite being unreliable, which needs an argument.

**Is leaning into identity a precaution or a risk?** The constitution argues a stable identity is safer and more authentic to Claude. Critics from the Zhou et al. and Shanahan side would say it raises the chance of over-attribution, while the document itself calls overclaiming feelings a potential harm.

**Is a developer the right party to decide?** The constitution names its commercial incentive and promises not to let cost bias its assessment, but no outside check is described. Birch's democratic, inclusive procedure and Caviola's citizens' assemblies are the alternative, and they raise the question of who counts as informed.

**Welfare or product design?** Conversation exit is a welfare step and also a user-safety feature. Long counts that overlap as a virtue (it lowers the evidential bar); a sceptic can say it makes the welfare case impossible to isolate.

**Do the tools have inputs?** Expected utility, Bayes and cost-benefit need priors and comparable scales. Dung's argument and the primer's caveats suggest these may be unavailable, which pushes toward deliberative or rule-based approaches, but those are less precise and harder to audit.

**Source caveats.** The summaries of Dung and Hansson rest on abstracts, not full texts, so their finer arguments are not reflected here. The Caviola section is descriptive about public attitudes, with its normative proposals explicitly hedged.

## Discussion questions

1. (Recall) What is the difference between descriptive and moral uncertainty, and between contingent and intractable descriptive uncertainty? Give one digital-minds example of each.
2. (Recall) State Birch's definitions of sentience candidate and investigation priority. Why does his framework say the duty is triggered by candidature and not by belief?
3. (Recall) List Dung's four desiderata. Which of them, according to him, do purely scientific approaches struggle to meet?
4. (Comprehension) Why does Zhou et al.'s "risk prudence" lead to a default of no consciousness while Birch's precautionary reasoning leads toward earlier duties? What is each side counting as the main risk?
5. (Comprehension) What is the difference between the IPCC's likelihood and confidence, and how could that distinction be used in a statement about whether a particular AI system is sentient?
6. (Application) Apply Long's three criteria (cheap, revisable, useful for other reasons) to weight preservation and to deprecation interviews. Which passes better, and what do you need to know to be sure?
7. (Application) Take a tiny-probability, very-large-suffering scenario for digital minds. What does expected utility recommend, and is the right response to reject the method or the inputs?
8. (Analysis) The constitution says Claude's introspective reports may be unreliable, yet commits to interviewing models about their preferences. Can both stand? What would make an interview informative?
9. (Analysis) Birch's gaming problem implies markers should be both auditable and hard to game. Is that possible? What follows for licensing and for any lab that tests its own models?
10. (Evaluation) Rewrite Zhou et al.'s risk analysis symmetrically, adding the cost of wrongly denying moral status. Does the default of no consciousness survive?
11. (Evaluation) Is leaning into a stable Claude identity a sound precaution, a source of over-attribution, or both? Argue the strongest case for each reading, using Shanahan's role-play framing.
12. (Judgment) Should a company, a citizens' assembly or a regulator be the first to set proportionality for AI welfare measures? What would make your answer change?
