# Moral Uncertainty

**One-line takeaway:** Being unsure what is morally right is a problem with its own structure, and the best-known shortcuts (follow your favourite theory, maximise expected moral value) each break down in ways that show what a workable approach has to handle.

## Purpose of the reading

Bykvist is writing for philosophers and advanced students who already know standard normative theories (utilitarianism, Kantianism, virtue ethics). His stated aim is to set out the main problems raised by moral uncertainty and to critically examine the solutions that have been proposed. He does not defend one complete theory. The paper is a survey in a *Philosophy Compass* format, and its own conclusion is that the field is still open ("the jury is still out").

The question it answers is: when an agent is not sure what she morally ought to do, what should she do? A secondary question is whether the notion of an ought under uncertainty makes sense at all, given that the plain answer, that you ought to do what you ought to do, seems obvious.

## Detailed summary

### 1. Introduction: where moral uncertainty comes from

Bykvist gives examples of questions where most people are not fully certain: how much non-human animal well-being matters relative to human well-being, how much to spend helping strangers, how much we should care about future generations, and how to weigh autonomy against benevolence. He separates two sources of doubt:

- **Empirical uncertainty** about facts (whether fish feel pain, whether aid reaches distant people, what the future holds).
- **Fundamental moral uncertainty**, where all the non-normative facts are known but it is still unclear how much a reason matters. Even with full knowledge of what autonomy and benevolence are, one can be unsure which is more important. Moral uncertainty can also be about which whole theory is correct, which is the common position of students after an introductory ethics course.

He notes that decision theory under empirical uncertainty has a long history, while moral uncertainty has received little attention. The earliest relevant discussion he cites is among Catholic theologians from the late 16th century, who considered what to do when unsure of divine moral law. Secular treatments are much more recent: Hudson (1989), Oddie (1995, applied to abortion and embryo experimentation), Lockhart's book (2000), Sepielli's work from 2009 onward, and MacAskill's 2014 thesis. He points to MacAskill, Ord and Bykvist (forthcoming book) as the up-to-date treatment.

### 2. The thought of moral uncertainty

**Two senses of "ought".** The obvious reply, that you ought to do what you ought to do whether or not you know it, assumes one sense of "ought" throughout. Bykvist proposes distinct levels. The first-level ought asks what is morally right, and standard theories answer it. The second-level ought asks what to do given credence in various first-level hypotheses. The second-level ought is a way of managing the risk of doing first-order wrong. He supports this with the observation that people are criticised for knowingly choosing a probable wrong when an option carrying no such risk was available.

**The regress objection.** If there is a second-level ought, one can be unsure about it too, leading to a third level and so on indefinitely. Bykvist answers that finite cognitive agents cannot climb infinitely, so there is a natural cutoff. He concedes the cutoff may not match any intuitive notion of ought: it is easy to reach the third or fourth level, but it is unclear there is an intuitive moral ought there.

**Rationality as the source of the ought.** An alternative is to treat the ought as one of rationality, given beliefs and preferences. He describes two spellings: coherence (acting in line with one's beliefs and preferences) and process (acting on what a rational process starting from those beliefs and preferences would output). He does not need to choose between them. He models a conscientious agent who cares about doing right and avoiding wrong, is indifferent between equally right options, and cares more about serious wrongs than minor ones.

**Dominance reasoning.** Standard decision theories agree that a dominating option should be chosen. Bykvist's example: one theory ("business as usual") says eating factory-farmed meat and eating vegetables are both permitted; another ("vegetarianism") says eating factory-farmed meat is impermissible. The agent has slightly more credence in business as usual. Eating vegetables is weakly preferred under every theory, so it dominates, and is the rational choice.

**The fetishism objection.** One might say the conscientious agent is a "moral fetishist" who cares about rightness as such rather than helping the needy or keeping promises. Bykvist calls this a false dichotomy. An agent can and should care about both the wrong-making features and the wrongness. Coming to believe an action is wrong should motivate an agent to care intrinsically about whatever makes it wrong. For example, an agent certain that lying is wrong because it disrespects others should come to care intrinsically about avoiding disrespect.

**Calibrated concern.** When the agent is not certain a feature is wrong-making, her intrinsic concern for that feature should be a function of two things: the probabilities of her hypotheses about how wrong-making it is, and how wrong-making it is according to each. In his vegetarian example, the agent becomes more averse to harming animals the more certain she is that this harm is wrong-making and the more seriously she takes it.

### 3. "My favourite theory" and the limits of dominance

The simplest response to moral uncertainty is to follow the theory you believe most. Bykvist lists three problems:

1. It gives no advice when credence is evenly spread across many theories, since there is no single favourite.
2. It ignores avoidable risks. In the meat-versus-vegetables case, it lets you eat meat, even though eating vegetables avoids any risk of wrongdoing at no cost.
3. It depends on how theories are individuated. Suppose you have 40% credence in Kantianism (which says action A is wrong and B right) and 60% in utilitarianism (A right, B wrong). Your favourite theory says choose A. But if you then split utilitarianism into hedonistic and non-hedonistic versions at 30% each, Kantianism, now the single largest credence at 40%, says B is right, so the verdict flips to B. Gustafsson and Torpman (2014) defend a more sophisticated favourite-theory view that avoids some of these problems.

Dominance reasoning also becomes contested when the top theory says some actions are supererogatory and very demanding. Peter Singer's utilitarianism requires helping others even at great cost to oneself. Suppose you have very little credence in Singer and a lot in a common-sense view that permits, but does not require, such sacrifice. Unqualified dominance reasoning would still require the large sacrifice, even though you barely believe the demanding theory. Bykvist says any full theory of moral uncertainty must solve this, and points to Barry and Tomlin (2016) for a statement of the problem and a new solution.

### 4. Maximising expected moral value

Dominance does not cover cases where theories rank options differently. Bykvist's example, borrowed from Zimmerman (2008): you have one pill. Giving it to John (a human) partially cures him, and giving it to Jane (an animal) fully cures her. You are equally split between impartialism, on which partially curing John is impermissible and fully curing Jane permissible, and speciesism, on which the reverse holds. The approach he describes is to compare how large the value difference between the two options is according to each theory, then weight those differences by credence. If the difference is larger on speciesism, you help John. If credence tilts toward impartialism, which sees a smaller gap, you need to weigh the ratios of differences. This works only if moral value is measured on an interval scale so that differences are comparable. Under that assumption, the standard expected-utility method applies to moral value.

### 5. Inter-theoretical comparisons of moral value

The expected-value method needs a comparison of value differences across theories, which is a big "if". Bykvist cites Gracely (1996) for the worry that such comparisons may be impossible. He notes, though, that people routinely make similar comparisons, such as judging that saving animals rather than humans matters more if impartialism is true.

**Endpoint normalisation.** Lockhart (2000) proposes that the best option for each theory counts as equally good, and the worst option for each counts as equally bad. Bykvist raises two objections. Some theories may have unbounded value, so there may be no best or worst option. And one theory's best option might have greater value than another's. He reports that other approaches avoid these problems, but whether any is defensible overall is unresolved (Hedden 2016, MacAskill 2014, Ross 2006, Sepielli 2009).

**Non-gradable theories.** Some theories may deny that there are graded value differences at all. Absolutist deontology, for example, says you either act in accordance with duty or you do not. In that case expected value does not apply. Bykvist's suggested response is pluralism: use expected value where comparisons are possible and other methods elsewhere.

**Voting analogy.** One alternative borrows from social choice theory (MacAskill). Treat each theory as a voter whose "vote" is its ranking of options, weighted by credence. The Borda rule gives the top-ranked option n points (n = number of options), the next n-1, and so on. His example has three rankings: Chris (A, B, C), Lisa (B, C, A), Eva (A, C, B). Scores are A = 7, B = 6, C = 5, so A wins. In the moral case, the points each theory gives an option are multiplied by the credence in that theory. This needs no inter-theoretical comparison of value at all.

### 6. High-stakes theories swamping low-stakes theories

Even where comparisons are possible, expected value can give a theory with high stakes and low credence too much influence. Bykvist's example: you have little confidence in a strict deontological view on which there is always very strong reason not to lie. You have much more confidence in a lenient theory on which there is some, but weaker, reason to lie, especially to save people from harm. If the strict theory's reasons are strong enough, expected-value maximisation lets it win anyway.

Bykvist says that a low-credence, high-stakes theory sometimes winning is not objectionable in general, because it is sensible to take small risks of very bad outcomes seriously. The lying case, however, looks like too much weight to the high-stakes view. Two responses are considered: modify expected value, for example by satisficing (choosing any option that is good enough, rather than requiring the maximum), or follow Barry and Tomlin (2016) in evaluating option sets rather than individual acts.

### 7. Metaethical implications

Moral uncertainty also has implications for metaethics. Non-cognitivism treats moral judgements as desires rather than beliefs. Bykvist argues that desires lack the structure to represent both degree of uncertainty and degree of importance. If uncertainty is identified with desire strength, importance has nothing left to explain. If importance is identified with desire strength, uncertainty has nothing left. The two come apart: one can be very unsure that autonomy has great intrinsic value but very sure that bodily pleasure has moderate value, and very unsure whether one has strong reason to save a stranger's two children at the cost of one's own child, while sure one has weak reason to satisfy a whim. Cognitivists have no such problem, because credence in a moral proposition varies independently of the importance attached to it. Non-cognitivism has no fully satisfactory reply yet.

MacAskill (2013) gives another metaethical argument: credence in nihilism (moral judgements are systematically false) makes value undefined under that hypothesis, which breaks expected-value reasoning.

### 8. Extensions and concluding remarks

Bykvist says the problems generalise to other kinds of normative uncertainty: about rationality, epistemic principles, prudential reasons, and decision-theoretic principles (Nozick 1993, Sepielli 2013, MacAskill 2016). Second-order credence (Christensen 2010, Lasonen-Aarnio 2014) and uncertainty about final or intrinsic value (Zimmerman 2008, 2014) are also open. He closes with climate change. Its moral questions involve uncertainty about probabilities of scenarios, how to know those probabilities, and how to evaluate the scenarios. He argues that an approach must take all these uncertainties into account at once.

## Techniques & methods explained

- **Second-level ought / managing first-order risk.** Treat the question "what should I do given uncertainty about what I ought to do?" as its own normative question. Works when the uncertainty is about first-order theories. Breaks down on the regress concern, which the paper answers only by appeal to finite cognition.
- **Dominance reasoning.** If option X is at least as good as Y under every live theory and strictly better under some, choose X. Easy when it applies, but fails when the dominating option is very demanding under a theory one barely believes (Singer case).
- **My favourite theory.** Pick the theory with the highest credence and follow it. Simple, but gives no verdict on ties, ignores avoidable risks, and depends on how theories are individuated.
- **Expected moral value.** Weight each option's value under each theory by credence and maximise. Requires interval-scale comparisons across theories.
- **Endpoint normalisation (Lockhart).** Set the best options of all theories equal and the worst options equal, to make values comparable. Problems: unbounded value and unequal best options.
- **Borda rule with credences.** Rank options under each theory, give points by rank position, multiply by credence, and choose the option with the highest total. Avoids inter-theoretical comparisons.
- **Satisficing and option-set evaluation.** Choose any option that is good enough, or evaluate sets of options rather than single acts, to stop high-stakes low-credence theories from dominating.
- **Calibrated intrinsic concern.** An agent's concern for a feature should scale with both the probability it is wrong-making and the degree to which it is.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Approach | Strength (as Bykvist presents it) | Weakness (as Bykvist presents it) |
|---|---|---|
| Second-level ought | Captures the intuition that risk-avoidance is itself morally assessable | Regress; unclear that higher levels have an intuitive moral ought |
| Dominance | Uncontroversial where it applies | Can force very demanding acts (Singer case) |
| Favourite theory | Simple, action-guiding | No verdict on ties, ignores avoidable risk, sensitive to individuation |
| Expected moral value | Handles graded trade-offs; standard tools | Needs interval-scale inter-theoretical comparisons; swamping by high-stakes theories |
| Endpoint normalisation | Makes comparison possible | Unbounded value; best options may differ in value across theories |
| Borda / voting | No inter-theoretical comparison needed | Does not use intensity of value differences; the author notes this only as one approach |
| Satisficing / option sets | Reduces swamping | Departs from expected-value theory; defensibility still contested |
| Metaethics (cognitivism) | Captures uncertainty and importance independently | Non-cognitivism has no fully satisfactory solution yet |

Bykvist's own verdict is restrained. He presents the paper as a critical examination, and the pluralist suggestion is the closest he comes to a position. The "jury is still out" language signals he does not regard any single approach as settled.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Moral uncertainty** - uncertainty about what one morally ought to do, which can concern how to weigh reasons or which whole theory is correct, even with all empirical facts known.
- **Fundamental (vs empirical) moral uncertainty** - doubt persisting after all non-normative facts are known.
- **First-order / second-order ought** - the first-order ought is what is morally right; the second-order ought is what to do given credence across first-order hypotheses.
- **Conscientious moral agent** - an agent who cares about doing right and avoiding wrong, and cares more about serious wrongs.
- **Dominance** - option X is weakly preferred to Y under every hypothesis and strictly preferred under some.
- **Moral fetishism** - caring about rightness or wrongness as such, rather than about what makes actions right or wrong.
- **Calibrated concern** - intrinsic concern for a feature that scales with the probability and degree of its wrong-making.
- **My favourite theory** - follow the theory you have most credence in.
- **Supererogation** - an act that is good to do but not required; here, a demanding act that a theory may require.
- **Expected moral value** - credence-weighted moral value of an option across theories, maximised as in expected utility.
- **Inter-theoretical comparison** - comparing how large value differences are across different moral theories.
- **Endpoint normalisation** - equating the best (and worst) options of different theories to compare them.
- **Borda rule** - a voting method scoring options by rank position; here applied with credences as vote weights.
- **Swamping** - a high-stakes, low-credence theory outweighing a lower-stakes, higher-credence theory under expected value.
- **Satisficing** - choosing any option that is good enough, rather than the best.
- **Non-cognitivism** - the view that moral judgements express desires or attitudes rather than beliefs.
- **Nihilism (moral)** - the view that moral judgements are systematically false, so moral value is undefined.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

This is a philosophy survey, so there are no experiments or statistics. The worked examples are the content:

- **Meat versus vegetables (dominance).** Slightly more credence in "business as usual" than in "vegetarianism" still makes eating vegetables the rational choice, because it is weakly better under both theories.
- **Favourite-theory flip.** A 40% Kantian / 60% utilitarian agent picks option A. Splitting the utilitarian credence into two 30% versions makes the verdict B. This is the paper's clearest illustration of individuation sensitivity.
- **Singer case.** Very little credence in demanding utilitarianism still yields a required great sacrifice under unqualified dominance.
- **John and Jane (Zimmerman 2008).** One pill, two patients. Equal credence between impartialism and speciesism leaves the choice open unless value differences can be compared.
- **Borda example.** Three voters-as-theories, three options. Scores A = 7, B = 6, C = 5, with A chosen. The paper's point is that the method needs no comparison of value magnitudes.
- **Lying case.** No numbers are given. The structure is: little credence in strict deontology, much in a lenient view, and expected value lets the strict view win if its stakes are high enough.
- **Non-cognitivist counterexamples.** Autonomy's intrinsic value (uncertain) versus bodily pleasure (certain, moderate). The stranger's children versus one's own child.
- **Nihilism (MacAskill 2013).** Under the nihilist hypothesis value is undefined, which disturbs expected-value reasoning.
- **Historical timeline.** Theologians from the late 16th century; Hudson 1989; Oddie 1995 (abortion, embryo experimentation); Lockhart 2000; Sepielli 2009-2016; MacAskill 2014.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my analysis, not the author's.*

- **Scope and dating.** The paper was received in June 2016 and published in *Philosophy Compass* in 2017 (article e12408). Its literature is mostly from 2000 to 2016. The metadata year is 2016. Since then the field has grown substantially: MacAskill, Ord and Bykvist's *Moral Uncertainty* (Oxford University Press, 2020) is the book-length treatment Bykvist anticipated. Work applying these tools to animal welfare and AI has also appeared since. This is from my general knowledge and was not checked against sources in this project.
- **No AI content.** Bykvist never discusses AI, sentience, or digital minds. The learner has to make the transfer, and the transfer is not automatic. The paper's examples assume that the relevant moral features (pain, wellbeing, autonomy) are known to be present in some beings. For AI, the first-order question of whether a system has welfare at all is the uncertainty, and that is a different kind of problem from the uncertainty in the John and Jane case.
- **Contested claims.** The inter-theoretical comparison problem is presented as open. Expected-value reasoning depends on it, so any AI application that relies on expected moral value inherits the same weakness.
- **Possible application.** The John and Jane structure maps onto a human-versus-AI wellbeing trade-off. Under uncertainty about AI moral status, a credence-weighted comparison would need some way to compare how much a unit of AI welfare matters relative to a unit of human welfare, which is the kind of comparison Bykvist says is hard.
- **Dominance and cheap precautions.** A plausible application is that some precautions for possible AI welfare cost little, like the vegetables example. Bykvist's framework would say to take them whenever they dominate. The demanding-theory case (Singer) warns that a precaution that is costly and that only a low-credence theory requires should not be taken as dominating.
- **Other course perspectives would push back.** Zhou et al. (Core reading 4 in this section) argue for a default of no consciousness, which the expected-value approach would treat as one credence among others and would not settle by fiat. Dung's suffering-risk framing and Hansson's extreme-precaution argument would push toward weighting high-stakes possibilities more. Bykvist's swamping discussion is the bridge: whether that weighting is correct is exactly what he calls contested.
- **Bykvist's own gaps.** The paper treats credences as given and does not say how an agent should form them. In the digital-minds setting, the credence question is often the hard one, and the paper says nothing about it.
