# Unit 6 Glossary

## Detailed summary

The source is short (about 360 words) and contains four entries. Each entry is paraphrased below.

**Precautionary principle.** When there is a threat of significant harm, scientific uncertainty should not be a reason to delay or avoid proportionate measures that protect against that harm. The entry stresses that there are several formulations of this principle, and they can recommend different actions at different levels of uncertainty. The one example given is the EU's version, which pairs precautionary action with additional safeguards: non-discrimination, cost-benefit analysis, proportionality, consistency, and an effort to build more scientific understanding while acting.

**Suffering-risks (s-risks).** Risks of astronomical future suffering. The entry points to the Center for Reducing Suffering for more discussion. Applied to digital minds, it gives the reasoning behind the concern: digital systems can be copied and deployed easily, and they might have different time perceptions or subjective experiences. If such systems can suffer, the total amount of suffering could be very large. The entry frames this as a concern ("leads to concerns that the scale ... could be extreme"), not as a finding that suffering occurs.

**Uncertainty, descriptive.** Uncertainty about matters of fact, where we do not know what is true of the world. It comes in two forms. Contingent uncertainty can in principle be resolved with better evidence or theory. Intractable uncertainty resists resolution because of basic limits on what can be known. The entry applies both to AI consciousness: we lack adequate theories and tests, and subjective experience may not be straightforwardly accessible to third-person scientific methods.

**Uncertainty, moral.** Uncertainty about what is ethically right, or about which values, principles or frameworks should guide action. The entry notes that this can arise even when all the relevant non-moral facts are known.

## How the terms fit together

*This section is my own synthesis of the four entries, not wording from the source.*

- The two uncertainty types are about different questions. Descriptive uncertainty is about what is the case (for example, whether a system is conscious). Moral uncertainty is about what we ought to do given that the facts may not be settled, or given that we are unsure which ethical theory is correct.
- The precautionary principle is a response to uncertainty of the first kind when the stakes are high. The entry's point that formulations differ means the principle is a family of positions, not one rule.
- Suffering risks are the high-stakes case that makes precaution seem urgent. Ease of copying and possible differences in subjective time are the features that raise the potential scale.
- Contingent versus intractable uncertainty matters for policy. If the uncertainty is contingent, the case for waiting for better science is stronger. If it is intractable, waiting may not resolve anything, which pushes toward decision frameworks that act under uncertainty.

Cons and points of ambiguity:

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Precautionary principle** - acting to prevent significant harm despite scientific uncertainty, with proportionate measures; formulations differ.
- **Suffering risk (s-risk)** - risk of astronomically large future suffering; here, linked to copying and deployment of digital systems.
- **Descriptive uncertainty** - uncertainty about matters of fact.
- **Contingent uncertainty** - uncertainty that better evidence or theory could in principle resolve.
- **Intractable uncertainty** - uncertainty that cannot be resolved because of fundamental limits on knowledge.
- **Moral uncertainty** - uncertainty about which ethical values, principles or frameworks should guide action, even with all non-moral facts known.
- **EU precautionary approach (as cited)** - precaution plus non-discrimination, cost-benefit analysis, proportionality, consistency and building scientific understanding.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- The descriptive/moral split is useful but not clean. Many disagreements about AI consciousness are about which theory of consciousness to use, which is a moral and epistemic question at once. The glossary's statement that moral uncertainty can arise with all facts known is correct in principle, but in practice the two often interact.
- The precautionary principle is defined broadly enough to cover very different policies. The entry's own remark that formulations "may make different recommendations" is an admission that the principle does not settle what to do. Critics of the principle argue that it can be used to block almost any new technology, and that it needs a principled threshold. The Hansson reading on the section page takes up the threshold question, but I have not checked its specifics against that text. The glossary does not take a side.
- "Suffering risk" rests on a chain: systems could suffer, they could be copied at scale, and subjective time could differ. Each link is uncertain, so the entry describes the concern without claiming the risk is likely. Readers should keep the conditional form.
- Nothing in the glossary has aged, because it contains no empirical claims. The EU approach it cites is a long-standing framework, and I am describing it from general knowledge rather than from this source.
- The course's other readings (Birch, Dung, Caviola) may push back on any reading that treats these definitions as settled. I have not checked this against their texts here.
