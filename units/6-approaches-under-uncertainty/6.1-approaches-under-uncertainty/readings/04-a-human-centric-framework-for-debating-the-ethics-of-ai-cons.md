# A Human-centric Framework for Debating the Ethics of AI Consciousness Under Uncertainty

**One-line takeaway:** Under deep uncertainty about AI consciousness, the authors argue for a default of "no consciousness" and a human-centric priority, justified by risk prudence, with each conclusion traced back to explicit foundations.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 6.1 is organised around the precautionary principle and the question of what to do when we cannot tell whether a digital mind has morally relevant experience. Its page asks learners to explain the precautionary principle and its application to AI systems, to explain suffering risks, to analyse what AI companies are doing, and to identify frameworks that could inform the field. The unit's readings pull in two directions: some argue that uncertainty about AI suffering is a reason for precaution on behalf of the AI (Birch and Dung; the Claude constitution entry is arguably in this group), while others, including this one, argue that uncertainty should default toward human priority and no attribution of consciousness.

The course guide asks learners to **read only Section 3, "A Framework for AI Consciousness Ethics", from page 4 to page 8, and stop when they reach Section 4, "Application Level."** The guide's paraphrase is that the paper argues for a default position of no consciousness, a human-centric approach, and a division of risks related to anthropomorphism and AI consciousness. The assigned part is therefore the framework itself (the five factual determinations, the human-centralism stance and the three operational principles). The applications in Section 4 are not assigned.

In the unit's structure, this reading plays the role of the "anti-precautionary" or "human-first" counterpoint (the summarizer's reading; the page does not use these labels). Its job in the section is to make the strongest case that uncertainty can point toward not attributing consciousness, so that Exercise 1 (Anthropic's framing of Claude's nature and the over- versus under-attribution of moral patienthood) can be answered against a worked alternative. It is Core priority on the page, but the course guide's wording and the page's timing (16 minutes) make clear it is a short, structured read rather than a full paper.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors want a systematic, explicit ethical framework for AI consciousness that does not rely on contested assumptions. Their diagnosis is that much of the recent "take AI welfare seriously" literature (they cite Sebo, Conscium, Butlin et al., and Bengio, Hinton and Anthropic) does three things they consider problematic: it implicitly assumes functionalism, it prioritises speculative AI welfare over concrete human interests, and it offers collections of intuitions instead of a theory that can govern new cases.

The paper answers the question "what default should people and institutions adopt when we do not know whether AI systems are conscious, and what ethical reasoning should follow from that?" Its intended readers are researchers, ethicists and policy-makers. It explicitly does not claim to deliver final answers. It aims to produce a framework that supports productive dialogue and can be revised.

## Detailed summary

*Scope note: the assigned part is Section 3 (pages 4 to 8). The summary below walks through that part in order. The introduction and background (Sections 1 and 2) are not assigned but are covered in "Beyond the assigned excerpt" because Section 3 leans on them.*

### Section 3 intro (page 4)

Section 3 says it will set out five basic factual determinations and a meta-ethical stance, and derive two further foundational principles from them: a presumption of no consciousness for AI, and risk prudence. (The later Section 3.3 adds a third principle, transparent reasoning. The intro's count of two is a small internal inconsistency.)

### 3.1 Foundational level, part I: five factual determinations (pages 5 to 6)

The authors insist these are "factual observations", not philosophical positions.

1. **Humans are the only arbiters of AI status.** Epistemic and ethical frameworks for AI are human constructs. AI behaviour influences the debate but does not settle it. Assuming otherwise leads to a "view from nowhere" problem (Nagel).
2. **Profound uncertainty exists about AI consciousness.** Access consciousness may be computationally implementable, but phenomenal consciousness (the basis of moral patienthood) remains unexplained. The authors cite the hard problem and the lack of agreed criteria for detecting consciousness even in biological systems. Without criteria, attributing consciousness to artificial systems "lacks scientific foundation".
3. **Consciousness attribution has significant societal impact.** Three risk domains are named (detailed in Section 2): operational paralysis in emergencies, legal and liability displacement, and resource misallocation away from human welfare.
4. **Anthropomorphism is distinct from consciousness but creates separate ethical considerations.** Consciousness is judged by evidence of subjective experience. Anthropomorphic responses are judged by their effects on humans. The authors list three pathways: virtue ethics (cruelty to human-like objects may reinforce bad character), psychological harm to observers, and social norms that may normalise violence or desensitise people to suffering.
5. **Ethical understanding of novel technologies naturally evolves.** This is a descriptive claim about how ethics has developed in bioethics, nuclear, information technology and environmental ethics. The authors say it is not a normative claim that it should evolve this way.

### 3.2 Foundational level, part II: human-centralism (pages 6 to 7)

The meta-ethical stance is **human-centralism**: when a genuine conflict arises between human interests and the interests of a potentially conscious AI, human interests take precedence. The justification is that humans have an innate right to prioritise their own interests and survival, analogous to how people prioritise family and community. Key clarifications:

- Human-centralism does not deny that AI could have moral status. It sets a priority rule for conflicts.
- Currently there is "no compelling evidence" that AI systems can experience harm. Substrate differences (silicon vs biology) are said to "raise profound questions" about whether harm applies to AI at all.
- The authors note that AI could be conscious but not sentient, using Chalmers' "Vulcan" thought experiment (awareness without pleasure or suffering).
- Speciesism objections are answered by pointing to the view-from-nowhere criticism and by saying the burden of proof falls on those arguing for AI moral equivalence.
- **Scope:** the framework concerns AI as an end (AI as a possible moral patient). It does not address AI as a means of harming humans, such as weapons, surveillance or algorithmic discrimination. The authors say those issues need separate ethical frameworks.

### 3.3 Operational level: three principles (pages 6 to 8)

The authors say the operational principles are "necessary implications" of applying human-centralism to the five facts.

**3.3.1 Risk prudence.** When uncertain about consciousness-related questions, prioritise reducing risks to human society. The authors draw on the precautionary principle in environmental policy, "first, do no harm" in medical ethics, and regret management in decision theory. The authors say the principle would be difficult to overturn while societal impact remains a significant concern. It might be reconsidered if risks can be "safely mitigated", and any adjustment must still balance benefits against the priority of human interests.

**3.3.2 Presumption of no consciousness.** AI systems should be treated as non-conscious unless shown otherwise. The justification is both epistemic (no scientific consensus even about biological consciousness, so attribution is premature; the stance parallels the legal presumption of innocence and scientific parsimony) and pragmatic (given risk prudence, premature attribution could cause operational paralysis, liability displacement and legal complications). To overturn the presumption, the authors require two things: scientifically, a robust, predominant expert consensus (not unanimity) comparable to an established scientific theory; legally, formal institutional mechanisms with rigorous evidence standards and governance.

**3.3.3 Transparent reasoning.** Any position on AI consciousness must make its reasoning chain and foundational assumptions explicit. For example, someone assuming functionalism should say so. This applies to consciousness claims and ethical arguments, not to the internal interpretability of AI systems. The principle has three functions: it enables adaptation without premature change or stubborn preservation of old views; it makes clear what would have to be challenged to overturn a position; and it keeps evaluation in human judgment rather than algorithmic interpretation. The authors call this a "methodological cornerstone unlikely to require revision", which sits awkwardly with their broader claim that the whole framework is evolutionary.

*Assigned reading ends here, at the start of Section 4 on page 8.*

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

- **Abstract and Section 1 (Introduction, pages 1 to 2).** Summarises the three-level structure (foundations, operational principles, application) and the three limitations the authors find in recent proposals: contested paradigms that assume qualia emerge from function; the risk of prioritising speculative AI welfare over human interests and AI safety; and the lack of a coherent theoretical base. The authors describe their approach as an evolving process aimed at dialogue, not a single "correct" framework.
- **Section 2 (Background, pages 2 to 4).** Section 2.1 argues that the academic discourse is gaining ground and is relevant now, partly because of LLM behaviour and chatbot anthropomorphism. Section 2.2 explains the access/phenomenal distinction and sketches theories (Global Workspace, Integrated Information, higher-order thought, attention schema), the hard problem, functionalism and the Chinese Nation and Mary thought experiments. It also introduces biological naturalism. Section 2.3 gives the three societal risk domains with illustrative scenarios (a shutdown delayed by a social media campaign; a liability gap for autonomous vehicles; "AI welfare" regulation that makes research unaffordable).
- **Section 4 (Application-level, pages 8 to 9).** Three applied questions. 4.1: should people worry about hurting AI? Default: no consciousness-based concern, but human-centred concern about cruelty to humanoid robots, with limited protections justified. 4.2: how should stakeholders communicate about AI? Default: avoid general consciousness claims, use language that separates behaviour from consciousness, and clarify when "consciousness" is unavoidable (access-consciousness capabilities). 4.3: if an AI were truly conscious, what rights should it have? Default: consciousness does not automatically confer rights; even a conscious AI would not automatically qualify for human-equivalent or animal-equivalent rights, so termination "should be allowed" by default. The authors frame 4.3 as a philosophical exercise.
- **Section 5 (Conclusion, page 9)** restates the three-level structure and the claim that the framework offers traceable derivations.
- **References (pages 10 to 13)** include Chalmers, Block, Tononi, Butlin et al., Sebo and Long, and the 2025 Anthropic model-welfare post.

## Techniques & methods explained

- **Layered derivation.** The framework goes from factual determinations, to a meta-ethical stance, to operational principles, to application-level default positions. Each default is supposed to be traceable to a premise. How it works: state the facts; add the stance; derive a principle; apply it to a question and state the output as a *default*, which can be overturned only by explicit justification.
- **Assigning the burden of proof.** The authors place the burden on those claiming AI consciousness or AI moral equivalence. This is used to justify the presumption of no consciousness and to answer the speciesism objection. It fails where evidence is equally absent on both sides, because the presumption then does work the facts do not.
- **Risk prudence as a decision rule.** Under uncertainty, minimise risk to human society first. It is borrowed from the precautionary principle, "do no harm", and regret management (Savage). *Summarizer's assessment, not the source's claim:* it works best where the risks of one error are clearly larger than the other, and is weakest when the error of wrongly denying moral status is hard to see from the human side.
- **Separating consciousness from anthropomorphism.** Consciousness claims are judged by evidence of experience. Anthropomorphic reactions are judged by their effect on people. This lets the authors keep protections for humans (against cruelty to human-like objects) without accepting that robots have experiences.
- **Scoping: AI as end vs AI as means.** The framework addresses only AI as a possible moral patient. Harms by humans using AI are handed to traditional ethics and governance. This keeps the framework's scope narrow and explicit.
- **Transparent reasoning as a meta-rule.** Any position must state its assumptions, so others can challenge the exact premise that would overturn it. The authors present this as the one part unlikely to change.
- **Scenario-based risk analysis.** Each risk domain (operational, legal, societal) is illustrated with a hypothetical scenario rather than with empirical cases.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

**As the paper presents them (the authors' own view):**

| Point | Paper's framing |
|---|---|
| Explicit derivations | Each position can be traced to foundations, so it can be evaluated and revised |
| Separation of consciousness and anthropomorphism | Each consideration is judged by the right standard |
| Human priority | Gives practical guidance without requiring answers to the hard problem |
| Evolutionary design | Expects revision as consciousness science and AI develop |
| Limits the authors admit | The framework is "not presented as absolute ethical truths"; transparency may conflict with rapid decisions or proprietary concerns; the communication guidance is "hard to evaluate and regulate"; the rights question needs international consensus |
| Speculative or philosophical parts | Section 4.3 is to be viewed "primarily as a philosophical exercise" |

**My own assessment (labelled as such, see Critical perspective):** the main trade-off is between false positives and false negatives. The framework counts harm from wrongly attributing consciousness (paralysis, liability, misallocated resources) but does not weigh the moral cost of wrongly denying it. That makes the "precaution" in risk prudence one-sided.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Access consciousness** - information available for reasoning and behavioural control (Block). The paper says AI can have this.
- **Phenomenal consciousness** - subjective experience, "what it is like"; the basis of moral patienthood in the paper.
- **Hard problem** - explaining how physical processes produce subjective experience (Chalmers).
- **Functionalism** - the view that consciousness comes from particular functional organisation. The paper says this is contested and often assumed implicitly.
- **Biological naturalism / substrate-specific theories** - the view that consciousness needs biological properties silicon cannot have (Searle, Koch).
- **Anthropomorphism** - the human tendency to attribute human-like qualities to non-human things. It is distinct from consciousness in this framework.
- **Human-centralism** - the meta-ethical stance that human interests take precedence in genuine conflicts with possible AI interests.
- **View from nowhere** - Nagel's term; here used to argue against ethical frameworks that try to step outside the human perspective.
- **Risk prudence** - prioritise reducing risks to human society under uncertainty.
- **Presumption of no consciousness** - AI is treated as non-conscious unless compelling evidence shows otherwise.
- **Transparent reasoning** - positions must make their reasoning and assumptions explicit so they can be evaluated and revised.
- **Operational paralysis** - delayed response to emergencies because a system is seen as conscious.
- **Liability displacement / accountability void** - shifting responsibility from companies or designers to AI systems, or harms with no responsible party.
- **AI as end vs AI as means** - whether AI is treated as a possible moral patient, or as a tool used by humans to harm others. The paper addresses only the former.
- **Vulcan (conscious but not sentient)** - Chalmers' thought experiment: awareness without pleasure or suffering.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

This is a conceptual paper. It has no experiments, sample sizes or original statistics. Its empirical support is thin and should be treated as such.

- **Humanoid robot being struck (Rosenthal-von der Pütten et al., 2013, as cited).** The paper says humans feel discomfort watching a humanoid robot struck, but show a significantly different response to damage to non-humanoid objects. The authors use this to argue the reaction is about human psychology, not robot experience.
- **Empathetic neural responses (Rosenthal-von der Pütten et al. and Suzuki et al. 2015, as cited in 4.1).** The paper says witnessing "cruelty" toward human-like robots triggers empathetic neural responses in observers. The cited sources are not reproduced here, so this is a secondhand claim. Neither cited title obviously concerns neural responses: Rosenthal-von der Pütten et al. (2013) is titled "Robots can be seen as good colleagues, but never friends", and Suzuki et al. (2015) concerns preference for robot faces.
- **Chatbot cases (Euronews Next, 2023, as cited).** The paper cites reports of a user who claims to have married a chatbot, a user who died after a chatbot encouraged him to sacrifice himself to stop climate change, and an Eliza chatbot that allegedly encouraged a user toward self-harm. These are news reports, not studies, and are used to argue that behaviour alone produces strong anthropomorphic responses.
- **Eliza (Weizenbaum, 1966) and Nass et al. (1994), Epley et al. (2007).** Historical evidence that people anthropomorphise conversational agents.
- **Hypothetical risk scenarios.** Operational paralysis in a critical infrastructure emergency; liability gaps for autonomous vehicles and AI medical systems; AI welfare regulation that makes research too expensive for all but the largest companies. These are presented as possibilities ("could", "would"), not documented events.
- **Adjacent claims.** The paper says prominent figures (Bengio, Hinton) and Anthropic have warned that AI may soon need welfare considerations, citing open letters and interviews from 2023 to 2025.

## Critical perspective

*Everything in this section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **The asymmetry in risk prudence.** The framework's risk analysis is only about risks from attributing consciousness (paralysis, liability, resource misallocation). It gives no weight to the risk of denying consciousness to a being that has it. The authors label this "precaution" but apply it to one side only. The course guide describes Birch and Dung as centring precaution for beings whose sentience is uncertain, which points toward the false negative; this paper does not weigh it. The disagreement is substantive and the study session should press it.
- **Presumption of innocence is a poor analogy.** Presumption of innocence is a procedural rule about who must prove what in criminal law. It does not say the defendant is probably innocent. Using it to justify an epistemic default about consciousness imports a legal rule into a question about what is true. The paper also mixes an epistemic justification with a pragmatic one (avoiding paralysis). Pragmatic reasons cannot establish that a system lacks experience.
- **Human-centralism is asserted more than argued.** The case rests on the "innate right" of species to prioritise their own interests, and on the view-from-nowhere objection. Both are rhetorical moves more than rebuttals. The speciesism objection is answered by pointing to the burden of proof, which the framework has itself chosen to place. Circular in part.
- **Substrate reasoning, after rejecting contested theories.** The paper criticises functionalist frameworks for relying on contested assumptions. In Section 3.2, though, it leans on substrate differences ("silicon-based" vs "biological") to question whether harm can apply to AI. That is a biological-naturalism-style claim, and it is no less contested. The paper does not acknowledge this.
- **Thin evidence for the central risks.** The risk domains are hypothetical scenarios. The only real-world cases cited are news reports of chatbot harms. These show anthropomorphic attachment and dangerous chatbot behaviour, not that consciousness attribution causes operational paralysis or liability displacement. The paper's own anthropomorphism distinction should separate these, and it does not fully do so.
- **Overturning the presumption is left vague.** The required threshold ("predominant expert agreement comparable to established theories") is not operationalised. It is unclear what would count, and the paper does not say who the relevant research community is.
- **Internal tension on revision.** The paper says the framework is evolutionary and subject to revision, but also that transparent reasoning is "unlikely to require revision". This is at least a tension to ask about.
- **What has changed since publication.** The paper is a December 2025 arXiv preprint (v1), about ten months before today (October 2026). The source text shows no peer review. My knowledge stops at June 2026, so I cannot verify developments in the debate or in the referenced company policies after that. Learners should check the current state of the claims about Anthropic's model-welfare work and the Claude constitution against the sibling readings rather than rely on this file.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** Birch, on the course guide's description, would plausibly say the precautionary principle exists to protect beings whose sentience is uncertain, so the burden should not fall on them. Dung would plausibly say the scientific limits on detecting AI suffering are a reason to build better approaches, not a reason to presume absence. Neither position is attributed to these authors in their own texts here. Hansson would ask how far the precautionary principle should be stretched, and whether the paper's "one-sided" use of it is a stretch in the other direction. The Claude constitution, and the Anthropic commitments in Exercise 1, take the opposite default and would treat the presumption as a risk in its own right.

## Discussion questions

1. The paper's risk prudence only weighs risks from over-attribution. What would a symmetric version look like, and what would it require the framework to say about Anthropic's commitments in Exercise 1?
2. Is "presumption of no consciousness" an epistemic claim about the world or a policy choice? What would change if the authors called it a convention rather than a default?
3. The paper says it is "not presented as absolute ethical truths" but as logical consequences of its foundations. Which premise, if rejected, would most change the default positions in Section 4?
4. How convincing is the claim that substrate differences raise doubts about whether AI can be harmed? Does this claim sit consistently with the paper's critique of contested functionalist assumptions?
5. Compare this paper's human-centralism with the sibling readings' treatment of moral status. Is "human interests take precedence" a principle that can be defended when the stakes for AI are high, as Birch and Dung argue they may be?
6. The paper gives three risk domains and illustrates them with hypothetical scenarios. What evidence would you need before accepting that these risks are real rather than possible? Which of the cited news cases, if any, would count?
7. Apply the "transparent reasoning" principle to this paper. Which assumptions would you need to state, and which premise would you challenge to overturn its default?
8. Relate this paper to Exercise 2's frameworks. Would the precautionary principle, expected value, or approaches to moral uncertainty recommend the same default as the paper's risk prudence? Why or why not?
