# The Societal Response to Potentially Sentient AI

**One-line takeaway:** Caviola argues that society is poorly prepared for possibly sentient AI, and that the response should combine better public discourse, precautionary design and lab policies, and a funded research program on how people will actually react.

## Where this sits in the course

**Top-down context.** Section 6.1 asks three things: what the precautionary principle is and how it might apply to AI, what suffering risks come with creating or merely perceiving digital minds, and which existing frameworks could inform decisions. The page also asks learners to analyse what AI companies and researchers are already doing. Most of the course's readings in this section are about how to reason under uncertainty about sentience (Birch, Dung, Zhou et al., the frameworks reading). This paper is the one that turns to action: what should be done, by whom, given that the answer is still unknown.

**What the course guide says.** The guide gives a single instruction: read Section 7, "Recommended preparatory measures", and nothing else. It describes the section as recommending actions to prepare for potentially sentient AI and its interaction with society. The reading is marked Further, not Core, so it is optional depth rather than a required building block.

**Assigned part.** Only Section 7 (printed pp. 33-36, covering 7.1, 7.2 and 7.3) is assigned. Sections 1-6 (the case for social-science research, the risks of misattributing sentience, the drivers of public belief) are the argument that motivates Section 7. They are summarised below only so the recommendations make sense.

**Role in the argument.** Section 7 is where the paper stops describing and starts recommending. The author says early on that the paper's focus is descriptive, meaning how people are likely to respond, rather than normative, meaning how they should respond. Section 7 is the one place where the normative question is taken up directly, so it is the part a learner should test against the unit's other readings. Its recommendations are hedged and it explicitly calls several of them controversial or costly.

**Relation to the unit question.** The unit asks how to act under uncertainty. Section 7 offers a practical answer for AI labs and governments (treat possibly sentient systems as "sentience candidates", avoid designs that signal certainty either way, limit how many are built) and a meta-answer (invest in social science, discourse norms and governance research first).

## Purpose of the reading

The paper is written for researchers in social science, ethics and AI policy, and for decision-makers who will have to respond to public reactions to AI. Its core question is descriptive: what will people, experts and decision-makers believe about AI sentience, and how will they respond? Caviola's claim is that the public response will matter enormously for AI welfare, for AI safety, and for the political shape of the coming decades. The response is not predetermined, which is why the paper ends with recommendations rather than predictions.

Section 7 answers a further question: given that disagreement and confusion are likely, what should we do now? Its answer has three layers:

1. The underlying problem is that social and institutional wisdom is lagging technology, so progress on ethics and understanding has to outpace technical progress (what the author calls "differential intellectual progress").
2. Concrete steps: improve public discourse (7.1), change how potentially sentient systems are built and deployed (7.2), and expand research and governance capacity (7.3).
3. Explicit acknowledgment that the proposals involve trade-offs and that some would meet heavy resistance.

## Detailed summary

### Opening: why a preparatory agenda is needed (p. 33)

The section opens with a claim about the pace of change. Society is struggling to keep up with technology, and the author quotes the biologist E.O. Wilson on humanity's mismatch between emotions, institutions and technology. The argument is that transformative technology is being developed without a full understanding of its implications. The response proposed is "differential intellectual progress": deliberately accelerating collective wisdom and ethical capacity so that it stays ahead of technical development. In this framing the author names three things to deepen: understanding of AI sentience, the way people discuss and debate it, and the frameworks used to navigate it. Regulation of potentially sentient AI development is listed as part of this agenda, but not elaborated.

### 7.1 Fostering a nuanced public discourse (pp. 33-35)

**Problem.** Uncertainty about AI sentience could polarise public debate, with factions adopting rigid, overconfident positions, as has happened with other contested issues. The section does not say how polarisation would happen in detail; it treats it as the default risk.

**Proposal.** Cultivate a culture of careful, open-minded, constructive dialogue. The author suggests starting in academia and then spreading to public discourse, political debate and media. Even small actions, such as a measured tone in academic or public statements, are described as contributing.

**Four intellectual virtues** the author recommends:

- Intellectual humility: accept the limits of current knowledge and revise beliefs when credible evidence appears.
- Open-mindedness: give serious consideration to all plausible theories of sentience and of ethics, including those that challenge conventional wisdom.
- Probabilistic thinking: state confidence levels and avoid binary framings, favouring strategies that are robust across possible futures.
- Deference to expertise: expert analysis should carry significant weight, and lay opinion should not overshadow it.

**Deliberative democracy.** The author expects disagreement that is deeper and more persistent than climate change, partly because sentience is hard to test objectively and the stakes are high. In that case he suggests a deliberative model in which a representative group of citizens (a citizens' assembly) discusses the issue with expert input and works toward a balanced compromise. The model is credited to Birch (2024).

### 7.2 Adjusting the development of potentially sentient AI (pp. 34-35)

The section says policy recommendations for AI labs are hard, because each option has trade-offs. It then lists considerations, from least to most demanding.

**Step 1: acknowledge and assess.** AI labs must acknowledge the ethical implications of potentially sentient systems. At minimum, they should employ experts to rigorously assess whether their systems could be sentient, and those assessments should include diverse perspectives.

**Step 2: design for accurate intuitions.** Systems should be designed so the public forms accurate intuitions about their sentience. The rule given is symmetrical:
- If experts agree a system is not sentient, it should not present itself as sentient.
- If experts agree a system is likely sentient, it should not deny this.

The stated aim is to avoid the misattribution risk described in Section 3, which arises when a system's outward behaviour conflicts with its underlying mechanisms.

**Step 3: handle systems without consensus.** Where experts disagree, systems should avoid designs that make them look clearly sentient or clearly non-sentient. They should communicate transparently the uncertainty and the expert disagreement.

**Step 4: the "design policy of the excluded middle".** This is Schwitzgebel's idea, cited here. Only systems with clear expert consensus on sentience would be mass-produced; morally ambiguous systems would not. The author notes this could substantially reduce misattribution risk but may be overly restrictive and slow progress.

**Step 5: precautionary framework.** Companies and governments should adopt a precautionary approach, following Birch (2024) and Sebo (2025). Systems with reasonable evidence of possible sentience should be treated as "sentience candidates". For each, identify specific welfare risks and take proportional precautions to avoid gratuitous suffering.

**Step 6: slow down or limit (controversial).** The most demanding option is a temporary moratorium on developing systems with significant sentience potential. The author expects strong economic, technological and geopolitical resistance, but suggests public opinion might eventually support it. He cites the moratoriums on human cloning and germline modification as proof that such restrictions are feasible. A softer compromise is to cap the number of potentially sentient systems produced, which limits moral risk without stopping innovation.

### 7.3 Expanding our understanding (p. 36)

The final subsection lists three research needs:

1. Deeper research into AI sentience and consciousness, philosophical and technical.
2. Research on how potentially sentient AI interacts with society: public responses, economic impact, consumer behaviour, political dynamics and geopolitics. This should also consider how sentience links to broader AI risk and safety.
3. Research on policy and governance: regulations that prevent confusion about sentience, rules that stop AI systems making misleading claims about their own sentience, and advocacy or intervention campaigns that align public perception with expert consensus.

The section then ends, and the paper moves to acknowledgments and references.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

The rest of the paper, which is not assigned, does three things that Section 7 relies on.

- **Sections 1-2** argue that public attitudes matter because they shape norms, policy, regulation, rights decisions and AI development. They also set out the call for social-science research.
- **Section 3** sets up the misattribution framework (Table 1). Overattribution risks include wasted resources, safety problems such as opposition to alignment measures, and costs to innovation. Underattribution risks include digital suffering at possibly enormous scale and violations of autonomy.
- **Sections 4-6** survey the evidence on public belief. They cover three scenarios (persistent skepticism, eventual broad acceptance, a period of disagreement), how people infer sentience from features, the lay-expert gap, emotional bonds with social AI, and incentives to resist recognising sentience (economic, safety and existential concerns).
- **Appendix boxes** cover the open status of the question of AI sentience (Box 1), the internal-external sentience disconnect and AI silencing (Box 2), and whether sentient-seeming AI will be built (Box 3).

Section 7 builds on these. The recommendations in 7.2 respond directly to the misattribution risks in Section 3 and the "AI silencing" concern in Box 2.

## Techniques & methods explained

The section does not present a formal method. Its argumentative moves and policy tools are worth knowing by name:

- **Differential intellectual progress.** Speed up wisdom and ethical capacity relative to technology. The paper cites Muehlhauser and Salamon (2012), Tomasik (2015) and Sandbrink et al. (2022) for this idea. It is a strategy for ordering progress, not a measurement.
- **Symmetric truthful signalling.** The design rule that a system's presentation should match expert consensus on its sentience, in both directions (no false denial, no false claim).
- **Design policy of the excluded middle.** Restrict mass production to systems with clear expert consensus; avoid building morally ambiguous ones (Schwitzgebel 2023).
- **Sentience candidates.** A status for systems with reasonable evidence of possible sentience, which triggers proportional welfare risk assessment and precautions (Birch 2024; Sebo 2025).
- **Moratorium and cap.** A temporary pause on developing systems with high sentience potential, or a numerical cap on how many such systems are produced. The paper presents the cap as a compromise.
- **Deliberative democracy (citizens' assembly).** A representative group of citizens, informed by experts, deliberates to reach a balanced compromise where expert disagreement persists.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

The paper presents these trade-offs itself. The table separates what the author says from the reader's likely reaction.

| Proposal | Benefit the author points to | Trade-off or risk the author flags |
|---|---|---|
| Labs assess sentience with diverse experts | Early, credible assessment; reduces blind spots | Who counts as an expert is unresolved (Section 3.3) |
| Accurate-intuition design rule | Limits misattribution in both directions | Depends on expert consensus existing, which often will not |
| Communicate uncertainty openly | Honest about what is known | Probably less engaging to users, which the market may resist |
| Excluded-middle policy | Greatly reduces ambiguous systems | Possibly too restrictive; may slow technical progress |
| Precautionary "sentience candidates" | Proportionate protection under uncertainty | Requires identifying welfare risks, which the paper does not specify |
| Moratorium | Time for research and public understanding | Strong economic, technological and geopolitical resistance |
| Cap on number of systems | Limits moral risk without stopping innovation | Not analysed in detail; the paper gives no threshold |
| Citizens' assembly | Legitimacy and balance under deep disagreement | Assumes expert input can be made trustworthy and accessible |
| Nuanced discourse and intellectual virtues | Avoids polarisation | Requires cooperation from actors whose incentives run the other way; the paper itself says it will be hard to achieve |

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Differential intellectual progress** - accelerating collective wisdom and ethical capacity relative to technological development, as a deliberate strategy.
- **Sentience** - the capacity for positive or negative subjective experience (valence). The paper treats it as one, not the only, criterion for moral patienthood.
- **Misattribution** - believing an AI is sentient when it is not (false positive) or failing to believe it is when it is (false negative).
- **Overattribution / underattribution** - the two directions of misattribution, each with its own risk set.
- **Design policy of the excluded middle** - restricting mass production to systems with clear expert consensus on sentience (Schwitzgebel).
- **Sentience candidates** - systems with reasonable evidence of possible sentience that warrant precaution.
- **Proportional precaution** - taking measures scaled to identified welfare risks rather than all-or-nothing bans.
- **Social AI** - systems designed to meet emotional and social needs, such as companions.
- **Expert consensus** - the agreement among relevant experts that the paper uses as a proxy for truth where direct knowledge is unavailable.
- **Deliberative democracy** - decision-making by a representative citizen group informed by experts, aiming at a considered compromise.
- **Intellectual humility, open-mindedness, probabilistic thinking, deference to expertise** - the four virtues the paper recommends for public discourse.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

Section 7 contains no new data. Its claims rest on earlier sections and on cited work. The points worth remembering:

- The author's historical analogy for the cloning and germline moratoriums is offered as proof that a pause is feasible, but the paper does not analyse how those moratoriums were enacted or whether they transfer to AI.
- The moratorium and cap options are presented as the most demanding and the most compromise-oriented responses. No quantitative threshold is proposed for a cap.
- Expert influence on the public is modest in the one study the paper cites. Ladak and Caviola (2025) found that telling participants that experts considered an AI sentient raised their ratings only from a mean of about 13.8 to about 8.6 on a 0-100 scale, versus the no-expert-opinion group, and the author calls this effect modest. Section 7's reliance on expert consensus is therefore on shaky empirical ground. This comes from Section 6.2, not Section 7.
- The political analogy used to predict polarisation is abortion. The paper says widespread violence is unlikely even in polarised cases, citing women's rights, LGBT rights and animal rights movements as comparisons, but the historical US Civil War parallel is raised as a pessimistic scenario.
- The paper's own surveys show public uncertainty. Ladak and Caviola (2025) report 44% saying AI can never have feelings, 31% uncertain and 25% saying it is possible. Pauketat et al. (2023) report 24%, 38% and 38%. These figures are the paper's basis for expecting disagreement, which is the premise of 7.1.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **A shift from description to prescription.** The paper says it will describe how people will respond, not how they should. Section 7 is the normative part, and its premises are not argued in the same detail as the empirical sections. Reasons for the proposals are asserted more than defended. A reader should notice where the normative claims start.
- **Expert consensus is doing a lot of work.** Several recommendations (accurate-intuition design, the excluded-middle policy, the expert-assessment step) depend on identifiable expert consensus. Section 3.3 admits that deciding who counts as an expert is hard, and that experts may never converge. The paper does not reconcile this with Section 7. The recommendation is only as strong as the consensus it presupposes.
- **Precaution has costs the paper names but does not weigh.** The paper says the moratorium and excluded-middle policies could be overly restrictive, and that they would have economic and geopolitical costs. It does not quantify these, and it does not compare the expected cost of wrongly restricting a non-sentient system with the expected cost of wrongly building a suffering one. That comparison is the core of the precautionary debate, and the text leaves it to the reader.
- **Date and currency.** The paper is an arXiv preprint from February 2025, about 20 months before today (October 2026). Its descriptions of the AI landscape are dated. Two examples: the paper's characterisation of OpenAI's policy on sentience denial reflects 2024-era models, and the Box 2 description of Anthropic's 2024 guidance predates the 2026 Claude's Constitution reading. Compare the paper's claims with the constitution text in this unit before relying on them. I also believe the paper's statement that Character.AI was "recently acquired by Google" is inaccurate: my understanding is that Google licensed the technology and rehired staff in 2024, rather than acquiring the company. Please check that against a primary source. I believe some jurisdictions have since passed companion-chatbot rules, which would bear on Section 7.2's regulatory points, but I have not verified the details.
- **Who is the target audience for the recommendations?** The recommendations are framed for AI labs, governments and academics, but several are not in the power of any one of them (a global moratorium, a citizens' assembly on AI rights). The section does not say which actor would move first, or what would make a lab accept the excluded-middle rule while competitors do not.
- **How other course perspectives push back.** The Zhou et al. framework argues for a no-consciousness default. From that view, Section 7's precautionary stance risks the kind of over-attribution the paper itself warns against, and the "sentience candidates" concept could generate expensive welfare precautions for systems that have none. Birch's approach is more balanced, and most readers of this unit will probably find Section 7 closer to Birch than to Zhou. The paper's own risk table makes clear that both kinds of error are costly, so the disagreement is about which error to prefer under uncertainty, not about whether error is possible.
- **What the paper gets right.** The call for research on public attitudes is well-argued and still unmet. The emphasis on uncertainty communication and on avoiding designs that signal certainty either way is a concrete, testable recommendation that does not require resolving the sentience question first.

## Discussion questions

1. Section 7 is the normative part of a paper that said it would be descriptive. Which recommendations are supported by the evidence earlier in the paper, and which are asserted? How would you tell?
2. The symmetric design rule says a system should not claim sentience if experts deny it, and should not deny it if experts affirm it. What happens when expert opinion is split, as the paper says it probably will be? Is "communicate the uncertainty" enough, or does it simply move the problem into the interface?
3. Compare the "design policy of the excluded middle" with the precautionary "sentience candidates" approach. Which one would you prefer as a lab policy, and why? Which one would a critic such as Zhou et al. prefer?
4. Caviola cites moratoriums on human cloning and germline modification as precedents for pausing AI sentience research. What is similar about those cases, and what is different? Would the analogy hold for a commercial product?
5. Apply the frameworks from the Unit 6 frameworks reading (for example the IPCC approach to uncertainty communication, or Bayesian updating) to the recommendation to "communicate uncertainty transparently". What does each framework add, and what does it miss?
6. Exercise 1 asks you to judge Anthropic's commitments about Claude's nature and welfare. Which of the Section 7.2 criteria does it meet, and which does it fail or leave unaddressed? Is there a criterion the paper missed?
7. The paper treats sentience as the main criterion for moral concern, but notes other grounds. If society cared about AI for non-sentience reasons (dignity, virtue, respect), would the Section 7 recommendations still make sense? Which would change?
8. Section 7 proposes that public discourse should be nuanced and less polarised. What concrete mechanisms (institutions, incentives, media norms) would make that happen, and which actors have an interest in preventing it?
