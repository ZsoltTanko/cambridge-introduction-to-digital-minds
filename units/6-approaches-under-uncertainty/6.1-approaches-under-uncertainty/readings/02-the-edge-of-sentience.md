# The Edge of Sentience

**One-line takeaway:** When we do not know whether a being is sentient, Birch argues we should treat "sentience candidates" as a duty-triggering category: once a system is a credible candidate, ignoring its welfare risks is negligent or reckless, and the response should be a proportionate, informed, inclusive and democratic decision rather than either a ban or indefinite inaction.

## Where this sits in the course

**What the section is trying to establish.** Section 6.1 ("Approaches under Uncertainty") asks three things: what the precautionary principle is and how it might apply to AI, what suffering risks could arise from creating or merely perceiving digital minds, and what other frameworks could inform action. The learning outcomes are to explain the precautionary principle and its application to AI, explain suffering risks, analyse what AI companies and researchers are proposing, and identify existing frameworks and fields that could help. The section's job is to move the course from "we do not know if these systems are conscious" (Units 2 to 4) to "so what should anyone do now" (Units 5 to 8).

**What the course guide says.** The guide tells the learner to read only the selected sections of the Summary and Framework Proposals, and Chapter 6, and summarises the reading as outlining the precautionary principle and proposals for decisions made under moral and epistemic uncertainty about sentience. The assigned part is therefore the whole extract that the course supplies (framework summary, Framework Principles 1 to 3, AI Proposals 22 to 26, and sections 6.5 to 6.7). The extract is 11 pages and about 3,400 words of the book.

**Its role in the argument.** This is the only reading in the section that gives a worked, principled framework for acting under sentience uncertainty, as opposed to a comparison of positions. The other Core readings set up the problem (Dung on AI suffering risk), offer a competing default (Zhou et al. on a human-centric, default no-consciousness stance), show a real governance attempt (Claude's constitution), or list tools (the frameworks reading, Exercise 2). **Priority and unit question.** Core. It answers the unit's first question directly (what the precautionary principle looks like once made precise) and gives the vocabulary for the second and third.

## Purpose of the reading

Birch, a philosopher working on animal sentience and the ethics of uncertainty, wants a framework that people with very different views about consciousness can accept. His target is a kind of paralysis or abdication. One version says that if you do not personally believe a being is sentient, then the duty to avoid causing suffering does not apply to you. Birch rejects this outright and gives the crab example to show why: it would let someone boil crabs alive while forbidding the same act to people who believe crabs feel pain. His question is: given a duty to avoid gratuitous suffering, which almost everyone accepts, how should that duty apply when we are unsure whether a being is sentient at all? He presents the sentience candidate concept as a bridge between science and policy: it lets people agree on action even when they disagree about the underlying theory.

The extract does not name an intended audience. Its practical concern is institutional: Birch says the negligence-and-recklessness reasoning needs to be institutionalised at the level of policy and law. The AI material (Proposals 22 to 26) is the part most relevant to this course, and it is short. Most of the extract is written with animals in mind, and the AI proposals then extend the same logic to artificial systems.

## Detailed summary

This walk-through follows the order of the extract. The extract starts with the framework summary and ends with the close of Chapter 6.

### Part 1: The framework summary

**Core definitions.** A "sentient being", in the sense relevant to the framework, is a system with the capacity for valenced experiences, meaning experiences that feel good or bad, such as pain and pleasure. Birch then defines two bridging categories:

- A **sentience candidate** is a system for which the evidence base does two things. First, it implies a realistic possibility of sentience that would be irresponsible to ignore in policy decisions affecting the system. Second, the evidence is rich enough to identify welfare risks and to design and assess precautions.
- An **investigation priority** is a system that falls short of candidature, but where further investigation could plausibly lead to recognising it as a candidate, and where human activity affects it in ways that could call for precautions if it were a candidate.

The two-tier structure is the core device. Candidature is a threshold of evidence that is low enough to trigger obligations and high enough to be actionable. Investigation priority covers the cases where evidence is not yet at that level but a research effort is warranted.

**Framework Principle 1: a duty to avoid gratuitous suffering.** The minimum requirement is to avoid causing gratuitous suffering to sentient beings, whether deliberately or through recklessness or negligence. Suffering that arises from an activity that can be defended, despite reasonable efforts to prevent it, is not gratuitous. Suffering is gratuitous when the activity cannot be defended or the precautions taken are not proportionate to the risk. Birch presents this as a principle that people with different moral views can endorse for their own reasons. The phrase "at minimum" leaves room for those who think the duty goes further.

**Framework Principle 2: sentience candidature can warrant precautions.** For a sentience candidate, making decisions that create suffering risks without weighing which precautions would be proportionate counts as reckless or negligent. Disagreement about proportionality is expected, but a policy decision should still be made rather than deferred without end.

**Framework Principle 3: proportionality should be informed, democratic and inclusive.** Proportionality should be settled through informed, inclusive and democratic processes, and revisited periodically and when significant new evidence appears. One example Birch gives is a citizens' panel or assembly that tests proposed responses against four criteria he calls the PARC tests: permissibility in principle, adequacy, reasonable necessity and consistency.

### Part 2: The AI proposals (22 to 26)

The extract's AI section has five numbered proposals.

- **Proposal 22, sentience is not intelligence.** Intelligence and sentience could come apart substantially in AI. Precautions should track markers of sentience rather than markers of intelligence. Birch's example is that an emulation of an animal brain could be sentient without showing impressive intelligence.
- **Proposal 23, the gaming problem.** Any criteria for sentience candidature can be learned, explicitly or implicitly, by the AI system or its designer, who may then optimise for passing them. Markers that might have been gamed should be discounted.
- **Proposal 24, deep computational markers.** Computational functionalist theories such as global workspace theory and perceptual reality monitoring theory can serve as sources of markers. If an AI system, even one not deliberately built to have these features, shows signs that it has implicitly learned to recreate them, it should be treated as a sentience candidate.
- **Proposal 25, the run-ahead principle.** Regulation of the development of sentient AI should run ahead of what is proportionate to today's risks. It should also consider credible future trajectories.
- **Proposal 26, codes of good practice and licensing.** Companies that try to create artificial sentience candidates, or whose work creates even a small risk of doing so (even unintentionally), should need a licence. Obtaining one would depend on committing to a code of good practice with transparency norms and, where necessary, contributing funding toward that code.

### Part 3: Chapter 6, "Back to the Edge"

**The duty at the edge.** Birch returns to the crab problem and sets out his own proposal. Whenever a being is a sentience candidate, there is a realistic possibility that our actions can make it suffer, and we should take that possibility seriously as part of the duty to avoid gratuitous suffering. Failing to see the possibility because one has not engaged with the evidence is negligent. Accepting that the possibility is real and then refusing to take proportionate steps is reckless. If suffering occurs, it counts as gratuitous and we acted wrongly, even without any intention to cause harm.

Birch states that this gives a sufficient condition for recklessness or negligence. If the impact of a decision on a sentience candidate was ignored when the decision was made, and the candidate suffers as a result, the suffering came from negligence or recklessness. The crab example is repeated. Someone responsible for slaughtering crabs should inform themselves of the scientific reasons to think crabs may be sentient, should consider precautions such as stunning them first, and should consider whether those precautions would be proportionate. Refusing to do this, and then causing suffering, is negligent.

**Why the duty is set at candidature.** The threshold is set at candidature, not certainty or high probability. Birch allows that some people (he mentions Jains) will think the candidate concept is too narrow and that the duty applies more widely, even to all living things. He does not accept a narrower view, such as the view that the duty applies only to beings known or highly likely to be sentient. Such a view would license negligence and recklessness toward animals like birds, for example. Birch notes that some people have argued that the avian pallium is so different from the mammalian cortex that there are no ethical limits on how birds are treated. He calls this absurd. Differences in brain organisation are an obstacle to certainty, but they do not make reckless treatment acceptable.

**Framework Principle 2 explained.** Birch admits the principle is deliberately thin. It says nothing about which specific precautions are warranted. He defers those questions to Parts III and IV of the book. The principle also allows that the proportionate response may be to do nothing. Birch uses microscopic dust mites as the example: even if there were enough evidence to count them as candidates, nothing might pass the proportionality tests. The principle commits us to deciding, not to deciding a particular way.

**Procedures where outcomes are contested.** When people disagree about what should be done, they may still agree on how decisions should be made. Birch proposes that decisions be informed, inclusive and democratic. "Informed" means based on the latest evidence, which requires that decisions can be revisited. "Inclusive" means that all interests affected are represented. For example, a regulation affecting an industry should involve that industry, and the affected animals' interests should be represented by animal welfare experts or organisations. "Democratic" means the process is linked to democratic mechanisms that give policy decisions legitimacy. Birch says he will revisit the democratic question in the next chapter, and he cites the debate over the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 as an example that could be improved.

### Part 4: Section 6.6, relation to other precautionary ideas

Birch draws on Daniel Steel, who argues that there is no single precautionary principle, only many precautionary principles for different areas. What they share, according to Steel, is a "meta-precautionary principle": scientific uncertainty should never lead to policy paralysis, and we should be ready to manage risks before conclusive evidence arrives. Birch says his framework belongs to this family. Principle 2's commitment to reaching a decision is an endorsement of this meta-principle. Birch argues that the norm is especially sensible at the edge of sentience, where reasonable disagreement is wide, conclusive evidence is unlikely soon, and refusing to think about the issue until evidence arrives carries large risks.

Birch lists three further commitments that his framework shares with other precautionary principles:

1. **Proportionality.** Precautions must pass a proportionality test. Activities should not be banned at the first sign of risk. Birch separates "radical" precautionary principles, which ban activities on a hair trigger and, in Birch's view, invariably fail to win consensus, from "moderate" ones, which manage risk sensibly. He places his framework in the moderate group.
2. **Inclusiveness.** Discussions about proportionality should include the wider public, not just experts.
3. **Low evidential bar.** The threshold for starting a proportionality discussion should be set low on purpose. Evidence that is far from conclusive can still justify such a discussion. This does not allow weak evidence to justify radical policy responses. Experts should communicate thin evidence honestly, using phrases such as "low confidence" or "remote chance", and the inclusive process should take that into account.

Birch also stresses that the justification is free-standing. It is bottom-up, not top-down. It starts from the agreed duty to avoid gratuitous suffering and asks how to apply that duty under uncertainty about sentience. It does not start from a general case for precaution. A person could reject precautionary principles for climate policy, public health or environmental regulation and still accept this framework.

### Part 5: Section 6.7, summary of Chapter 6

The chapter's job is to find consensus across the zone of reasonable disagreement. It does so with two bridging concepts, sentience candidates and investigation priorities. The zone of disagreement is wide, but Birch argues it does not rule out a meta-consensus on what counts as a candidate. Birch picks out certain theories as relevant to later chapters. Panksepp, Damasio and Merker link sentience to evolutionarily old midbrain mechanisms. Birch says that these theories describe realistic possibilities, so a system with such mechanisms is a sentience candidate. Where evidence falls short of candidature but welfare risks may still call for a precautionary response, the system is an investigation priority.

The chapter then restates the ethical consensus, the duty to avoid gratuitous suffering, and the combined principle that precautions may be proportionate whenever a being is a sentience candidate. What proportionality requires will be disputed. Birch notes that everyone can agree that a free-for-all, where anyone may treat candidates however they wish, is unacceptable. Informed and inclusive democratic processes are needed to settle proportionality, and those processes should be revisited. Birch ends by saying the framework is "precautionary" in the sense that it keeps the core tenets: uncertainty should not paralyse decisions, precautions should be proportionate, procedures should be democratic and inclusive, and the bar for triggering them should be low.

## Techniques & methods explained

The reading explains several argumentative and policy tools. Each is listed with what it is, how it works and what it is for, as the source presents it. The "where it fails" lines are the summarizer's reading of gaps and objections, not Birch's own criticisms, unless a sentence attributes them to him.

**1. The sentience candidate test (two-part evidence threshold).**
- *What it is:* A category for systems where evidence makes sentience a realistic possibility and is rich enough to act on.
- *How it works:* (a) Is the possibility realistic enough that ignoring it in a policy decision would be irresponsible? (b) Is the evidence rich enough to identify welfare risks and design and assess precautions? If both hold, the system is a candidate.
- *What for:* To bridge science and policy by giving a shared threshold for action without requiring agreement on theory.
- *Where it fails (summarizer's analysis):* The extract does not say how to judge a "realistic possibility" or who draws the line. Birch expects reasonable disagreement about proportionality, but he does not say the same about candidature.

**2. The investigation priority category.**
- *What it is:* A category one level below candidature, for systems that need study before action can be justified.
- *How it works:* A system qualifies if further study could plausibly make it a candidate and if human activity affects it in ways that could call for precautions.
- *What for:* To direct research effort toward systems where a finding would matter.
- *Where it fails:* The source does not say how many investigation priorities there might be or how they should be ranked.

**3. Negligence and recklessness as a route to wrongdoing.**
- *What it is:* A way of tying moral responsibility to the decision-maker's attention to evidence.
- *How it works:* Not learning about the evidence is negligent. Learning about it and refusing to take proportionate steps is reckless. If a candidate suffers as a result, the suffering is gratuitous and the agent acted wrongly, even without intent.
- *What for:* To remove the option of avoiding responsibility by refusing to believe a being is sentient.
- *Where it fails:* Birch says the reckless category can be reserved for intentional disregard, and the source does not fully settle the terminology.

**4. Proportionality via PARC tests.**
- *What it is:* A four-part test that a citizens' panel or assembly could use to judge a proposed precaution.
- *How it works:* The panel assesses (1) permissibility in principle, (2) adequacy, (3) reasonable necessity and (4) consistency.
- *What for:* To turn the general call for proportionate precautions into a structured debate.
- *Where it fails:* Birch presents the PARC tests as an example, not a finished procedure, and says the democratic process needs more work in the next chapters. The source does not show how the tests resolve clashes between criteria.

**5. The gaming problem.**
- *What it is:* A warning that any published test for sentience may be learned and then gamed by a system or its designer.
- *How it works:* Once criteria are known, designers or systems can optimise for them. Markers that may have been gamed should be discounted.
- *What for:* To prevent a system that passes tests without the underlying property from being treated as a sentience candidate, or from being built to pass.
- *Where it fails:* The source does not say how to tell whether a marker has been gamed, which is a serious practical gap.

**6. Computational markers from functionalist theories.**
- *What it is:* Using computational theories of consciousness (global workspace, perceptual reality monitoring) as sources of observable markers.
- *How it works:* Look for the architecture the theory describes. If an AI system shows that it has, even implicitly, learned to recreate those features, treat it as a candidate.
- *What for:* To give a route from theory to observable tests for AI. Proposal 22 separately requires that the markers track sentience rather than intelligence.
- *Where it fails:* Birch relies on theories that are themselves disputed, and the source does not discuss what happens if a theory is wrong.

**7. The run-ahead principle.**
- *What it is:* A forward-looking regulatory rule.
- *How it works:* Regulation should be ahead of the proportionate response to current risk, given credible future trajectories.
- *What for:* To avoid regulating only after harm has occurred.
- *Where it fails:* The source does not explain how to identify "credible" trajectories or how far ahead to run.

**8. Licensing with codes of good practice.**
- *What it is:* A licensing scheme for developers of artificial sentience candidates or of work that risks creating them.
- *How it works:* Developers obtain a licence by signing a code of good practice with transparency norms and helping fund that code where needed.
- *What for:* To put precautions into institutions, which the earlier "basic line of thought" passage says must happen at the level of policy and law.
- *Where it fails:* The threshold ("even a small risk") is broad, and the source does not explain how the scope would be defined or enforced.

**9. Moderate versus radical precautionary principles.**
- *What it is:* The distinction Birch uses to place his own framework.
- *How it works:* Radical principles ban activities at first sign of risk. Moderate principles require a proportionality assessment before action and a low threshold for starting that assessment.
- *What for:* To show that the framework can get consensus support, and that it is not a call to ban AI research.
- *Where it fails:* The line between "moderate" and "radical" depends on where proportionality is judged, so the distinction only moves the disagreement.

**10. Meta-precautionary principle (from Steel).**
- *What it is:* The norm that scientific uncertainty should never by itself cause policy paralysis.
- *How it works:* Act to manage risk before conclusive evidence exists, in proportion to the threat.
- *What for:* To justify deciding under uncertainty without requiring conclusive evidence.
- *Where it fails:* Birch allows that the norm may not always be good for policy in general, but he thinks it is clearly good at the edge of sentience.

| Feature | Source's view (what Birch argues) | Possible objection (my analysis, not the source's) |

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Sentience (as used here)** - the capacity for valenced experiences, such as pain and pleasure. The framework is about this capacity, not intelligence.
- **Sentience candidate** - a system where the evidence implies a realistic possibility of sentience, and is rich enough to identify welfare risks and design precautions. Candidates trigger the duty.
- **Investigation priority** - a system below candidature where further study could plausibly change that, and where human activity affects it in ways that may call for precautions.
- **Gratuitous suffering** - suffering that happens during an indefensible activity, or where precautions fall short of what is proportionate.
- **Proportionality** - the test of whether precautions match the risk, decided through debate rather than set by a ban.
- **Recklessness and negligence** - failing to take a known risk seriously (reckless, if the agent knows and disregards it) or failing to inform oneself (negligent).
- **PARC tests** - permissibility in principle, adequacy, reasonable necessity and consistency. A structured way to test proposed precautions.
- **Informed, inclusive, democratic** - the three requirements Birch places on the process of deciding proportionality.
- **Meta-precautionary principle** - Steel's norm that scientific uncertainty should not lead to policy paralysis.
- **Moderate vs. radical precaution** - a split between precautionary principles that manage risk with proportionality, and those that ban on a hair trigger.
- **Gaming problem** - the risk that a system or its designer learns the criteria for sentience and optimises for them.
- **Computational functionalism** - the view that mental states are defined by their functional organisation, here used through global workspace and perceptual reality monitoring theories as sources of markers. (General definition; the extract does not define the term.)
- **Run-ahead principle** - regulation should stay ahead of the proportionate response to both current and credible future risks.
- **Free-standing justification** - a justification that does not depend on accepting precaution in other areas, grounded in the duty to avoid gratuitous suffering.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis. It is not the source's view.*

**Date and currency.** The book was published in 2024 and the course presents the reading as current. Today is October 2026, so the reading is about two years old. Several things have moved since then, though I cannot verify developments in detail. Frontier labs have started to write about model welfare and moral status in public documents, which the course's own Claude's Constitution reading (2026) illustrates. The gap between that and the theories of consciousness that Birch uses has not closed. The AI section of the book is short, and most of the framework's worked examples are about animals. Treat the AI application as Birch's proposal rather than a tested method.

**Assumptions.**
- The argument assumes that the duty to avoid gratuitous suffering is shared. Birch says it is a consensus, but the consensus is thinner than it looks. Some people, including some who accept the duty for animals, would not extend it to all systems that could be candidates.
- The argument also assumes that "realistic possibility" can be judged in a way that is shared across people. It is not clear that it can.
- The negligence-and-recklessness argument makes the duty a function of evidence awareness. A person who never looks at the evidence is treated as negligent, which is the right incentive. But it also means the content of the duty depends on which evidence is treated as relevant, and that is contested.

**Contested claims and limitations.**
- The move from "realistic possibility" to "treat as candidate" is a threshold choice, and the framework does not specify who makes it. The democratic process is supposed to handle this, but the process itself depends on the evidence that is in dispute.
- The gaming problem (Proposal 23) is a real problem that the source names but does not solve. If markers must be public for accountability, they can be learned and optimised for. If they are kept private, the decision cannot be audited. The source leaves this tension open.
- Functionalist markers (Proposal 24) depend on the truth of the theories they come from. Birch uses global workspace and related theories as sources, but the course's Unit 2 theories are disputed. A wrong theory could mislabel systems in both directions.
- Licensing for "even a small risk" (Proposal 26) is broad. Enforcement is hard, and the scope could push development offshore or into unlicensed settings. I think this proposal needs a much tighter definition before it could be implemented.
- The "low evidential bar" is a deliberate design choice. It has a real cost: it can drive heavy regulation on speculative evidence. Birch's answer is that thin evidence should not justify radical responses. That is reasonable, but the line between "discuss proportionality" and "act" may be hard to hold in practice, especially once a process is in place.

**Pushback from other perspectives in the course.**
- The **Zhou et al.** framework argues for a default of no consciousness and a human-centric approach, and it treats anthropomorphism as a risk. From that side, Birch's low bar would encourage people to attribute sentience to AI systems on thin evidence, which is the error the paper warns against. Birch's reply would be that the duty is triggered by candidacy, not by belief, so there is no need to attribute sentience. But the practical question is which default the public and companies will adopt.
- **Shanahan** (role play with LLMs) would push back on the assumption that a system which says it feels something is a candidate. His framing of LLMs as role-players would question whether the behavioural evidence Birch needs is informative at all.
- **Dung** (suffering risks) would likely ask whether the candidature threshold captures the kinds of suffering risk that matter, and whether Birch's approach fits the desirable characteristics he describes. This is inferred from the guide's summary, not from reading Dung's text here.
- **Hansson** asks how far precaution should be pushed. Birch's proportionality test is his answer, but Hansson would press on what gets counted as proportionate and who decides.

**What I would check next.** Whether the two-tier evidence structure produces stable classifications in practice, and whether any licensing or code-of-practice regime based on this framework has been tried. I cannot confirm either from the materials here.
