# Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence: Insights from the Science of Consciousness

**One-line takeaway:** Judge whether an AI system might be conscious by checking whether its internal computational architecture has features that leading neuroscientific theories tie to consciousness (14 "indicator properties"), not by how human-like it acts; by that rubric no current system is a strong candidate, but the authors see no obvious technical barrier to building one that satisfies the indicators.

## Where this sits in the course

## Purpose of the reading

The report is written by 19 researchers from philosophy, neuroscience and AI, with Patrick Butlin and Robert Long as joint first and corresponding authors. Its aim is to show that whether AI systems might be conscious is a question that can be approached scientifically and to provide a provisional rubric for doing so. The authors give three reasons for publishing now: they take seriously that conscious AI could be built within a few decades; large language model systems that imitate conversation will likely lead many people to believe that some AI systems are conscious; and both possibilities raise moral and social questions, for which society will be better equipped if it is better informed about the science.

The question it answers is: given our best-supported scientific theories of consciousness, what can we say about whether current and near-term AI systems are likely to be conscious, and what would a more conscious-looking system have to be like? The report aims for a mainstream, interdisciplinary perspective that can serve as a basis for future research.

## Detailed summary

### The assigned part: Executive Summary (report pp. 3-6)

**Opening motivation.** The summary starts from the pace of AI progress and from the observation that leading researchers are borrowing functions associated with consciousness in human brains to improve AI. It adds that conversational AI is likely to make people attribute consciousness to systems that may not have it. The authors say the report's contributions are: (1) showing that assessing consciousness in AI is scientifically tractable because consciousness can be studied scientifically and the findings apply to AI; (2) proposing a rubric in the form of indicator properties derived from scientific theories; and (3) giving initial evidence that many of these properties can be built with current techniques, while no current system looks like a strong candidate.

**The three tenets.**
1. *Computational functionalism* is adopted as a working hypothesis. It holds that performing computations of the right kind is necessary and sufficient for consciousness. The authors call it mainstream but disputed. They adopt it for pragmatic reasons: unlike rival views, it implies that AI consciousness is possible in principle and that studying how AI systems work is relevant to whether they are conscious.
2. *Neuroscientific theories of consciousness* have meaningful empirical support, and they aim to identify functions that are necessary and sufficient for consciousness in humans. If computational functionalism holds, similar functions would suffice in AI.
3. *A theory-heavy approach* is the most suitable method. Ask whether an AI system performs functions similar to those that the theories associate with consciousness, then set credence from three things: how similar the functions are, how strong the evidence is for the theory, and how confident one is in computational functionalism. The main rival, behavioural testing, is judged unreliable because AI systems can be trained to mimic human behaviour while working in very different ways.

**Which theories, and which are left out.** The authors do not endorse any one theory. The theories used are recurrent processing theory, global workspace theory, computational higher-order theories and others (attention schema theory, predictive processing, and agency and embodiment considerations). Integrated information theory is excluded because its standard form is incompatible with computational functionalism. The summary notes that each indicator is said to be necessary by one or more theories and that some subsets are said to be jointly sufficient, but the authors' own claim is weaker: systems with more indicators are more likely to be conscious.

**The indicator list (Table 1).** There are 14 indicators:
- Recurrent processing theory: RPT-1 (input modules using algorithmic recurrence); RPT-2 (input modules generating organised, integrated perceptual representations).
- Global workspace theory: GWT-1 (multiple specialised modules operating in parallel); GWT-2 (limited-capacity workspace, a bottleneck, and a selective attention mechanism); GWT-3 (global broadcast to all modules); GWT-4 (state-dependent attention allowing the workspace to query modules in succession).
- Computational higher-order theories: HOT-1 (generative, top-down or noisy perception modules); HOT-2 (metacognitive monitoring that separates reliable perceptual representations from noise); HOT-3 (agency guided by a general belief-formation and action-selection system, with a strong disposition to update beliefs from monitoring); HOT-4 (sparse and smooth coding generating a "quality space").
- Attention schema theory: AST-1 (a predictive model representing and controlling the current state of attention).
- Predictive processing: PP-1 (input modules using predictive coding).
- Agency and embodiment: AE-1 (learning from feedback and selecting outputs to pursue goals, especially with flexible responsiveness to competing goals); AE-2 (modelling output-input contingencies and using that model in perception or control).

The authors stress that the list is provisional and expected to change.

**Implementation and case studies (summary of Section 3).** Most indicators could be built with standard machine learning, though combining several is harder and needs experimentation. RPT-1 is already clearly met by existing systems, and the first part of AE-1 (agency) is arguably met. Some researchers have built systems designed around global workspace or attention schema theory. The authors then examine large language models and the Perceiver architecture against the global workspace indicators, and three embodied or agentic systems (DeepMind's Adaptive Agent, a virtual rodent, and PaLM-E) against the agency and embodiment indicators. Their verdict is that none is a strong candidate, although the case studies are used to show how the indicators should be applied.

**Closing lines of the excerpt.** The authors say the report does not have the last word. It recommends support for research on the science of consciousness and its application to AI, and says the moral and social risks of building conscious AI need urgent attention, a topic the report says it does not address. The authors conclude that, if computational functionalism is true, conscious AI could plausibly be built in the near term.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

**Section 1 (Introduction: terminology, methods, assumptions).** The report uses "conscious" to mean phenomenal consciousness, or subjective experience: a system is conscious when there is "something it is like" to be it. Because this is hard to define, the authors work with examples, positive ones such as seeing, hearing and bodily pain, and negative ones such as hormone regulation and masked stimuli that affect behaviour without being seen. They distinguish phenomenal consciousness from access consciousness (information available for reasoning and report, after Block) and treat "sentience" as a term that is used inconsistently. They identify two problems with relying on reports: reports may track cognitive access rather than experience (reporting itself needs processing), and some conscious subjects, such as non-human animals, infants and some people with cognitive disabilities, cannot report. They mention no-report paradigms and metacognitive confidence as partial remedies. Box 1 notes that consciousness might be indeterminate, graded, or multidimensional; the authors write as though it is all-or-nothing for convenience. They recommend thinking in terms of credence. Section 1.2.1 explains computational functionalism: a state is conscious if it plays the right role in implementing the right algorithm, and substrate matters only insofar as it affects which algorithms can run. The authors note that a substrate such as Swiss cheese cannot carry the relevant computations, so the thesis is not "anything goes". Section 1.2.2 separates scientific theories (which say which brain processes go with consciousness) from metaphysical positions (materialism, property dualism, panpsychism, illusionism), arguing that the science can proceed under each. Section 1.2.3 explains the theory-heavy approach and Birch's objection that evidence from humans cannot show how far a theory's conditions can be relaxed while remaining sufficient. The report notes that theories can be read restrictively (very specific human features) or liberally (surprisingly simple systems qualify), and that moderate readings are attractive but poorly supported.

**Section 2.1 (Recurrent processing theory).** RPT says conscious vision needs recurrent signals, from higher to lower visual areas, after an initial feedforward sweep that is not sufficient for experience. Evidence includes backward masking and transcranial magnetic stimulation studies suggesting that feedforward activity in primary visual cortex is not enough. The report distinguishes implementational recurrence (feedback loops in hardware) from algorithmic recurrence (a deep feedforward network with shared weights that repeats operations). Current AI has algorithmic recurrence but not implementational recurrence, and the authors adopt only the algorithmic version, which gives RPT-1. RPT-2 concerns organised perceptual scenes with figure-ground segregation. The report sets aside a biological interpretation (a particular neural mechanism would rule out AI) and a visual-only interpretation (which would leave AI with little relevance).

**Section 2.2 (Global workspace theory).** Specialised modules work in parallel but share information through a limited-capacity workspace. Whatever is broadcast from the workspace is conscious, on the phenomenal reading the authors adopt. The neural version (Dehaene and colleagues) says that when a percept is strong enough, "ignition" occurs in widely distributed frontoparietal "workspace neurons", sustained by recurrence; ignition is a step function. Evidence is from contrastive analysis: conscious perception is associated with late (around 250-300 ms) reverberant activity including prefrontal cortex. Two monkey studies are discussed: a binocular rivalry study in which prefrontal activity could decode the presumed percept without requiring reports, and a study in which prefrontal activity seemed to encode the conscious percept even in false alarms. The report then argues that GWT can be expressed in four progressively stronger conditions (GWT-1 to GWT-4), with a bottleneck and attention forcing efficient information sharing. It describes functions the workspace is said to support: sustained control of modules, learning from novel stimuli, crossmodal analogies, and "System 2" planning. Box 3 contrasts neuroscience attention with transformer self-attention: self-attention is graded, not ignition-like, and has no model of attention on top of attention.

**Section 2.3 (Higher-order theories).** Higher-order theories hold that a conscious state is one the subject represents, or monitors, through a higher-order representation. The "simple argument" motivates them: if a state is conscious, the subject is aware of being in it, and awareness involves representation. The report focuses on perceptual reality monitoring (PRM) and higher-order state space (HOSS) theory. PRM says the monitoring mechanism discriminates meaningful perceptual activity from noise (activity from stimuli, from memory or imagination, or random noise) and tags reliable representations as "real". Conscious states have "assertoric force", a tendency to be believed, so PRM requires the output to feed a general belief-forming and decision system. Higher-order theories reject the claim that global broadcast is necessary and sufficient. The Lau and Passingham (2006) masking experiment is central: two masking conditions gave equal discrimination performance but different rates of reporting having seen the stimulus, which higher-order theorists see as a problem for GWT. The report presents this as the higher-order theorists' argument, and adds that performance confounds may undermine the evidence for GWT. The quality-space hypothesis (HOT-4) says phenomenal qualities are discriminations allowed for the system, and that sparse, smooth coding grounds them.

**Section 2.4 (Other theories and conditions).**
- *Attention schema theory (AST).* The brain builds a model of attention that represents, and may misrepresent, what is attended. This model helps control attention, and its content explains why experience seems immediate and mysterious. AST is a higher-order theory that also aims to explain why people believe what they believe about consciousness (the meta-problem), so it can be read as explaining consciousness away or as giving conditions for it.
- *Predictive processing (PP).* Presented as a framework for cognition, not as a theory of consciousness, with active inference. PP-1 is added as an indicator because many researchers treat predictive coding as a plausible necessary condition.
- *Midbrain theory.* Merker argues that the midbrain integrates spatial, vestibular and homeostatic information into a model of the self in the environment, which is sufficient for experience. This favours indicators about integrated, purposeful navigation.
- *Unlimited associative learning (UAL).* Ginsburg and Jablonka's framework treats UAL as a transition marker for consciousness in animals. The authors do not add UAL as an indicator because it is a behavioural marker, and an AI could have the capacity without the hallmarks.
- *Agency and embodiment.* AlexNet is used as a contrast: it classifies images but does not act or pursue goals. The authors weigh arguments that agency (learning from feedback to pursue goals), flexible goals and intentional agency are necessary (Hurley, Dretske, Russell and Norvig's liberal definition). They propose a substantive definition of agency that requires outputs to affect later inputs, goal pursuit, and learning goal-conducive outputs, and say reinforcement learning typically meets these criteria. Embodiment is defined by output-input models (forward models) used in perception and control. The report discusses AlphaGo as an agent without embodiment, Hurley's notion of a "perspective", the sensorimotor theory, and autopoiesis and self-maintenance (Thompson, Godfrey-Smith, Seth).
- *Narrow formulations.* Self-maintenance and some agency and embodiment conditions depend on external conditions, so they may be incompatible with computational functionalism. The authors reformulate AE-1 and AE-2 "narrowly" so that they refer only to the system's internal learning and control. They say a narrow self-maintenance indicator would go against the spirit of the theories and so leave it out. Intentional agency is folded into HOT-3 and the flexible-goals point into AE-1.
- *Time and recurrence.* Human experience seems integrated over time, which might count against discrete forward passes in neural networks. The report gives responses: amnesia patients may have disjointed experiences; the "dynamic snapshot" view says experience is discrete but sampled from continuous activity. It still concludes that algorithmic recurrence is probably necessary for human-like temporal experience, which reinforces RPT-1.

**Section 2.5 (Indicator list and entailments, Table 2).** The indicators are described as a rubric, not as conditions. Some are plausibly necessary but do not raise the probability much alone (perhaps RPT-1, GWT-1, HOT-1). The Table 2 entailments: RPT-1 and RPT-2 are largely independent; GWT-1 to GWT-4 build on each other, and GWT-3 and GWT-4 entail RPT-1; HOT-1 to HOT-3 build on each other, and HOT-3 entails AE-1; HOT-4 is independent; PP-1 entails RPT-1 and HOT-1; AE-2 is supported by the midbrain and UAL theories, and AE-2 systems are likely, though not guaranteed, to meet AE-1.

**Section 3.1 (Implementing the indicators).** The authors show how each indicator might be built.
- *RPT and PP.* Recurrent neural networks, LSTMs and GRUs already have algorithmic recurrence. Predictive-coding systems such as PredNet predict video frames and seem to represent objects and their spatial relations. Some deep convolutional networks are criticised as sensitive to local textures rather than global shapes. DALL-E 2 struggled with unfamiliar object arrangements.
- *GWT.* VanRullen and Kanai (2021) propose a roadmap with specialised generative modules, a shared latent workspace with lower capacity than the modules combined, and key-query attention for selection. Goyal et al. (2022) implement key-query selection with a shared space and end-to-end training, but the "modules" are token or image-patch elements, so it is unclear they are specialised. Attractor dynamics in a recurrent network can implement the bottleneck. Global broadcast needs inputs from the workspace to every module, and the authors discuss a leaky integrator for sustained broadcast. GWT-4 can be met by unrolling the workspace-module loop through time, but this requires training that teaches sequential module recruitment.
- *PRM and HOT.* HOT-4 is met by smooth representations, and sparsity can be encouraged by regularisation or softmax. HOT-1 and HOT-2 call for a second-order network that outputs a probability that a first-order representation is veridical, trained with ground truth (for example, averaging noisy representations over time), with surrogate tasks (predicting future representations, where externally generated input is more predictable than noise), or as a world model (Bayesian inference, GFlowNets). A GAN discriminator can play the reality-monitoring role. HOT-3 can be approximated with a transformer whose queries attend only to percepts tagged "real", which makes percepts hard to reason away.
- *AST.* Wilterson and Graziano (2021) trained a reinforcement learning network with a 200-neuron, three-layer architecture to catch a falling ball. An "attention schema" input helped even though the spotlight was still present. This shows part of AST-1 but is not a predictive model. Liu et al. (2023) used multi-agent reinforcement learning with multi-head attention and a recurrent "internal control" network that learns to predict the attention layers, which is closer to a learned predictive model.
- *Agency and embodiment.* Reinforcement learning is argued to be sufficient for AE-1 because it optimises cumulative reward over interactions where outputs shape later inputs. Flexible responsiveness to competing goals is discussed through homeostatic models and multi-module reward schemes. For AE-2, most video prediction is not enough, because the perceptual use must handle unpredictable exogenous change. Friedrich et al. (2021) is an example of a forward model used with Kalman filtering for motor control, but for relatively simple effectors in virtual environments.

**Section 3.2 (Case studies).**
- *Transformer LLMs (GPT-3, GPT-4, LaMDA).* Self-attention can be read as modules sharing information, and the residual stream as a workspace. But the residual stream is the same dimensionality as the input (so it is not a bottleneck), and Transformers are not recurrent (there is no module that writes to the workspace and reads back). The authors conclude that there is only a relatively weak case for any GWT-derived indicator.
- *Perceiver and Perceiver IO.* These use a limited-capacity latent space that integrates specialists via cross-attention, which gives GWT-1, GWT-2 and part of GWT-4. But modules are optional, the number of inputs processed in sequence is limited, the system must be reset for each task, and there is no global broadcast: input modules do not receive workspace information.
- *PaLM-E.* A decoder-only language model paired with a separately trained policy unit that controls a robot. The authors see the complete system as naturally embodied, but both components imitate human behaviour (next-token prediction, imitation of human control), and the system does not learn from success or failure. The policy unit is the best candidate for embodiment, since it can detect some mismatches between observation history and prediction, but it does not learn how its own outputs affect inputs.
- *Virtual rodent (Merel et al. 2019).* A 38-degree-of-freedom body modelled on a rat, controlled by an LSTM actor-critic trained end-to-end by reinforcement learning on four tasks in a static 3D environment. The architecture could learn a self-model, but the environment did not change exogenously, and the tasks could be solved with stereotyped movements, so it is unclear whether the system needed a self-model.
- *AdA (DeepMind Adaptive Agent).* A Transformer that encodes the past few hundred timesteps, feeding an LSTM, trained by meta-reinforcement learning over many tasks, so that it learns an implicit online learning algorithm and adapts to new tasks at human timescales. The authors judge it the most likely of the three to be embodied by their standards, though it is not physically embodied, because it has an explicit objective to predict sequences of interleaved inputs and outputs.

**Section 4 (Implications).** The authors consider under-attribution (failing to recognise conscious AI, with moral risk; since being conscious differs from being capable of valenced suffering, the key question for moral status may be sentience, and theories of valence are underdeveloped) and over-attribution (anthropomorphism and the "intentional stance" make people attribute mental states; the authors cite the case of Blake Lemoine and companion chatbots; over-attribution could misallocate resources, undermine better-supported claims, justify differential treatment, and expose vulnerable people to manipulation). Section 4.2 argues that consciousness does not imply human-like motives or emotions, and that some current AI concerns (bias, repression, job displacement, existential risk through goal misspecification) do not depend on consciousness. Section 4.3 recommends research on the science of consciousness, a "theory-heavy" method both prospectively and retrospectively (with interpretability tools), research on valence and affect, and better behavioural tests if they can be made hard to game. Box 4 lists open questions: other theories, computational functionalism versus rival views (including whether conventional hardware can host consciousness), valence in AI, interpretability, behavioural tests and introspection, and the ethics of research on conscious AI.

## Techniques & methods explained

**1. The theory-heavy assessment.** *What it is:* a way of judging AI consciousness by checking whether a system performs functions that scientific theories associate with consciousness. *How it works:* (a) choose theories and the working assumption of computational functionalism; (b) translate each theory into computational indicator properties; (c) inspect the system's architecture, training and, where needed, internals (for example, through interpretability) to see whether each indicator is present; (d) combine evidence into a credence, weighing similarity, theory strength and confidence in computational functionalism. *Purpose:* to avoid being fooled by behaviour and to allow systematic comparison. *Where it fails:* the indicators are ambiguous and need interpretation; architecture and behaviour may not reveal internal models; the method rests on computational functionalism; there is no formula for aggregating indicators into a single credence; the authors' illustration of about 0.5 credence in a conjunction of theoretical claims comes without a method to justify it (my analysis); and a system can satisfy an indicator in a weak way (the report calls RPT-1 a weak condition that many systems already meet, so meeting it says little on its own; this is my analysis).

**2. Contrastive analysis and no-report paradigms.** *What it is:* the experimental method used to find neural correlates of consciousness. *How it works:* present stimuli that are consciously and unconsciously processed under otherwise matched conditions, compare brain activity, and control for other differences. No-report paradigms take reports out of the main experiment and calibrate other measures in separate experiments. *Purpose:* to separate experience from the processes that support report. *Where it fails:* it assumes that reports track experience; its no-report variant is not a "magic bullet"; and phenomenal consciousness may overflow cognitive access, which is disputed.

**3. Algorithmic versus implementational recurrence.** A way of distinguishing feedback loops in hardware (brain) from repeated operations in a weight-shared network (AI). The authors adopt only the algorithmic reading, which makes the indicator available for software systems but is a weaker claim than the implementational reading. Doerig et al. (2019) read RPT as requiring implementational recurrence, while Lamme has suggested to the authors that the algorithmic reading is also available to RPT.

**4. Key-query attention, attractor dynamics and the workspace bottleneck.** Attention selects which inputs to pass on; attractor dynamics create a many-to-one mapping that limits capacity; the workspace is a shared latent space that broadcasts to all modules. *Where it fails:* self-attention is graded, with no ignition step; the proposals are roadmaps rather than working systems; and training for sequential control is hard.

**5. Second-order monitoring networks (PRM implementation).** A first-order network gives perceptual representations and a parallel second-order network outputs the probability that each is veridical. Training options include supervised signals (averaged noisy representations), surrogate predictive tasks, world-model inference (GFlowNets), or a GAN discriminator. A threshold then tags percepts as "real". *Where it fails:* the ground-truth signals may be hard to obtain; the authors knew of no implementation attempt for PRM when the report was written.

**6. Forward models for perception and control.** An output-input model predicts the sensory effects of the system's own outputs. Used in perception to separate self-caused from external change and in control for state estimation. *Where it fails:* video prediction alone is not enough; planning models do not count as embodiment; and it is unclear whether a model is used for control or planning.

**7. Narrow formulation of indicators.** Rewriting externally-defined conditions (self-maintenance, embodiment) so they refer only to internal processing, to stay consistent with computational functionalism. *Where it fails:* the narrow version may be a different theory from the one that motivated it (my analysis).

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Phenomenal consciousness** - subjective experience; there is something it is like to be the system in that state. Used as the report's meaning of "consciousness".
- **Access consciousness** - information broadcast for free use in reasoning and control of action, including report. Conceptually distinct from phenomenal consciousness.
- **Computational functionalism** - the thesis that implementing computations of a certain kind is necessary and sufficient for consciousness, adopted as a working hypothesis.
- **Theory-heavy approach** - assessing AI by whether it has functions that scientific theories associate with consciousness, rather than by behavioural signatures.
- **Indicator properties** - the 14 properties derived from theories that make a system a better candidate for consciousness; a rubric, not a set of conditions.
- **Algorithmic recurrence** - repeated processing in a weight-shared network; contrasted with implementational recurrence (feedback loops in hardware).
- **Global workspace / global broadcast** - a limited-capacity shared space whose contents are made available to all specialised modules.
- **Ignition** - the sudden, non-linear onset of workspace activity by which a representation becomes globally broadcast.
- **Perceptual reality monitoring (PRM)** - a higher-order theory in which a monitoring mechanism tags reliable perceptual states as "real".
- **Assertoric force** - the feature of conscious experience that makes its contents seem accurate and hard to resist believing.
- **Quality space** - the similarity structure of phenomenal qualities, grounded in discriminations the system can make.
- **Attention schema** - a model of attention that the system uses to control attention; central to AST.
- **Forward model (output-input model)** - a model of how the system's outputs change its inputs; the basis of the embodiment indicator.
- **Contrastive analysis** - comparing conscious and unconscious conditions to find neural correlates of consciousness.
- **No-report paradigm** - an experimental design that avoids requiring reports in the main task.
- **Sentience** - used inconsistently; the report prefers "conscious" and, where it uses the term, means capacity for valenced (good or bad) experience.
- **Credence** - degree of belief; the report recommends thinking about AI consciousness in these terms.
- **Intentional stance** - Dennett's strategy of predicting behaviour by attributing beliefs and desires; a driver of over-attribution.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- **Authors and format:** 19 authors (Butlin and Long joint first authors); arXiv version 3, August 2023; about 88 pages. (Report data, from the source.)
- **Indicator count:** 14 indicators across six theories (2 RPT, 4 GWT, 4 HOT, 1 AST, 1 PP, 2 AE). (Report Table 1.)
- **Excluded theory:** integrated information theory, excluded because its standard form is incompatible with computational functionalism.
- **Timing of global workspace activity:** conscious perception associated with widespread, reverberant activity appearing around 250-300 ms after stimulus onset. (Cited by the report from Mashour et al. 2020.)
- **Monkey prefrontal evidence:** a binocular rivalry study (Panagiotaropoulos et al. 2012) decoded the presumed percept from prefrontal activity with monkeys viewing passively; a 2018 study (van Vugt et al.) found prefrontal activity present even in false alarms.
- **Higher-order evidence:** Lau and Passingham (2006) found two masking conditions with equal discrimination but different rates of reporting having seen the stimulus; the report presents this as a challenge that higher-order theorists raise against GWT.
- **Transformer limits:** the residual stream has the same dimensionality as the input, so the report questions whether it is a bottleneck; Transformers are not recurrent.
- **Wilterson and Graziano (2021):** a three-layer, 200-neurons-per-layer network that catches a falling ball; the attention-schema input improved learning even though the spotlight remained.
- **Virtual rodent (Merel et al. 2019):** 38 degrees of freedom; four tasks; LSTM actor-critic trained end-to-end.
- **AdA:** Transformer encoder over the past few hundred timesteps feeding an LSTM; meta-RL; adapts to new tasks at human timescales.
- **Deep networks and smoothness:** the authors note that deep networks have smooth representation spaces, one ingredient for HOT-4, and that some DNN representations resemble human visual dissimilarity structure (Kriegeskorte 2015), with later studies complicating that picture.
- **Case-study verdicts (section 3.2):** Transformer LLMs have only a relatively weak case for any GWT-derived indicator: it is questionable whether the residual stream is a bottleneck, and Transformers are not recurrent. Perceiver and Perceiver IO come closer (GWT-1, GWT-2 and part of GWT-4) but lack global broadcast. For embodiment, PaLM-E's policy unit is the best candidate, though both components imitate human behaviour; the virtual rodent's tasks may not have required a self-model; AdA is judged the most likely of the three to be embodied, though it is not physically embodied.
- **Over-attribution examples:** the Blake Lemoine case (2022) and companion chatbots such as Replika, cited as evidence that people attribute consciousness to language models.

## Critical perspective

*The following is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Date and currency.** The report is from August 2023. Its verdicts concern systems such as GPT-3/GPT-4, LaMDA, PaLM-E, AdA and the 2019-2021 Perceiver and virtual-rodent work. By October 2026 the frontier of AI systems has moved well beyond these, and the case studies are dated. The rubric's logic may still hold, but the verdicts should not be read as current. I cannot verify from the source which later assessments exist. As I recall, overlapping authors have since extended this line of work into AI welfare policy (for example, a 2024 report titled "Taking AI Welfare Seriously"), but learners should check that before relying on it.
- **The rubric is unweighted and partly trivial.** The authors deny that indicators are conditions and do not give a way to combine them into a probability. Several indicators (RPT-1, HOT-4, PP-1) are met by most current deep networks, so a checklist can be satisfied in ways that say little about experience. The report acknowledges this, but a learner should ask what it would take for an indicator to discriminate.
- **Gaming risk applies to indicators too.** The report worries that behavioural tests can be gamed. A system could be built to show indicator-like architecture (a workspace bottleneck, a second-order monitor) without the functions the theories care about. The narrow reformulations, which keep the rubric compatible with computational functionalism, could make this easier.
- **Working assumptions carry the argument.** The conditional conclusion about near-term conscious AI depends on a contested thesis that the authors themselves call "plausible" rather than established. If it is false, the indicators may be irrelevant. Seth, and the substrate-dependence view the report sets aside, press exactly on this point.
- **Theory selection is contestable.** Excluding IIT on compatibility grounds is a principled choice, but it means the rubric is conditional on a subset of theories. Adding a theory could change the list. The authors' promise that the list will change is honest, but it also means the rubric is not stable enough to settle disputes.
- **Evidence is contested within the theories.** The report acknowledges disputes over whether prefrontal activity is needed for consciousness (RPT versus GWT and HOT), and over whether reports confound these studies. Applying the theories to AI inherits these disagreements.
- **Strength of the moral framing.** The report's argument that under-attribution is a serious risk is strong; the argument for over-attribution risk is also reasonable. Both are in tension with commercial incentives, which the authors note only briefly. The report does not evaluate whether any moral obligations follow from an indicator being present, which it leaves to other work.
- **Other perspectives would push back.** Seth would say the report's indicators leave out what matters, namely that living systems are self-producing; Cao would say consciousness may depend on biological organisation that no computation captures; Chalmers' 2023 analysis would likely treat LLM features as weak but not decisive evidence; and Schneider's tests would treat behavioural evidence as necessary but not sufficient. The report engages Seth, Chalmers and Schneider, but not Cao, and does not settle the disagreement.

## Discussion questions

1. The Executive Summary says systems with more indicator properties are "more likely" to be conscious, but gives no way to combine them. How would you turn these 14 indicators into a credence for a specific system? What would be a reasonable weighting, and what would you need to know to justify it?
2. Computational functionalism is adopted "for pragmatic reasons". Is that a good enough justification for building a method on it? What would the method look like if you rejected the assumption, and which indicators would survive?
3. Choose one of the theories from Exercise 1 (for example, global workspace theory on p.22 or the computational higher-order theories on p.29). What is its core claim, what is the evidence for it in humans, and which indicator would you assess in a current system?
4. The report says a system could be built to satisfy the indicators without consciousness, and that behavioural tests can be gamed. How would you tell a system that has a workspace from one that only mimics one? What role should interpretability play?
5. Compare the report's account of agency and embodiment with Seth's biological view in the "Mythology" reading. Which parts of the disagreement are empirical, and which are about what counts as the relevant explanation?
6. The report separates consciousness from valenced experience and says the moral question may turn on sentience. Why might that matter for how we treat AI systems, and what would evidence of valenced experience look like under this framework?
7. The report argues that under-attribution and over-attribution are both costly. Which risk do you think is more pressing in 2026, and what evidence would change your mind?
8. If conscious AI is possible with current techniques, why might capability research produce conscious systems without anyone intending it? Does the report's answer on capabilities (Section 4.2) convince you?
