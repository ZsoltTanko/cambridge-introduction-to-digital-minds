# The Mythology of Conscious AI - Consciousness and Computation Section

**One-line takeaway:** Seth argues that computational functionalism (the idea that running the right computation is sufficient for consciousness) is a shaky assumption, and that life, metabolism and physical time are better candidates for what consciousness depends on.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 2.1 opens Unit 2 with three questions: what consciousness is and how well we understand it, what computational functionalism is and what speaks for and against it, and what makes consciousness hard to study in AI systems. Its stated outcomes include explaining computational functionalism and discussing arguments for and against it. Seth's essay is the course's main accessible "against" case, and it is marked Core.

The course guide says to read only sections 1 ("Brains are not computers"), 2 ("Other games in town") and 3 ("Life matters"), and to stop at section 4 ("Simulation is not instantiation"). The guide does not name a start point, so this summary treats the assigned span as beginning at the "Consciousness & Computation" heading, which sets up the assumption under attack and previews the four arguments. The essay's opening (on the cultural history of synthetic minds and on the psychological "temptations" of conscious AI) comes before that heading and is covered under "Beyond the assigned excerpt".

The course guide describes the essay as one that outlines and argues against computational functionalism and makes the case for a biological view of consciousness. Within the unit, the assigned part does three things. Section 1 attacks the brain-as-computer metaphor. Section 2 argues that computation is only one kind of dynamics. Section 3 gives the positive proposal that life matters. Section 4, which students skip, adds the simulation argument that connects most directly to uploading and digital minds.

The essay is paired with Exercise 2 (weigh one argument for and one against computational functionalism from cf-debate.com). Its biological-naturalism theme also connects to Exercise 1, where biological naturalism is one of the suggested theories. The unit's other Core readings (Butlin et al. and Schneider et al.) supply the theories and the more permissive side of the debate.

## Purpose of the reading

Seth, director of the Centre for Consciousness Science at the University of Sussex and author of *Being You*, writes for a general but serious audience. His aims are to show that the assumption that consciousness is computation is deeply ingrained and rarely examined, to argue that it is probably wrong, and to argue that the belief that AI is or will soon be conscious carries real ethical and psychological costs whatever the truth turns out to be.

The question the excerpt answers is whether real artificial consciousness is possible for the kinds of AI we have. Seth's answer is that it is very unlikely for standard digital computers and perhaps for AI in general, but that nothing he says rules out artificial consciousness altogether.

## Detailed summary

### Preamble: the assumption behind conscious AI

The idea of conscious AI rests on the claim that consciousness is a matter of computation: implementing the right kind of information processing is sufficient for consciousness. Seth calls this computational functionalism and argues that it is so ingrained it is often not recognised as an assumption. If it is wrong, real artificial consciousness is effectively ruled out, at least for familiar AI. He announces four arguments that undermine the idea that computation, as standard digital computers implement it, is sufficient for consciousness.

### Section 1: Brains are not computers

- **The metaphor.** Mind as software and brain as hardware is intuitive and productive. It has driven insights into brain function and most of today's AI.
- **Turing's contribution.** Turing's 1936 definition of computation, based on the Turing machine, and the idea of a universal machine that can simulate any other, underpins modern computing. Because computation is defined over abstract symbols, it is "substrate independent" in principle. Seth adds that in practice it is "substrate flexible": you cannot build a computer from any material, and cheese is a poor choice.
- **Neural networks.** McCulloch and Pitts showed that networks of highly simplified neurons can perform logical operations. Kleene later showed that such networks with tape-like memory are Turing complete. Today's artificial neural networks descend directly from this. Seth calls the result a marriage of convenience between mathematics and neuroscience: treat brains as networks of summing units and you inherit everything Turing computation offers.
- **Where it breaks.** In a computer, hardware and software are cleanly separated. In a brain there is no sharp line between "mindware" and "wetware". Brain activity runs across scales from cortical territories down to neurotransmitters, all tangled with metabolism. Each neuron is also autopoietic: it maintains and produces its own material basis. Seth notes that evolution had no obvious pressure to create the software-hardware separation, and that maintaining such a separation is energetically expensive, as server farms show.
- **Generative entrenchment.** Because of this integration, you cannot assume a biological neuron can be swapped for a silicon equivalent with its input-output behaviour preserved. Seth cites work by Chintaluri and Vogels showing that some neurons fire spikes apparently to clear metabolic waste. A silicon replacement would need a silicon metabolism, which he argues silicon cannot supply.
- **The neural replacement thought experiment.** Chalmers's thought experiment, in which brain parts are gradually replaced with functional silicon equivalents, is a staple of the case for substrate independence. Seth says it fails at the first step, because a perfect silicon replacement is impossible. Hinton has used the same argument to support the view that conscious AI already exists.
- **Time.** In "Turing world" only sequence matters: the same algorithm runs whether a step takes a microsecond or a million years. Brains are physically and continuously timed, and living systems must keep resisting entropy. Seth adds that computation exists outside time while computers do not, and that holding bits stable costs energy. Phenomenologists also describe conscious experience as a flowing stream rather than discrete states.
- **Closing move.** Citing Whitehead, Seth warns against confusing a metaphor with the thing itself. Viewing the brain through "Turing glasses" underestimates its biology and overestimates the substrate flexibility of what it does.

### Section 2: Other games in town

- **Limits of Turing computation.** Turing computations map finite strings of symbols to other strings, and only sequence matters. Turing himself identified non-computable functions such as the halting problem. Continuous and stochastic functions lie outside Turing's remit strictly speaking, and simulations can only approximate them. Seth says that a simulation of a property is not the same claim as the property being a Turing computation.
- **Biology is continuous and noisy.** Electromagnetic fields, neurotransmitter flux and other continuous, stochastic dynamics may play a role in consciousness. Seth thinks it presumptuous to assume that only Turing computations matter.
- **Older and alternative computing.** Early computers were analogue; the Antikythera mechanism is his example. Cybernetics, which emphasised control and regulation over symbol manipulation, was prominent at AI's birth in the 1950s. Neuromorphic computing uses detailed properties such as spike timing. Hinton's "mortal computation" stresses algorithms inseparable from their physical substrate, which, metaphorically, die when their particular implementation ceases to exist. These approaches are less substrate-flexible than standard digital computation.
- **The steam governor.** Van Gelder's example: a steam-engine governor regulates speed through physics, with spinning balls closing a valve. Describing it as a computer that senses, calculates and signals would be inefficient and would misdescribe what happens. Systems that do not need a computational description exist.
- **Dynamical and 4E approaches.** Dynamical systems theory and enactive, embodied, embedded and extended cognition reject the idea that mind can be fully captured algorithmically. They use attractors and phase spaces. Seth thinks it plausible that the brain processes needed for consciousness depend on such non-computational dynamics, or on a broader notion of computation.
- **Functionalism versus computational functionalism.** Seth keeps the general view that mind depends on the functional organisation of the embodied brain, which he says is compatible with his position. The confusion he identifies is between this liberal functionalism and the specific claim that the relevant organisation is Turing-style computation.
- **Conclusion of section.** The further one moves from Turing world, the more tied consciousness becomes to randomness, dynamics and a particular material substrate. The question changes from which algorithm produces consciousness to how brain-like a system has to be.

### Section 3: Life matters

- **The claim.** Seth calls this biological naturalism (a term from John Searle): properties of life are necessary, though perhaps not sufficient, for consciousness. He says plainly that he has no knock-down argument for it. His main reason is that every candidate for consciousness that most people agree is conscious is also alive.
- **Predictive processing.** Perception is a best guess. The brain predicts the causes of its sensory input and updates those predictions by minimising prediction error. Seth describes conscious perception as what he calls controlled hallucination: top-down predictions continually calibrated by bottom-up sensory signals. He illustrates this with a coffee cup (his Figure 4).
- **Self and body.** The same predictive machinery produces experiences of being a self: body ownership, and interoceptive inference about internal physiology. Emotion and mood differ from vision in being mainly about valence, whether things are going well or badly.
- **Control and life.** Prediction also supports control. The brain's primary duty is keeping its body alive by regulating quantities such as heart rate and blood oxygenation. Seth proposes that this regulatory role helps explain why embodied experience feels as it does.
- **Metabolism.** Prediction error minimisation, he argues, is inseparable from the material processes of metabolism and self-production. He claims a line can be drawn from autopoiesis to the Bayesian inference underpinning perception.
- **Three converging threads.** (1) There are glimmers of an explanatory link between life and conscious feelings such as emotion, mood and the basic feeling of being alive. (2) The predictive processes are rooted in biological nature. (3) The processes are continuous, dynamical and non-algorithmic, so they are likely inseparable from their material basis.
- **Overall picture.** We experience the world with, through and because of living bodies. Seth's rhetorical conclusion is that life, not information processing, may be what makes experience possible.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

- **Opening and "temptations" (before the assigned span).** Seth opens with the cultural history of artificial minds (Golem, Frankenstein, HAL 9000, Ex Machina, Klara and the Sun) and recounts Blake Lemoine's 2022 claim that Google's LaMDA was conscious. He argues that intelligence and consciousness are different: intelligence is the ability to achieve complex goals by flexible means, while consciousness is mostly about being. Three biases push us to bundle them: anthropocentrism, human exceptionalism and anthropomorphism. Language is especially persuasive. Seth argues that "hallucinate" is a misleading description of AI errors and that "confabulate" is better, since confabulation in humans is about making things up without noticing, which he treats as a matter of doing rather than experiencing. He also discusses exponential growth, where every point on the curve feels like an inflection point, and the techno-rapture fantasy of escaping the body into a silicon cloud.
- **Section 4: Simulation is not instantiation.** Simulating digestion produces no digestion, and simulating rain produces no wetness. Computation simulates X without instantiating X unless X is itself computational. Whole-brain emulation therefore presupposes computational functionalism. Seth argues that more detailed neuron-level models make this presupposition less natural, not more. He describes the mind-uploading hope as something of a contradiction: if the brain's detailed biophysics matters, you would be no more likely to exist in the simulation than a hailstorm is to arise inside a weather forecast computer. He then discusses Bostrom's simulation argument, which needs the assumption that a computer running a suitable program would be conscious. Seth says that assumption is taken without examination.
- **Summary.** Seth lists the social and cognitive biases, the four arguments and their cumulative force. He says each stands on its own, but together they make consciousness unlikely to come along as AI gets smarter. He again concedes that nothing rules out artificial consciousness altogether.
- **What (not) to do.** Seth argues that the fact of the matter matters because conscious beings have moral standing. He concedes he may be wrong. Biological naturalism is a minority view, and other theories generally assume computational functionalism rather than defend it. Deliberately creating conscious machines would be an ethical disaster, citing a proposed moratorium by Metzinger. He says the accidental emergence of consciousness in cerebral organoids worries him more than any LLM. Conscious-seeming systems are a more immediate problem. Seth uses the Müller-Lyer illusion to argue that the feeling of AI being conscious may be as impenetrable to thought as a visual illusion. He says no definitive tests exist, and that the Garland test in Ex Machina measures human persuadability rather than machine consciousness. He argues that our biases produce more false positives than false negatives, and that AI differs from animals in ways that matter, since AI resembles us in language but not in being alive.
- **Soul machine.** Seth ends by linking the cartoon of digital immortality to the Cartesian soul. He turns to the Greek *psychē* (breath) and to Ātman (pure witnessing awareness) and argues that what makes us who we are is an inchoate feeling of being alive. He closes by saying technology may make us forget what we know about life, and that it is time to remember.

## Techniques & methods explained

- **Substrate independence test.** Specify a function as an algorithm. Ask whether the function can be cleanly separated from its hardware. Seth's answer for brains is no, because the software-hardware separation is absent and expensive. *Where it fails:* it depends on how the function is specified. Functionalists can describe the relevant organisation at a level that does not mention metabolism. Seth's argument does not show why metabolic waste clearance belongs to the consciousness-relevant organisation.
- **Neural replacement thought experiment (as critiqued).** Replace one neuron, then another, with a functional equivalent. If the replacement is impossible, the argument has no support. *Where it fails:* Seth's critique shows the replacement is hard. It does not show that a partial replacement at the right functional grain must lose consciousness. Chalmers's argument has a different structure and does not require perfect metabolic mimicry.
- **Sequence-versus-time analysis ("Turing glasses").** Ask whether a process's properties depend only on the order of states or also on continuous, physical timing. Seth argues brains are timed, while algorithms are not. *Where it fails:* discrete-time simulations approximate continuous dynamics well enough for many engineering purposes, and the essay does not say how close an approximation would need to be.
- **Non-computational dynamical description.** The steam governor shows that a system can be described by physics without any computation. Seth uses this to suggest that some cognitive functions may be better captured by attractors and phase spaces. *Where it fails:* the essay describes these approaches as "plausible", not established, for consciousness.
- **Predictive processing as controlled hallucination.** Perception is top-down prediction corrected by sensory input, and interoceptive prediction links experience to regulation of the body. *Where it fails:* the essay presents predictive processing as the explanatory bridge to life, but does not show that the regulatory role requires living metabolism rather than some functional analogue.
- **Argument structure.** Four arguments, each meant to stand alone but reinforcing each other. *Where it fails:* the essay admits there is no knock-down case for the positive view in section 3.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Computational functionalism** - the claim that implementing the right computation is sufficient for consciousness, as used here.
- **Functionalism (in general)** - the view that mind depends on functional organisation; compatible with biological naturalism, and distinct from the computational version.
- **Substrate independence** - a function that can be realised in any material; in Turing computation, a property of algorithms in principle.
- **Substrate flexibility** - the more realistic point that computation needs some suitable material.
- **Universal Turing machine** - an abstract machine that can simulate any other Turing machine from a description of it.
- **Turing world** - Seth's name for a view that treats brains as abstract sequence-based algorithms.
- **Generative entrenchment** - the property of deep multiscale integration that prevents clean replacement of a part.
- **Autopoiesis** - self-production; the continuous regeneration of a living system's own material basis.
- **Biological naturalism** - Searle's term for the view that properties of life are necessary for consciousness.
- **Predictive processing** - the view that the brain continually minimises prediction error by updating predictions about the causes of sensory input.
- **Controlled hallucination** - Seth's phrase for perception as top-down prediction calibrated by sensory input.
- **Interoceptive inference** - inferences about the body's internal physiological state.
- **Valence** - the good-or-bad quality characteristic of emotion and mood.
- **Neuromorphic computing** - hardware that copies neural properties such as spike timing.
- **Mortal computation** - Hinton's idea of algorithms inseparable from their hardware.
- **4E cognition** - the embodied, embedded, enactive and extended approaches to mind.
- **Attractor / phase space** - concepts from dynamical systems used to describe continuous system behaviour.
- **Simulation vs instantiation** - a simulation of X does not make X unless X is itself computational (section 4).
- **Confabulation** - Seth's preferred term for AI errors, as opposed to "hallucination".

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

The essay is conceptual and argumentative. It reports no experiments of its own and gives almost no quantitative data. The concrete points are these.

- **Metabolic spiking (Chintaluri and Vogels, PNAS).** Some neurons fire spikes apparently to clear metabolic waste, so a silicon replacement would also need to replicate metabolism.
- **Neural networks and Turing completeness (McCulloch and Pitts; Kleene).** Simplified neuron networks can perform logic, and with tape-like memory they are Turing complete.
- **The steam governor (van Gelder).** A non-computational physical system regulates an engine. Seth uses it to illustrate a system that does not need a computational description.
- **Mortal computation (Hinton).** Seth presents it as a way to save energy by tying algorithms to their hardware.
- **Energy costs.** Server farms' energy use illustrates the cost of separating software from hardware. This is qualitative in the essay.
- **Lemoine and LaMDA (2022).** A Google engineer claimed a chatbot was conscious and was dismissed for breaching confidentiality. Seth uses it as an example of a conscious-seeming system already in public view.
- **Expert positions.** Chalmers thinks conscious machines may be possible in the not-too-distant future. Hinton thinks they already exist. Seth cites a 2024 group of researchers calling for attention to AI welfare. Seth says that many experts treat the question as one of when, not whether.
- **Antikythera mechanism.** Seth says it dates to around 2,000 BCE. See Critical perspective: this date appears to be wrong.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **The neural replacement objection may be aimed at the wrong target.** Seth shows that perfect replacement of a neuron requires a silicon metabolism. A functionalist can reply that the relevant organisation is the one that matters for consciousness. Waste clearance may not belong to it. Seth needs an argument that metabolic function is consciousness-relevant, and the essay does not supply one.
- **The brain-is-not-a-computer argument may attack a weak target.** Many philosophers who defend computational functionalism do not claim that brains are universal Turing machines. The claim is about the sufficiency of certain computations at some level of description. Seth's section 2 then concedes that broader kinds of computation are possible. If computation is defined broadly enough to include continuous and analogue processes, the dispute becomes partly verbal.
- **The life argument rests on a small sample.** The premise that all agreed conscious beings are alive is true, but it is a sample of one kind of system. It does not show that life is necessary. The essay admits this. The premise is also consistent with life being only the way consciousness has so far arisen.
- **Predictive processing does not force a biological conclusion.** Predictive processing is often implemented in artificial systems. The essay shows that prediction serves regulation in animals, but not that living metabolism is required for the same computational role. This is the gap the essay has to close and does not.
- **Rhetoric shapes the case.** The "hallucinate" versus "confabulate" point and the "Turing glasses" metaphor are persuasive as well as argumentative. The claim that human confabulation is about doing rather than experiencing is itself contested in the psychology literature.
- **Check the Antikythera date.** The essay dates the mechanism to around 2,000 BCE. My understanding is that it is generally dated to roughly the second or first century BCE. Verify this before relying on the chronology.
- **Energy and time arguments are weaker than they look.** The energy cost of digital computation is an engineering point. It does not bear directly on whether a system is conscious.
- **Timing of the source.** The essay is dated 14 January 2026, about nine months before today (8 October 2026). My knowledge runs only to June 2026, so I cannot say what has changed since then. As of my cutoff, the essay's reference points (Chalmers and Hinton's positions, the 2023 Butlin et al. report and the 2024 AI welfare call) remain central. The debate has moved toward indicator-based and theory-based assessments, which the essay engages only lightly. Check the course's later readings for the current state.
- **Where other course perspectives push back.** Chalmers would contest the replacement objection and the claim that current LLMs cannot be conscious in principle. Butlin et al. would contest the claim that other theories are merely assuming computational functionalism, since they propose indicator-based tests. Shiller and Cao give partial support to Seth but on different grounds.

3. Section 2 concedes that broader forms of computation exist. If "computation" is defined broadly enough to include continuous dynamics, is the disagreement with Seth still substantive?
