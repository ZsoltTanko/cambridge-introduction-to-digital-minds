## Purpose of the reading

The abstract states the authors' aims directly. There has been a "blossoming" of theories about the biological and physical basis of consciousness. Good theories guide empirical research, let us interpret data, suggest new experimental techniques, and let us manipulate the phenomenon. The authors argue that empirical discoveries only deliver a satisfying understanding when they are framed by a theory. The problem they identify is that it is unclear how current theories relate to each other, and whether they can be empirically distinguished at all.

The review is aimed at researchers and advanced students in consciousness science and philosophy of mind. It is written as a review article in a neuroscience journal rather than a philosophy paper, so its focus is on what each theory commits to neurobiologically and what data are offered for it.

The question it answers is: what are the main theoretical approaches to consciousness, what does each one propose to explain, what does it say about the brain, and how might empirical work tell them apart? Its concluding claim is optimistic: iterative development, testing and comparison of theories is expected to deepen understanding.

## Detailed summary

Because the body text is unavailable, this walk-through follows the abstract's own structure, the figure captions and the glossary. It does not reproduce the authors' section-by-section argument.

### 1. The four approaches reviewed

The review covers four prominent theoretical approaches:

- **Higher-order theories** (Figure 1 in the paper).
- **Global workspace theories** (Figure 2).
- **Integrated information theory** (Figure 3).
- **Re-entry and predictive processing theories** (Figure 4).

The figure captions are the only visible structural signal in the file. Each figure presumably summarises one theory's architecture, and the review's comparison is built around them.

### 2. The organising method

For each approach the abstract says the review does three things:

1. Identifies **which aspects of consciousness** the theory proposes to explain. Some theories aim at the presence of any experience at all, others at specific contents, and others at the reported or accessible contents.
2. States the theory's **neurobiological commitments**: what physical or neural features it treats as necessary or sufficient.
3. Identifies the **empirical data adduced in support**.

This three-part template is the review's main analytic device. It lets the authors compare theories that use very different vocabulary.

### 3. Testing theories against each other

The review considers "some prominent empirical debates" and asks how each could distinguish among the four theories. The glossary and bibliography point to what these debates include:

- **No-report paradigms**: experiments in which participants give no subjective or behavioural report, so that report-related processing is not confounded with the neural signatures of experience. The bibliography includes a no-report study of bistable perception (Brascamp et al., 2015) and a review arguing that no-report designs are needed to isolate the true neural correlates (Tsuchiya et al., 2015).
- **Adversarial collaborations**: projects in which proponents of rival theories jointly design an experiment and agree in advance how a result will favour one theory over another. The glossary defines this. The bibliography annotation for Melloni et al. (2021) says that paper sets out how an adversarial collaboration between integrated information and global workspace theories is planned to arbitrate between them.
- **Measures of brain complexity and integration**, such as the perturbational complexity index and related indices, used to tell conscious from unconscious states (Casali et al., 2013; Casarotto et al., 2016 in the bibliography).

### 4. Three requirements for maturing the field

The abstract says the review outlines "three ways" in which theories of consciousness need to be developed before they can support mature theory-testing. The body text that would name these three is not in the file, so I cannot report them accurately. I will not guess at them here.

### 5. Conclusion

The closing sentence of the abstract is optimistic. The authors argue there are good reasons to expect that iterative development, testing and comparison of theories will lead to a deeper understanding of consciousness, which they call "this most profound of mysteries".

## Techniques & methods explained

The review's methods are the theories it compares and the empirical methods it uses to test them. The file supports only a partial account of each, so I list what the source does establish and then add background, labelled as such.

**Theories as explanatory targets (from the abstract and glossary).** The review treats each theory as a claim about which neural or physical features make a state conscious. The glossary's distinction between *global states* (overall level of consciousness, linked to arousal and responsiveness) and *local states* (specific conscious contents such as one perception or one thought) is important: theories differ in which of these they try to explain.

**Background, not from the source's body text:**
- *Higher-order theories* hold that a mental state is conscious when there is a representation of that state, a meta-representation. The glossary defines meta-representation as a mental representation whose target is another mental representation. Some versions require the higher-order state to be conscious, others only that it exists.
- *Global workspace theories* hold that a state becomes conscious when it is broadcast widely across the brain, typically in fronto-parietal networks, making it available to many cognitive systems at once. The bibliography cites the global neuronal workspace work of Dehaene and colleagues.
- *Re-entry and predictive processing* hold that conscious experience depends on feedback (re-entrant) signals within sensory hierarchies, or on the brain's predictions about its inputs and the errors between prediction and input. The glossary defines interoceptive predictions as predictions about the causes of bodily sensations.
- *Integrated information theory* holds that consciousness is identical to integrated information in a physical system. The glossary defines Φ as the amount of information a system specifies that is irreducible to its parts, and a *complex* as a subset of a system that underpins a maximum of irreducible integrated information.

**Empirical methods (from the bibliography and glossary).** No-report paradigms, adversarial collaborations and complexity indices (see section 3 above). The bibliography also cites binocular rivalry, TMS-based work on re-entrant processing, and intracranial electrical stimulation.

**Where these methods fail (background).** No-report designs are hard to build, because removing report can also remove attention or task demands. Complexity indices depend on how complexity is measured. Adversarial collaborations require agreed predictions, and a null result may not decisively favour either theory.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

The abstract presents the review's position fairly directly. The table below separates the authors' stated claims from my own observations, which are labelled.

| Point | Source's view (abstract, glossary, annotations) | My view (labelled) |
|---|---|---|
| Value of theory | Theories guide research and make data interpretable; understanding requires a theory | Few would dispute this in principle, though whether any current theory is sufficiently precise is open |
| Relations between theories | Unclear how current theories relate to one another | This is a fair diagnosis. Theories often use different vocabularies |
| Empirical distinguishability | Unclear whether current theories can be empirically distinguished | Adversarial collaborations and no-report designs are the proposed remedies |
| Outlook | Iterative development and testing should deepen understanding | This is a programme, not a result. The optimism is a stated expectation, not a demonstrated outcome |

**Trade-offs the approach implies.** Reviewing four theories side by side makes comparison possible, but it also means each theory is presented in its strongest form for comparison purposes. The four-way split is a choice. Other taxonomies, such as the Landscape of Consciousness website (reading 13), survey hundreds of theories on a physicalist-to-nonphysicalist spectrum, which is a very different level of granularity.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs)** - the minimal set of neural events jointly sufficient for a conscious state, as defined in the glossary.
- **Explanatory gap intuitions** - the intuition that no physical or mechanistic explanation of consciousness could be fully satisfying.
- **Adversarial collaboration** - a joint experiment by proponents of rival theories, with agreed-in-advance criteria for which theory wins.
- **Global states** - the overall level of consciousness, linked to arousal and responsiveness.
- **Local states** - specific conscious contents such as a perception, emotion or thought, also called conscious contents.
- **Phenomenal character** - the experiential nature of a local state, such as the redness of a red experience, also called qualia.
- **Meta-representation** - a mental representation whose target is another mental representation.
- **No-report paradigm** - an experiment in which participants give no subjective or behavioural report.
- **Φ (phi)** - the information a system specifies that is irreducible to its parts; there are many variants.
- **Complex (IIT)** - the subset of a physical system that underpins a maximum of irreducible integrated information.
- **Posterior hot zone** - rear cortical regions (parietal, temporal, occipital, and precuneus) that some theories implicate in conscious experience.
- **Interoceptive predictions** - predictions about the causes of bodily sensory signals.
- **Unity of consciousness** - the fact that the experiences of a single agent at one time seem to form one complex experience.
- **Cognitive access** - the functional property whereby a mental state is available to many cognitive processes, usually including report.
- **Computational (neuro)phenomenology** - using computational models to account for the phenomenal character of a state in terms of neural mechanisms.
- **Measurement problem** - the problem of determining whether a given mental state, organism or system is conscious.
- **Cerebral organoids** - lab-grown neural structures resembling aspects of the developing human brain, which the bibliography links to the question of whether isolated neural systems could be conscious.

- **Melloni et al. (2021), ref 17:** sets out an adversarial collaboration to arbitrate between integrated information and global workspace theories.
- **Lau and Rosenthal (2011), ref 30:** a summary of empirical evidence favouring higher-order theories.

- **Tononi et al. (2016), ref 59:** an account of the core claims and concepts of integrated information theory.
- **Yaron et al. (2022), ref 126:** the ConTraSt online database, which organises empirical studies by the theory they test.

- **Sergent et al. (2013), ref 122:** conscious perception of a stimulus can be influenced by events hundreds of milliseconds after it appeared ("retro-perception").
- **Casali et al. (2013), ref 158:** a complexity measure of the cortical response to transcranial magnetic stimulation separates global conscious states, including disorders of consciousness.
- **Bayne, Hohwy and Owen (2016), ref 21:** argues against a single dimension of "level of consciousness" in favour of a multidimensional account.
- **Bayne, Seth and Massimini (2020), ref 136:** examines whether consciousness is possible in isolated neural systems such as brain organoids, disconnected hemispheres and ex-cranial brains.
- **Dehaene, Lau and Kouider (2017), ref 137:** a Science article on what consciousness is and whether machines could have it. This is directly relevant to the course.
- **Chalmers (1995), ref 164:** the classic statement of the hard and easy problems.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Dated but still central.** The review is from May 2022, so it is about four and a half years old as of October 2026. The four-theory taxonomy has not been replaced, but the empirical landscape has moved on. The most important development I am aware of is the large adversarial collaboration between global workspace and integrated information theories that the Melloni et al. paper planned. I believe its results were published in 2025, but I have not verified this and I have not read the paper, so please check the primary source before relying on it.
- **The body text is unavailable to me.** Because I could only read the abstract and annotations, I cannot say how the authors handle objections to each theory. Claims about their treatment of counter-evidence should be checked against the full article.
- **Abstract-level concerns.** The abstract's central worry, that theories are hard to relate and hard to distinguish empirically, is a real one. It is also a stance: critics of the field would say the taxonomy itself builds in assumptions about what consciousness is.
- **Who would push back.** Illusionists such as Dennett (ref 14) and Frankish (ref 15) reject the phenomenal assumptions that these theories take for granted. Functionalists such as Chalmers would resist the implicit claim that neurobiology is the right level of explanation for AI questions. Seth's own later work in the course leans against computational functionalism, which the course treats as a debate rather than a settled point.
- **Relevance to digital minds.** These theories were built to explain human and animal consciousness. Whether they transfer to artificial systems depends on whether their neurobiological commitments are essential or only contingent. The review does not settle that question, and the course's Unit 2 is designed to leave it open.
