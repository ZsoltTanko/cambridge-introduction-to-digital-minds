## Detailed summary

### Section 1: Introduction (assigned)

The guide says the introduction is about **problems in studying consciousness**. The unit page names three that the course wants learners to know:

- **The hard problem of consciousness.** Explaining the functions and mechanisms of cognition does not, on its own, explain why there is subjective experience at all.
- **The problem of other minds.** We cannot observe another being's experiences directly. We infer them from behaviour and structure, and that inference is the central difficulty for AI.
- **The gaming problem.** A system can be built or trained to produce behaviour that looks like evidence of consciousness without having the underlying experience. Any test based on behaviour is therefore open to being gamed.

### Section 2: AI Consciousness and the Philosophy of Mind (assigned)

The guide describes this section as the link from general philosophy of mind to AI. The course's own unit questions point to two topics here:

- **Computational functionalism** is the view that mental states are individuated by their computational or functional organisation, so that a system implementing the right computations could be conscious whatever it is made of. The unit page asks learners to evaluate arguments for and against it.
- **Potential application to digital minds.** The section asks what follows if the functionalist view is true, or if it is false, for the question of whether an AI system can have experiences.

## Techniques & methods explained

The reading is conceptual, not empirical, so the course-relevant methods are conceptual tools:

- **Separating the hard problem from functional explanation.** Learners should be able to say what a functional or mechanistic account explains and what it leaves open. This is the lens the unit uses throughout.
- **Inferring minds from behaviour (the problem of other minds).** The method is to ask what evidence could support an attribution of consciousness, and whether behaviour alone can do that.
- **Testing for gaming.** The gaming problem asks whether a test can be passed by a system that lacks the property the test is meant to detect. It is a check on whether indicators are reliable.

Where these methods fail, according to the framing the course gives, is where the inference runs from behaviour or structure to experience. The guide gives no further detail on failure modes, so the PDF must be checked.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Hard problem of consciousness** - why physical or functional processes come with subjective experience at all, as the unit page frames it.
- **Problem of other minds** - the difficulty of knowing another being's experiences directly, which applies to AI as much as to other humans.
- **Gaming problem** - the risk that a system passes a consciousness-related test without having the underlying property.
- **Computational functionalism** - the view that mental states are fixed by computational or functional organisation and not by physical substrate.
- **Digital mind** - a possibly conscious AI system (the course uses this term for the object of study).
- **AI consciousness** - whether artificial systems have subjective experience.

## Discussion questions

1. Why does the hard problem of consciousness matter for deciding whether an AI system is conscious, even when its behaviour is sophisticated? Could an answer to the hard problem ever be reached through functional analysis?
2. How does the problem of other minds change when the other mind is built by humans and its architecture is fully known? Does full architectural knowledge help or hurt the inference?
3. What is the gaming problem, and why is it a particular concern for behavioural tests of AI consciousness? Can you name a test that resists it?
