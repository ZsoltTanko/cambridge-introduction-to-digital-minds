# Phenomenal Consciousness, Defined and Defended as Innocently as I Can Manage

**One-line takeaway:** You can pin down what phenomenal consciousness is without committing to dualism, infallibility, privacy or any other contested theory, by teaching the concept through positive and negative examples and relying on the single obvious feature they share.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 2.1 ("Theories of Consciousness") is the unit's core question: what consciousness is, what theories of it exist, and whether any of them can be applied to AI. Its page asks learners to grasp the hard problem, the problem of other minds and the "gaming" problem, to explain computational functionalism, and to weigh arguments for and against it. The section's readings move from conceptual groundwork (the glossary, the Saad and Mogenson primer, the Schneider et al. primer) toward specific theories (Butlin et al., Seth, Chalmers, Seth and Bayne) and toward applications to LLMs and digital minds.

The course guide for this reading is short: it describes the paper as "an attempt to define phenomenal consciousness in a way that is clear, substantive, and innocent of problematic philosophical assumptions." The course does not give a "read only" instruction, so the whole paper (about 4,600 words) is the assignment. It is marked **Further**, so it is not required for the section's core readings, but it supplies the definitional vocabulary that the section keeps using.

Its role in the section's argument is to fix the target. Almost every later reading assumes some notion of phenomenal consciousness, usually without defining it. Schwitzgebel's paper makes an explicit choice about what the word should pick out before any theory is tested. That matters for digital minds, because if the target is defined with built-in commitments (for instance, that consciousness requires biology, or that it requires a global workspace), then any verdict about AI is already settled by the definition. The paper is therefore a reference point for judging the theory readings: when a theory claims that some system is conscious, the learner can ask whether that claim relies on the innocent concept or a loaded one.

## Purpose of the reading

Schwitzgebel is responding to Keith Frankish's illusionism, the view that phenomenal consciousness does not really exist. He is writing as a commentator, and his aim is narrower than defending any particular theory of consciousness. He wants to show that a philosopher can say phenomenal consciousness exists, meaning the experiences we have, without signing up for contested metaphysics or epistemology.

The question the paper answers is: what is the least committal concept of phenomenal consciousness that is still substantive enough to be worth studying? It is written for philosophers of mind and consciousness researchers, and its method (definition by example) is meant to be usable by anyone who can recognize experiences in themselves.

## Detailed summary

### 1. Introduction: Frankish's dilemma

Frankish offers what Schwitzgebel reads as a dilemma for "phenomenal realists," people who believe phenomenal consciousness exists:

- **Horn 1, inflation.** If phenomenal consciousness is defined with dubious commitments such as infallible access to it, metaphysical dualism, or other contested features, it probably does not exist.
- **Horn 2, deflation.** If it is defined so weakly that it collapses into dispositions to make judgments, into Block's "access consciousness," or into Chalmers' "easy problems," it has effectively vanished.

Schwitzgebel's answer is to give a clear definition that picks out a phenomenon that is substantively interesting but free of the problematic assumptions. He adds a condition he calls **wonderfulness**: the phenomenon must keep a superficial air of mystery. If reducing it to physical or functional terms is possible, that reduction should take real work, not be obvious from the definition. We should be able to wonder how consciousness could arise from functional mechanisms and matter in motion.

### 2. Defining consciousness by example

Schwitzgebel first rules out three standard routes:

- **Analytic definition** (decomposition into component concepts). Consciousness is treated as foundationally simple, and any proposed decomposition would beg the question against researchers who reject it.
- **Functional definition** (by the causal role it plays). Its causal role is too contested. Compare defining a heart by its function of pumping blood, which is fine only because the function is uncontroversial.
- **Synonymy** (with terms like "qualia," "what-it's-like-ness," or "stream of experience"). These suffer the same inflation-or-deflation problem as the target.

What remains is **definition by example**. It works when examples are diverse, include near-miss counterexamples, and point to a concept the audience will naturally latch onto. The furniture example illustrates this: tables and chairs are in, pictures and doors are out, and a ballpoint pen is a borderline case. Schwitzgebel stresses a background condition: there must be one obvious or readily adopted category that fits the examples. Otherwise the examples are ambiguous. He concedes that this assumption needs defending, and says he will return to it in Section 3.

**Positive examples** (experiences that are phenomenally conscious):
- Sensory and somatic experience: seeing the text on the page, hearing a fan, tasting a drink, the pain of a headache, proprioception of limb position. He notes that pressing the palms against closed eyes produces phosphenes, which are also experiences.
- Conscious imagery: visualizing the Eiffel Tower, imagining a tune, imagining a motor action.
- Emotional experience: sudden fear on a road, vivid joy, surprise, anger.
- Thinking and desiring that you can vividly recall, such as thinking "what a jerk!" or craving dessert. He allows that not all thought is conscious in this sense.
- Dream experience, which is conscious even though we are not conscious in the everyday sense while dreaming.
- Other people's experiences, which we normally assume are similar to ours.

**Negative examples** (things that are not phenomenally conscious):
- Growth of fingernails, absorption of lipids in the intestine, release of growth hormone.
- Dispositional knowledge, such as the disposition to answer "twenty-four" to "six times four" before the question is consciously considered.
- Standing intentions, such as planning to stop for lunch at 11:45.
- Early auditory processing structures.
- The visual effect of a briefly flashed, quickly masked display, which still influences behavior.
- Parts of the environment you are not sensing, such as the world behind your head.
- Dreamless sleep, which is normally taken to involve complete absence of consciousness.

Schwitzgebel's central claim is that **the feature all positive examples share and the negative examples lack is the most obvious one**: they are *conscious experiences*. He does not analyze it further. He compares this to knowing what furniture is without being able to analyze the concept. The analysis, reduction and metaphysics can come later.

He warns the reader not to be clever. Many candidate features fit the examples but are not the target. Examples include a gerrymandered "Cambridge property" such as being conscious and within 30 miles of Earth's surface, scientifically constructed features such as access to a "central workspace" or in-principle reportability, and Frankish's own candidates such as "quasi-phenomenality" or the disposition to judge that one has wonderful experiences. None of these is the intended feature.

### 3. Contentious cases and wonderfulness

Schwitzgebel addresses the worry that the single category may not be obvious after all. Researchers disagree about whether consciousness outruns attention (for example, whether you constantly feel your feet in your shoes), whether it is limited to sensory, imagery and emotional kinds, whether it includes imageless thought or a sense of self or agency, and whether it always involves reflective self-awareness.

His strategy is to exclude these contentious cases from the examples, so the definition does not settle them. He concedes that there may be two natural concepts: the broad "phenomenal consciousness" and a narrower "phenomenal-consciousness-along-with-attention," both of which fit the examples. He chooses the less committal one, arguing that it is probably the more obvious concept anyway. The same holds for organisms: whether snails are conscious should be left open, rather than defined as "sensory sensitivity" or as "processes reportable via introspection."

This openness is what makes the wonderfulness condition work. We can wonder about the relation between consciousness and reportability, sensory sensitivity or any functional process. Wonder does not require an insurmountable epistemic gap, only "a moment's epistemic breathing space." He illustrates this with a quadratic equation with no real roots: one can wonder where it crosses the x-axis before doing the calculation, even though the answer is impossible.

Schwitzgebel closes with his bottom line: a single folk-psychologically obvious concept, perhaps blurry at the edges, fits the examples and leaves the contentious cases open.

### 4. Problematic assumptions

The final section checks whether the definition is innocent. Schwitzgebel disavows the commitments Frankish lists. Phenomenal experiences need not be simple, ineffable, intrinsic, private or immediately apprehended. They need not have non-physical properties, be inaccessible to third-person science, or be inexplicable in physical terms. He thinks these claims are probably false as universal generalizations. He allows that a feature present in all the examples may be indirectly built into the definition, but says the troubling commitments are the inflationary ones absent from the target states.

He admits three commitments:
1. **A folk-psychological commitment:** there is a single obvious folk concept that matches the examples.
2. **Realism:** that concept is not empty, broken or a hodgepodge, but picks out a feature most positive examples share and negative examples lack. He notes a panpsychist could say the negative examples are falsely assumed negative, in which case the concept can still be salvaged.
3. **A mild epistemic commitment near non-physicality:** the relation between consciousness and functional or physical processes is not straightforwardly obvious from the definition. This is compatible with a later argument showing that consciousness is reducible.

He argues that Frankish's quasi-phenomenality fails the wonderfulness condition, because it leaves no room to wonder whether phenomenal consciousness exists without the disposition to judge it. Schwitzgebel does wonder about this, for instance whether snails are conscious without judging that they are, or whether unattended fleeting experiences can exist without judgment, and even whether a group such as the United States could have group-level experience.

He ends with a pink analogy. You can teach the concept "pink" by showing many pink things and non-pink near-misses without giving an account of the metaphysics of color. Phenomenal consciousness, he suggests, can be taught the same way. He states that if he had to bet, he would bet that consciousness is mundane, but he does not build that bet into the concept.

## Techniques & methods explained

**Definition by example.** Give diverse positive instances (things that clearly belong to the category) and negative instances (things that clearly do not), including borderline and contentious cases where needed. It works if the category is natural and obvious to the audience. Steps:
1. Gather clear positive instances from diverse domains (sensory, imagery, emotion, thought, dreams, other minds).
2. Gather clear negative instances that are close enough to test the boundary (automatic processes, dispositions, standing intentions, masked stimuli).
3. Check that one obvious feature separates the two sets.
4. Resist adding analysis or contentious commitments.
5. Leave contentious cases open rather than resolving them by definition.

*Where it fails:* if no obvious category exists, if the audience invents a non-obvious one that fits the examples, or if the instances are too contentious to settle the category. Schwitzgebel acknowledges all three.

**Method of avoiding inflation and deflation.** Use the examples to block both horns of Frankish's dilemma. The definition should be neither so loaded it cannot be true nor so light it turns into a functional or physical concept.

**Wonderfulness test.** A conceptual check. A definition passes if the relation between the phenomenon and physical or functional processes remains an open question for a while after reading the definition. It fails if the definition already makes the relation obvious.

**Method of wondering about edge cases.** Use the open definition to ask whether snails, unattended experiences, or group entities are conscious. The definition is supposed to make these questions answerable by evidence, not by stipulation.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Aspect | Schwitzgebel's view (as presented) |
|---|---|
| Strength | Gives a usable definition without dualism, infallibility, privacy or ineffability |
| Strength | Keeps consciousness open to empirical inquiry (the wonderfulness condition) |
| Strength | Defends realism against illusionist deflation |
| Limitation | Depends on a single obvious folk concept; contentious cases are left deliberately open |
| Limitation | Definition by example needs the audience to latch on; success is partly sociological |
| Trade-off | Neutral on contested cases (attention, snails, self-awareness) versus giving a sharper target |
| Trade-off | Admits a folk-psychological and epistemic commitment, which he thinks is minor |

Schwitzgebel's own view of these trade-offs: the minimal commitments are acceptable and the openness is the feature, not a bug. The paper does not claim the definition settles any theory of consciousness.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Phenomenal consciousness** - the feature of experiences that makes there be "something it is like" to have them, as used here; not analysed further.
- **Phenomenal realism** - the view that phenomenal consciousness exists, as opposed to illusionism (Frankish) that denies it.
- **Frankish's dilemma** - the choice between inflated and deflated conceptions of phenomenal consciousness.
- **Horn 1 (inflation)** - defining consciousness with dubious commitments (infallibility, dualism, privacy and similar).
- **Horn 2 (deflation)** - defining consciousness so weakly that it becomes dispositions, access consciousness or easy problems.
- **Definition by example** - defining a concept by diverse positive and negative instances plus a natural category the audience latches onto.
- **Wonderfulness condition** - the requirement that the phenomenon keep a superficial air of mystery, so reduction takes work.
- **Access consciousness** - Block's notion of information available for reasoning and report; treated here as a deflationary target.
- **Easy problems** - Chalmers' term for functional questions about cognition, which Schwitzgebel says should not exhaust consciousness.
- **Phosphenes** - visual experiences of light and colour produced by pressure on closed eyes; used as a positive example.
- **Masked display** - a briefly shown visual stimulus followed by a mask, which influences behaviour without being consciously seen; a negative example.
- **Cambridge property** - a gerrymandered feature defined by relations to other things (for example, within 30 miles of Earth's surface), ruled out as the intended concept.
- **Quasi-phenomenality** - Frankish's candidate feature defined by dispositions to make phenomenal judgments; rejected here as failing wonderfulness.
- **Phenomenal-consciousness-along-with-attention** - the narrower concept that includes only attended experience, left open as an alternative.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

Schwitzgebel gives no experiments or statistics. The evidence is introspective examples that the reader is invited to check in their own experience. Points worth remembering:

- Pressing the palms against closed eyes produces phosphenes, which he uses as an example of visual experience that is conscious even without external light.
- Many automatic processes are negative examples: nail growth, lipid absorption, growth hormone release, and early auditory processing.
- Dispositional knowledge (the answer to "six times four") and standing intentions (lunch at 11:45) count as mental but not phenomenally conscious until they are actively experienced.
- The masked display example shows stimuli can drive behaviour while not being experienced.
- Schwitzgebel cites his own earlier work (Schwitzgebel 2011, *Perplexities of Consciousness*) to argue that confidence about introspective contentious features is "often misplaced."

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **The "obvious feature" is doing a lot of work.** Schwitzgebel relies on one feature that "smacks you in the face." That is an introspective appeal, and the paper's own discussion of introspective confidence (citing his 2011 book) suggests such appeals can be unreliable. Frankish would likely reply that the apparent obviousness is itself a product of theory.
- **Definition by example strains for AI.** The method works for pink, rectangles and furniture because we share reliable intuitions about the examples. For AI systems, we have no agreed positive examples and no shared intuitions about the borderline cases. The "wonderfulness" guard is useful, but it does not tell us how to decide the AI cases.
- **Does the definition really avoid commitments?** Schwitzgebel admits an epistemic commitment and a folk-psychological commitment. A critic could argue that choosing the less committal concept (leaving attention and snails open) is itself a substantive position, since it counts certain organisms and states as candidates.
- **Post-2016 developments.** The paper predates the rapid progress in large language models, the 2023 Butlin et al. indicator report, and the recent growth of consciousness-in-AI research. Its core distinctions still hold, but the stakes of the negative cases (such as dispositions and standing intentions, which AI systems have in abundance) are now sharper. Today, October 2026, the paper is best read as groundwork rather than a live position in the AI debate. I have not checked for specific later replies to Frankish's illusionism and would not vouch for any particular response without looking it up.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** Seth and the biological naturalists would say the definition is too thin: if consciousness depends on living processes, a neutral concept cannot capture that. Chalmers would largely accept the paper, since he shares the target, but would disagree that the question for LLMs is closed by the definition. Butlin et al. would find the definition useful for indicator-based tests, but would need to say which theory's indicators track the concept.
- **Source-quality note.** The fetched text has encoding artefacts (apostrophes and dashes appear as replacement characters). They do not affect the argument.

## Discussion questions

1. Schwitzgebel says the definition works only if there is one obvious category that learners latch onto. Is that true for a concept like phenomenal consciousness, and how would you test whether two people are latching onto the same thing?
2. Pick a negative example from the paper (for instance, dispositional knowledge or a standing intention). Could an AI system have states of that kind without any phenomenal consciousness? What would it take to decide?
3. Schwitzgebel's wonderfulness condition requires that the link between consciousness and physical or functional processes stay open for a while. Does this condition favour or disfavour computational functionalism, and why?
4. The paper avoids defining consciousness by whether it is reportable or attended. Why might that choice matter for judging whether a language model is conscious?
5. Use the Exercise 1 theory you chose (for example, global workspace or biological naturalism). Does the theory's claim rely on the innocent concept, or does it build in a commitment that Schwitzgebel would reject?
6. Frankish's illusionism says phenomenal consciousness does not exist. What would an illusionist say about the negative examples, and does Schwitzgebel's "obvious feature" appeal beat the illusionist's explanation of the same intuitions?
7. Schwitzgebel gives a pink analogy. Where does the analogy hold for consciousness, and where does it break down when applied to digital minds?
