# Unit 2 Glossary

**One-line takeaway:** Seven working definitions (consciousness, access consciousness, functionalism, computational functionalism, qualia, substrate independence, philosophical zombie) that the rest of Unit 2 assumes you can use without stopping to define them.

## Detailed summary

The glossary is short and has seven entries. The order below follows the document.

**Conscious / consciousness.** Something is conscious if there is "something it is like" to be it (crediting Nagel, 1974). By default the course uses "conscious" to mean phenomenal consciousness, i.e. subjective experience. It is explicitly not about intelligence, self-concept, or related notions: the question is whether there is experience at all. Consciousness is what disappears in deep, dreamless sleep, though the entry notes that it is debated whether consciousness is strictly on/off or has borderline cases. The entry stresses that consciousness and intelligence come apart in both directions (some animals may be conscious without much intelligence; an intelligent system need not be conscious), and that an experience need not be emotional or involve a sense of self.

**Access consciousness.** States or contents of mind that are available for report, reasoning and control, without necessarily being felt. This contrasts with phenomenal consciousness, and the entry notes that how the two interact is debated. Availability for report, reasoning or control is the hallmark; mere influence on behaviour does not count. The entry points to Ned Block for the distinction.

**Functionalism.** The view that consciousness depends on functional organisation: the relations between a system's possible internal states, inputs and outputs. Roughly, what matters is what a system does rather than what it is made of (crediting Putnam, 1975). The entry draws the consequence that systems with the same functional organisation have the same experiences, whatever their substrate.

**Functionalism, computational.** A narrower version: the functional organisation that matters for mind and consciousness is computational. Consciousness depends only on the manipulation of information by an algorithm, whether the implementing system is neurons, silicon or something else (crediting Putnam, 1988, and Shagir, 2010). The entry concedes that there is debate about which kind of computation is meant, and points to Anil Seth's treatment of this.

**Qualia.** The introspectively accessible phenomenal aspects of mental states, the felt character of experience (examples: the redness of red, the sting of pain). In standard usage, qualia name felt qualities, not their functional or representational roles. The entry refers to the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy for more.

**Substrate independence.** The claim that the same kinds of mental states, conscious ones included, can occur in systems with a wide range of physical substrates (citing Seth, 2025). The entry notes that the same idea is sometimes called "substrate flexibility" to stress that conscious states are not necessarily tied to carbon-based biology (citing Kirkpatrick, 2022).

**Zombie, philosophical.** A thought experiment: a being that behaves exactly like a human but has no subjective experience.

## How the terms fit together

The entries form a chain. Consciousness is defined as phenomenal (felt) experience. Qualia are the felt contents of that experience. Access consciousness is a different notion, defined by availability for report and control, and it is introduced so that learners do not confuse being reportable with being felt. Functionalism is the general thesis that the relevant feature of a system is its functional organisation. Computational functionalism narrows that to computation. Substrate independence is, on the summarizer's reading, the consequence that functionalism is usually taken to support: the same mental states can appear in silicon as in neurons. The philosophical zombie is the thought experiment that tests whether phenomenal experience can be separated from behaviour and function at all. On the summarizer's reading, in the section's debates the zombie is what a functionalist must deny is conceivable, or conceive of as a case where function is present without experience.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Phenomenal consciousness** - subjective experience; there is something it is like to be the system. The course's default meaning of "conscious."
- **Access consciousness** - content available for report, reasoning and control, not necessarily felt. Contrasted with phenomenal consciousness (Block).
- **Functionalism** - consciousness depends on functional organisation (input-state-output relations), not on substrate.
- **Computational functionalism** - the functional organisation that matters is computational; consciousness depends only on algorithmic information processing.
- **Qualia** - the introspectable felt qualities of experience, distinct from functional or representational roles.
- **Substrate independence** (substrate flexibility) - the same mental states can occur on different physical substrates.
- **Philosophical zombie** - a thought-experiment being behaviourally identical to a human but without experience.
- **Something it is like** - Nagel's formulation of what makes a state conscious.
- **Phenomenal vs access (report-based) consciousness** - a distinction whose interaction is debated.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the course's.*

- **The functionalism definition is stronger than it looks.** The entry says systems with the same functional organisation have the same experiences. That is the strong form of functionalism, and many functionalists would accept only a weaker claim (for example, that functional organisation is necessary, not sufficient, or that finer-grained implementation matters). Shiller's paper and Cao's challenge both press on this.
- **"Substrate independence" and "substrate flexibility" are treated as synonyms.** Some authors distinguish them: independence says mental states could occur on any substrate, flexibility says they are not tied to biology. The glossary's merging is convenient but glosses over a real difference, which matters when the question is whether silicon can be conscious and not merely whether biology is special.
- **The access definition is a paraphrase, not a neutral one.** Block's distinction is contested. Some theorists think access and phenomenal consciousness are not separable in the way the entry implies. Treating them as two definitions makes the separation look more settled than the glossary's own "debate" caveat suggests.
- **"Qualia" is narrowed on purpose.** Restricting the word to felt qualities keeps representational theories from being counted as accounts of qualia. Some theorists would object that this bakes in one side of the phenomenal-versus-representational debate.
- **Zombies cut both ways.** The thought experiment is used against physicalism and, in the Chalmers papers, to test functionalism. The entry does not say which way it cuts, which leaves the learner to supply the argument.
- **Dating.** The glossary is dated April 2026 and the course cites 2025 and 2026 sources. As of October 2026 the concepts are standard, but the debate over which computation is meant has moved since the older citations (Putnam 1988, Shagrir 2010), and learners should check the sibling readings for the newest positions.
- **Other perspectives in the course would push back.** Seth (biological naturalism) would say substrate independence is the claim to reject, not a neutral definition. Cao would say multiple realizability is overstated. Chalmers would say the glossary's zombie entry is the right test, and that the functionalist can accept the logical possibility of zombies while denying their nomological possibility.

## Discussion questions

1. The glossary says consciousness is absent in dreamless sleep. What would you need to show for something else to count as a case of consciousness going away, and does the definition help you decide?
2. Why does the course insist that phenomenal consciousness is different from intelligence and self-modelling? Give an example of a system that has one without the other.
3. Access consciousness is defined by availability for report and control. If an AI system reports its internal states, what would tell you that the report reflects a phenomenal experience and not just access?
4. Write the strongest version of the functionalist claim the glossary states, then the strongest objection to it. Which of the siblings (Cao, Shiller, Seth) is closest to your objection?
5. Is "substrate independence" the same claim as "substrate flexibility"? If they come apart, which one do Butlin et al. or Chalmers actually need, and why?
6. Exercise 2 asks for an argument for and against computational functionalism. Using only the glossary's definitions, can you state what the computational version adds to plain functionalism?
7. Is a philosophical zombie conceivable, and if so does that show anything about what consciousness is? How would a functionalist answer, and what would a biological naturalist say?
