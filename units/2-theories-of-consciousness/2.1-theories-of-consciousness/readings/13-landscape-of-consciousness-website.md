# Landscape of Consciousness Website

**One-line takeaway:** A searchable, theorist-curated map of hundreds of theories of consciousness, grouped into broad families from materialism to idealism, useful for finding a theory to study for Exercise 1 and for seeing how many live options there are.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 2.1 asks three questions: what consciousness is and where our understanding stands, what computational functionalism claims and how it is argued for and against, and what makes studying consciousness hard, especially in AI systems. The section's learning goals include discussing leading theories of consciousness and how they might apply to digital minds. The readings are sequenced to get from definitions (the Unit 2 Glossary, the Saad and Mogenson primer) to specific theories (Butlin et al., Seth and Bayne, Schiavo) and then to sceptical and alternative positions (Cao, Shiller, Schwitzgebel, Chalmers).

The course guide for this reading is short. It describes the site as surveying over 350 theories of consciousness, arranged on a roughly physicalist-to-nonphysicalist spectrum. The guide does not tell learners what to take from it, and the page gives no "read only" restriction, so the whole site is the assignment in the loose sense.

Its priority is **Further**, not Core. It sits at the end of the section's reading list, after the technical papers. Its role is orientation: it shows the scale and variety of the field that the Core readings are only sampling. It is the natural place to go when you need to pick a theory for Exercise 1, which asks you to spend 20 minutes on one leading theory or related condition and note its claims, evidence and relevance to digital minds. The map is not itself evidence for any theory, so it does not settle the question the unit asks.

Important: the page is an interactive map. The fetched copy contains category and theorist labels, not the per-theory summaries the site offers when you click through. The summary below therefore describes what the map shows, not what each entry argues.

## Purpose of the reading

The site is a reference resource built by Closer to Truth, a media project that runs interviews and essays on big questions in science and philosophy. It was created by Robert Lawrence Kuhn and Àlex Gómez-Marín. Its stated aim is to be a comprehensive resource for theories of consciousness, with the entries checked by the theorists themselves and kept up to date. It is open to any professional consciousness community worldwide.

It answers a practical question for a student or researcher: what positions exist on consciousness, and how do they relate? It also flags the implications that people often care about most: meaning and purpose, AI consciousness, virtual immortality and life after death. Its target audience is researchers, students and interested readers, not specialists in one theory.

### Top-level categories

The map is organised into eleven categories:

4. Information
5. Panpsychisms
6. Neutral Monism
7. Dualisms
8. Idealisms
9. Phenomenology
10. Anomalous & Altered States
11. Challenge

The page does not itself state an ordering. The guide's "physicalist to nonphysicalist" description comes from the reading's course guide and the secondary article, and it is a useful way to read the list, not a feature the page shows.

### Materialism

This is the largest and most finely divided family. Its philosophical subgroups include Eliminative / Illusionism, Mind-Brain Identity, Neurobiological, Electromagnetic Field, Computational & Functionalism, Homeostatic & Affective, Embodied & Enactive, Relational, First-Order, Higher-Order, Language Relationships, and Phylogenetic / Evolutionary, along with Overview and History entries. The labels list many named figures without subgroup headers in the fetched copy, so exact placements are uncertain. Among those named are Dennett and the Churchlands, Baars and Dehaene (global workspace), Friston and Seth (in a predictive group), and A. Clark/Chalmers. The Computational & Functionalism subgroup lists figures such as Turing, Putnam, Minsky and Bostrom, which is where the course's central question about computational functionalism is located.

### Non-Reductive Physicalism, Quantum & Dimensions, Information

Non-reductive physicalism lists Ellis, Maxwell, Van Inwagen, Sperry and others. Quantum & Dimensions contains the Penrose-Hameroff, Stapp and Wigner / von Neumann lines, along with Wolfram, Rovelli and quantum-Bayesian (QBism) entries. Information gathers Tononi, Koch, Chalmers, Davies, Levin and Hoel, with a "Critiques" subgroup. It is the category most directly relevant to integrated information theory, which the Seth and Bayne review covers.

### Panpsychisms, Neutral Monism, Dualisms, Idealisms

Panpsychism includes micropsychism, panprotopsychism and cosmopsychism, with figures such as Chalmers, Strawson and Goff. Neutral monism covers Spinoza, Leibniz, James, Whitehead and Russell, among others. Dualisms covers a historical sweep from Sāṃkhya, Aristotle, Aquinas and Descartes to Popper-Eccles interactionism and emergent dualism. Idealisms includes Kant, Hegel, Schopenhauer and Berkeley, as well as contemporary analytic idealists such as Kastrup and Hoffman, and a wide range of Indian and Buddhist traditions.

### Phenomenology, Anomalous & Altered States, Challenge

Phenomenology lists Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Heidegger, Sartre, Zahavi and Varela. Anomalous & Altered States covers parapsychology, near-death experiences, psychedelic and meditative states, with names such as Broad, Radin and Sheldrake. Challenge is a category of sceptical and critical positions, with names such as Nagel, McGinn, Fodor, Schwitzgebel, Pinker, Shermer and Sam Harris. Schwitzgebel's phenomenal consciousness paper, one of the section's Further readings, is therefore filed as a challenge to theorising rather than as a theory itself.

### Implications and other content

Four implication pages are linked: Meaning / Purpose, AI Consciousness, Virtual Immortality and Life after Death. The site also includes landscape articles, including one on the meta problem of consciousness, a taxonomy of explanations and a page on Closer to Truth's own views. A 2025 essay competition run with the Berggruen Institute, on consciousness and intelligence in artificial systems, is featured. The copyright line reads 2025 and 2026.

## Techniques & methods explained

The site does not explain experimental or argumentative methods. Its method is classification.

- **Categorise by family, then by theorist.** Each theory is placed under a family (for example, Higher-Order under Materialism), and each family lists the theorists who hold it. This lets a reader compare positions that share an ontology.
- **Map theories on a spectrum.** The guide and the secondary article describe a rough spectrum from physicalism to nonphysicalism. The categories are roughly in that order, though the page does not display the ordering.
- **Inclusion by argument.** The stated criterion is clear argumentation, not popularity or intuitive appeal. This is a methodological choice that also determines what is left out.
- **Theorist validation.** Entries are said to be checked by the theorists themselves, which reduces misrepresentation but means the map reflects the self-description of each camp.

Where this fails: a map of positions cannot say which positions are supported by evidence, how they compare on predictions, or whether two labels pick out the same view. The site does not claim otherwise, but it is easy to read the map as a ranking.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Aspect | Source's view (as stated) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Breadth | Hundreds of theories, including scientific, philosophical and religious traditions | Useful for seeing the full field |
| Curation | Theorists verify their own entries | Reduces error, but entries reflect self-presentation |
| Inclusion | Only theories with clear argument | Excludes theories that are popular but unargued |
| Structure | Categories roughly from physicalist to nonphysicalist | The page does not show the ordering |
| Neutrality | Kuhn argues for engaging the full range | Breadth can dilute evidential comparison |

Caution: the site is run by a media project and a foundation, and the survey is the editors' framing of the field. It is not a neutral census.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Materialism** - the family of views on which only physical states are real and consciousness is explained in physical terms.
- **Non-reductive physicalism** - physical views on which mental states are not reducible to physical description, while still held to be physical in some sense.
- **Dualism** - the view that mind and matter are distinct kinds of substance or property.
- **Idealism** - the view that only mental states, or mind-like states, are fundamentally real.
- **Panpsychism** - the view that consciousness, or something like it, is a feature of matter in general.
- **Neutral monism** - a view on which mind and matter are two aspects of one underlying neutral stuff.
- **Phenomenology** - the tradition that starts from first-person experience and its structure.
- **Challenge** - on this map, sceptical and critical positions about the very project of theorising consciousness.
- **Computational & Functionalism** - the materialist subgroup on which mental states are defined by computational or functional organisation.
- **Meta problem of consciousness** - a landscape article topic about why we find the hard problem puzzling, which the site treats as its own category of inquiry.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- The site lists **487 theories and 447 theorists** at capture. The course guide gives "over 350" theories, which matches the secondary article's wording. The difference is a sign the database has grown.
- The **Materialism** family has the most subgroups, about a dozen philosophical ones, which suggests that most of the theories on the map are materialist in some sense.
- The featured initiative is a **2025 Berggruen Prize Essay Competition** on consciousness, intelligence and the nature of mind in advancing artificial systems. It shows the site's own interest in AI.
- The secondary article says the map runs from materialism to idealism and that a theory's position shapes beliefs about free will, life after death and AI. That is Kuhn's view, not a result of the map itself.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **The map is a catalogue, not an argument.** It shows that many theories exist. It does not show which are true, which are testable, or how far the evidence goes. Using the map to choose a theory for Exercise 1 is reasonable, but the learner should not read its category order as a verdict.
- **The "physicalist to nonphysicalist" spectrum is a framing, not a measurement.** The page itself does not show the spectrum. The course guide and the secondary article supply it. Placing a theory on the spectrum is a judgement, and reasonable people will place Chalmers's panpsychism or Seth's biological naturalism differently.
- **Inclusion by argument is a double-edged criterion.** It removes theories with no argument, which helps. But whether a theory counts as argued is itself contested, and the criterion may favour theories whose proponents write in academic philosophy.
- **The mix of science, philosophy, religion and parapsychology.** Placing Nagarjuna, Aquinas, Radin and Hoffman on one map is valuable for breadth and misleading if read as a set of comparable research programmes. Some entries are empirical theories with predictions, while others are metaphysical views with no empirical test in view.
- **Counts change.** The count moved from over 350 in the guide to 487 at capture. Any learner quoting a number should date it.
- **Self-validation by theorists.** Entries checked by the theorists themselves are accurate about each view's self-description but may not reflect how critics read them.
- **Changes since publication.** The page is dated 2026, and the course is dated October 2026, so the map is current. The count has grown since the guide was written, so the guide's "350" is out of date. The site and the course both post-date the main AI-consciousness debate of 2023-2025 (Butlin et al., Chalmers), so the map's AI Consciousness page may already reflect later positions than the course's readings.
- **Other perspectives in the course would push back.** Seth and Shiller would question whether a long list of theories helps when many entries lack testable predictions. Chalmers would likely say the breadth shows that the question stays open and that AI consciousness deserves serious attention. Schwitzgebel would argue that the definitional question is itself part of the difficulty, which the map handles by putting him under Challenge.

## Discussion questions

1. The map lists 487 theories. What would it take for a theory on the map to be ruled out, and which categories on the map are most easily ruled out by evidence?
2. How much should a learner trust the "physicalist to nonphysicalist" ordering, given that the page does not show it? Where would you place Seth's biological naturalism and Chalmers's panpsychism, and why?
3. Choose one theory from the Materialism subgroups (for example, global workspace, higher-order or attention-schema). What does the theory claim about AI, and what evidence do Butlin et al. give for and against it?
4. Kuhn argues that experts become invested in one theory and that engaging with the full range is the remedy. Does breadth help a non-expert, or does it make the field look more open than the evidence supports?
5. The Challenge category places Schwitzgebel, Pinker and Nagel together. Are these positions best understood as theories of consciousness, or as arguments about the limits of theorising? Does the course's framing of "Further" reading fit that role?
6. Compare the Computational & Functionalism subgroup with the Information category. Which theories would be affected if Cao's multiple-realizability challenge succeeded?
7. Exercise 1 asks you to spend 20 minutes on one theory. Which entry on the map would you choose, and how would the map help or mislead you in finding its evidence and its implications for digital minds?
