# Computational Functionalism Debate | The Co-Sentience Initiative

**Linked from:** Exercise 2 of section 2.1 asks you to spend 15 minutes reviewing one argument for and one argument against computational functionalism (CF) on this site, then note what you find convincing or unconvincing about each.

## What it is

cf-debate.com is a structured index of arguments about whether computation alone can give rise to consciousness. It is run by The Co-Sentience Initiative, which holds the site's copyright. The homepage lists 31 arguments challenging CF and 13 supporting it, each on its own page, and marks three from each side as "key" arguments. It also has a short quiz, an FAQ, an email update sign-up, and a feedback form that offers a reward for major contributions. It is a reference resource rather than a paper, so it works best as a map of the debate that you then dip into.

## Key content

The homepage itself gives only argument titles. The three key arguments against CF are:

- **Physics Violations.** Functionalist theories such as global workspace theory need a system to carry forward its history. The fundamental equations of physics are Markovian, meaning the future depends only on the present state, and the present state cannot reveal which past produced it. The page concludes that history-dependent functionalism either relies on physics we do not have or faces serious difficulties. Its main rebuttal targets are that non-Markovian equations are only coarse-grained approximations, that the present may encode the past, and that Markovian and non-Markovian formulations are formally interchangeable. Each is answered, mostly by arguing that the memory involved is merely epistemic or that Markovian formulations are better motivated.
- **Phenomenal Binding Problem** and **Counterfactual Computation Critique** are listed but I could not retrieve their page content.

The three key arguments for CF are:

- **Fading Qualia.** Imagine neurons replaced one by one with silicon chips that behave identically. Behaviour never changes, yet experience would have to fade or vanish without the person noticing, which the argument treats as absurd. The conclusion is that functional organisation, not the biological material, is what matters. The page's main response is that the functions that matter may include non-computable processes, or physical effects such as electromagnetic coupling between neurons, that a silicon replacement would not reproduce. The page links this to Chalmers's 1995 paper of the same name.
- **Church-Turing Thesis** and **Multiple Realisability Argument** are listed but I could not retrieve their page content.

Reading both sides shows the shape of the debate. Each argument rests on a contested premise: for Fading Qualia, that a gradual replacement preserves experience, and for Physics Violations, that consciousness needs the history-dependent processes that current physics lacks. A useful comparison for Exercise 2 is whether the objection targets the argument's logic or its empirical assumptions.

## Why it's linked here

Exercise 2 needs a concrete, bounded set of arguments on each side of CF, and this site supplies one with a short page per argument. Pairing it with the readings on CF (for example Chalmers and Seth) lets you test a view against a structured counterargument rather than only against authors' own rebuttals.
