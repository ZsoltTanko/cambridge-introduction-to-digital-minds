# What 350 different theories of consciousness reveal about reality

**Linked from:** The "Why read this" guide for the Landscape of Consciousness Website (loc.closertotruth.com) in section 2.1. The guide's one-line blurb ("Surveys over 350 theories ... arranged on a roughly physicalist-to-nonphysicalist spectrum") is taken from this essay, so the essay is the source of that claim.

## What it is

An essay by Robert Lawrence Kuhn, the philosopher and host of Closer To Truth, written for *New Scientist* and republished on the Closer To Truth site. It is a popular-level introduction to the Landscape of Consciousness project: an attempt to chart the many theories of consciousness, from science, philosophy and religion, onto a single map. The essay itself is short. The Closer To Truth page carries only the introduction and points to New Scientist for the rest.

## Key content

The introduction makes three points:

- **There is no consensus on what consciousness is.** Kuhn calls it the ultimate question, and says we may not even have a clue yet.
- **Experts tend to over-commit to one theory.** Kuhn argues this blinds them to alternatives that could move the field forward. His response is to accept any theory, across disciplines, as long as it rests on clear arguments.
- **The theories form a spectrum.** He has mapped more than 350 theories (and counting) onto a "landscape". The spectrum runs from materialism, where only physical states are real, to idealism, where only mental states are real, with many positions in between.

He also argues that the choice of theory has practical stakes. A person's view of consciousness shapes their beliefs about free will, life after death and whether AI can be conscious. Kuhn notes that neuroscientists often assume experience comes from neurons firing, while other theories treat consciousness as fundamental or physical reality as an illusion. The essay poses the two extremes as questions: does consciousness arise when quantum wavefunctions collapse, and is the cosmos itself conscious in some sense?

The essay does not give a conclusion or argue for a specific theory. The excerpt is an invitation to explore the landscape, not a finished argument.

The companion website is a catalogue of several hundred theories grouped into families (materialism, idealism, dualism, panpsychism, higher-order and computational theories), plus essays and pages on implications for AI, free will and life after death. Its map data is interactive. This summary is based on the essay excerpt and the site's navigation, not on the full text.

## Why it's linked here

The guide's "Why read this" note sends learners to the Landscape of Consciousness Website as a further resource after the section's core readings. The essay is the accessible entry point: it explains what the landscape is and why mapping theories matters before a learner explores the site's detail. Its framing (no consensus, many theories, real stakes for AI and free will) matches the section's theme of comparing theories of consciousness.
