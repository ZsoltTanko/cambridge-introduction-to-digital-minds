# Should digital minds governance prevent, protect, or integrate?

**Linked from:** Exercise 1 of section 7.1 asks learners to rate preventive, protective and integrative digital minds governance on five dimensions, using a matrix tool. This post by Bradford Saad is where those three categories and five dimensions come from, and it contains the author's own ratings for comparison.

## What it is

A long essay by the philosopher Bradford Saad, published on his Substack, *Meditations on Digital Minds*. It takes a strategic question that he raised in earlier posts: if digital minds (AI systems that deserve moral consideration for their own sake) come to exist, what form should governance take? He compares three options: preventive governance, which stops digital minds from being created; protective governance, which safeguards the basic interests of digital minds that exist; and integrative governance, which brings digital minds into society with legal rights and duties.

The piece is an informed guess rather than an empirical study, and the author says so repeatedly. It is roughly 10,000 words, and the course link points to the full text.

## Key content

Saad scopes the analysis to "competent" efforts over roughly the next decade, meaning humanly competent rather than flawless. He sets aside hybrid approaches, the question of how governance should evolve over time, and economic feasibility, on the view that failure would most likely come through politics or poor design.

He rates each approach on five dimensions:

- **Design difficulty.** Preventive is moderate: it needs digital-minds evaluations, monitoring and prevention, and the evaluations are within near-term reach. Protective is very hard, mainly because it also needs "moral interest" evaluations, which must establish what a system's interests are, not only whether it has them. Integrative is likely infeasible, since existing institutions are built for humans.
- **Implementation difficulty.** Preventive is moderate, because it can focus on architectural markers and on developers. Protective is very hard, since it would need a much larger monitoring capacity across deployment. Integrative is likely infeasible, given alignment demands and the need for international agreements.
- **Political prospects.** Preventive is good, because actors with very different motives could converge on it, and it can be pursued technocratically. Protective is dicey: appeals to basic decency have traction, but public support is far from settled. Integrative is bleak, because it looks like a competition with humans for resources and invites opposition on grounds of human dignity.
- **Near-term impact on digital minds.** Preventive gives large harm reduction with modest downside risks, such as developers moving to permissive jurisdictions. Protective gives modest harm reduction with moderate downside risks, including complacency and the chance that protections themselves shape AI self-concern. Integrative offers doubtful upside and major downside risks.
- **Relation to AI safety.** Preventive is positive, since fewer moral-patiency-relevant systems tend to mean less agentic systems. Protective is ambiguous but has significant potential for interference, because models could influence how they are treated. Integrative is also ambiguous but has strong potential for interference, since rights such as property, labour compensation and representation could be used to advance unsafe goals.

His bottom line: preventive governance looks like the best option. Preventive or protective governance beats the status quo of treating digital minds as mere tools. Integrative governance would be a strategic mistake in the near term.

## Why it's linked here

Exercise 1 asks learners to fill in the same matrix for the same three approaches and five dimensions. The post is the source of that framework, and the exercise credits Saad. The post also asks readers to form their own view before reading further, so the link works as a comparison point after a learner has made their own ratings, not as a required reading. Learners who want to check their reasoning against an expert view can open it there.
