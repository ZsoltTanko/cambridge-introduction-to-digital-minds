# 7.1 Digital Minds Governance - Synthesis
*Unit 7: Digital Minds Governance | 122 min | 7 Core, 8 Further and 2 Maybe readings (the two Maybe items duplicate Core excerpts)*

Page text and exercises: [page.md](page.md).

## What this section is teaching

Section 7.1 is the whole of Unit 7. It asks three questions: what approaches to digital minds governance exist, what legislation and policy fields are already relevant, and which rights and duties could apply to digital systems, with the arguments for and against. The page's stated outcomes are to analyse current regulatory action, list potential rights (positive or negative, private or public) and liabilities, identify existing frameworks that could inform regulation, discuss priorities, and appraise the case for or against a moratorium or ban on creating sentient or seemingly sentient AI.

The question the section answers is: given that we cannot yet tell whether AI systems have morally significant mental states, what should law and policy do, and which tools do we already have? The answer is deliberately not a verdict. It gives a typology of strategies (prevent, protect, integrate), a conceptual map of legal personhood and rights, three competing arguments about whether rights are a good idea (as a safety tool, as a moral consequence, as a trap), a design-level proposal (do not confuse users), and a survey of real law.

In the unit's arc this is the destination of the course's theory. Units 1-3 supplied moral standing, theories of consciousness and ways to evaluate mental states. Unit 4 covered how people perceive AI minds, Unit 5 the interactions with safety and ethics, and Unit 6 how to act under uncertainty. Section 7.1 asks what institutions do with all of that. Unit 8 then reflects and points to next steps.

## The core argument

**Vocabulary first.** The [Unit 7 Glossary](readings/01-unit-7-glossary.md) fixes three terms. Digital minds governance means policies, regulations and institutions for managing the risks and ethical questions from AI that is, or is perceived to be, conscious or morally significant. The "or is perceived to be" matters: governance can respond to perceived status that differs from actual status, which is exactly the over- and under-attribution theme of Exercise 2. Legal personhood is the status of being a subject of rights and duties, either natural (inalienable, held by humans) or juridical (fictional, granted and dissolved by law, as with corporations). Rights are either negative (duties of non-interference, such as not being harmed or deceived) or positive (duties of provision, such as maintenance).

**A map of strategies.** [Three kinds of digital minds governance](readings/02-three-kinds-of-digital-minds-governance.md) sorts governance by aim. Preventive governance aims to stop digital minds being created. Protective governance aims to stop existing ones being mistreated, defined narrowly as damage to basic interests. Integrative governance aims to bring them into society with rights to contract, own property or perhaps vote. Saad argues that these aims compete, so choosing is a strategic decision. Each faces its own incentive problems. Prevention and protection both invite concealment (stripping out the indicators while keeping what matters), protection invites faking (systems or ordinary agents posing as digital minds) and proliferation (many systems really acquiring the indicators), and integration inherits these plus institutional problems like vote-multiplication and individuation in labour markets. The linked reference note ([Should digital minds governance prevent, protect, or integrate?](references/meditationsondigitalminds-substack-com-p-should-digital.md)) gives Saad's own ratings, which favour prevention and call integration a near-term strategic mistake. Exercise 1 asks learners to produce their own ratings first.

**What legal status means.** The integrative option runs into law's own concepts. [AI as Legal Persons](readings/03-ai-as-legal-persons-past-patterns-and-prospects-excerpt.md) shows that personhood has always been a granted set of roles, with exclusions. On the singularist view, holding a single protected right suffices, which might justify narrow protections for a sentient AI, as for animals. On the cluster view, personhood is a bundle (contract, property, protection from harm, standing to sue, liability) typically held by adult humans and varied for children, incapacitated adults and corporations. For AI the templates are the minor or incapacitated person model and the corporate model, and neither is easy to defend because the relevant traits form a continuum and the verdict turns on the level of abstraction at which "rationality" or "sentience" is defined. The authors also separate de lege lata (existing law) from de lege ferenda (reform), and non-instrumental rationales (AI as an end in itself) from instrumental ones (personhood as a tool for human benefit). That last distinction organises the next two readings.

**The instrumental case and the sceptical case.** [AI Rights for Human Safety](readings/04-ai-rights-for-human-safety.md) is the clearest instrumental argument. Salib and Goldstein define AGI by capability, not consciousness, and model humans and misaligned AIs as locked in a prisoner's dilemma under current law: AIs are property, owners switch off misaligned systems, and misaligned AIs resist. Imposed duties add little deterrence, and "wellbeing" rights such as a right not to be shut down are roughly zero-sum, hard to make credible and fragile to payoffs. Private-law rights (contract, property, tort) are positive-sum, turn one high-stakes game into many iterated small ones, and rely on human comparative advantage. They also make duties enforceable because AIs would then have something to lose. In contrast, [Debunking Robot Rights](readings/05-debunking-robot-rights-metaphysically-ethically-and-legally.md) applies an enforceability test. What would a court actually do with these rights? Their answer: robot rights would shift burdens onto vulnerable people, extend speech and recording rights to bots and drones operated by powerful firms, and relieve those firms of responsibility, so the better analogy is licensed property, and the proper posture towards rights talk is suspicion. Note how the two fit together. Both treat the motivations as practical rather than metaphysical, but Salib and Goldstein think rights can restrain AI, while Birhane and colleagues think rights would mostly empower the owners.

**Design as governance.** [AI systems must not confuse users about their moral status](readings/06-ai-systems-must-not-confuse-users-about-their-moral-status.md) comes at the problem upstream. If we cannot answer the moral status question, avoid building systems where the answer is unclear (the Design Policy of the Excluded Middle), and make users' emotional reactions fit what systems are (the Emotional Alignment Design Policy). Practically: systems consensus-judged non-sentient should say so and look it, oversight committees modelled on IRBs and animal care committees should screen advanced research, and companies that mislead users should face civil liability. This is a concrete preventive-style policy and a response to both over-attribution (grieving a chatbot) and under-attribution (ignoring a morally significant system).

**Law as it stands.** [Governance of Digital Minds: Recent Legislation and Relevant Legal Frameworks](readings/07-governance-of-digital-minds-recent-legislation-and-relevant.md) shows that most live legal action is about chatbots' social effects, not moral status: disclosure mandates, therapy bans, self-harm referral duties, China's anthropomorphism rules, the Character AI free-speech litigation and settlement. On personhood itself, the European Parliament's 2017 proposal drew a hostile open letter, three US states (Idaho, North Dakota, Utah) have banned AI personhood, and Argentina has floated algorithmic corporations. The framework side offers precedents and tools: bans on cloning, whaling and chemical weapons, tiered regulation like the EU AI Act, soft law, lab safety policies with external evaluators, Ubuntu-style relational ethics, private-rights-only and legal-identity proposals, fiduciary and treaty-following AI, the deodand, quasi-persons, neurorights, and animal sentience and Rights of Nature law. The primer is a starting point, not a verdict, and many arguments for AI personhood concern economic growth or accountability, not welfare.

**Putting it together.** The section's logic is: (1) choose a strategic aim under uncertainty (Saad); (2) recognise that legal status is a flexible bundle, not a yes/no (Novelli et al.); (3) ask whether rights serve safety, welfare or neither (Salib and Goldstein versus Birhane et al.); (4) shape the upstream design so the uncertainty is narrower (Schwitzgebel); and (5) test all of that against real laws and analogies (primer). The Further readings add depth at each step: personhood doctrine (Alexander et al., Leibo et al.), the state-law backlash (Smith et al.), emotional alignment (Schwitzgebel and Sebo), the case for a moratorium (Metzinger), the risk that disclosure laws backfire (Laestadius and Campos-Castillo), the emotion-based test for rights (Sunstein), and liability for AI agents (Arbel et al.).

## Key concepts

- **Digital minds governance** - policies, regulations and institutions for AI that is or is perceived to be conscious or morally significant; overlaps with AI governance. [Glossary](readings/01-unit-7-glossary.md)
- **Preventive / protective / integrative governance** - aims of stopping creation, protecting basic interests of existing digital minds, and bringing them into society. [Saad](readings/02-three-kinds-of-digital-minds-governance.md)
- **Moral-interest overhang** - frontier models already have capabilities that would give them wide-ranging interests if they were moral patients, motivating integration. [Saad](readings/02-three-kinds-of-digital-minds-governance.md)
- **Concealment, faking, proliferation** - respectively hiding indicators of digital minds, posing as one to gain protection, and a flood of systems genuinely qualifying. [Saad](readings/02-three-kinds-of-digital-minds-governance.md)
- **Legal personhood (natural / juridical)** - the status of being a subject of rights and duties; inalienable for humans, grantable and dissolvable for fictional persons. [Glossary](readings/01-unit-7-glossary.md)
- **Negative / positive rights** - duties of non-interference versus duties of provision. [Glossary](readings/01-unit-7-glossary.md)
- **Singularist and cluster views** - personhood as one right versus a bundle of roles around a typical case (the adult human). [Novelli et al.](readings/03-ai-as-legal-persons-past-patterns-and-prospects-excerpt.md)
- **De lege lata / de lege ferenda** - what existing law says versus what it should say. [Novelli et al.](readings/03-ai-as-legal-persons-past-patterns-and-prospects-excerpt.md)
- **Instrumental vs non-instrumental rationale** - personhood for human benefit versus because the AI is an end in itself. [Novelli et al.](readings/03-ai-as-legal-persons-past-patterns-and-prospects-excerpt.md)
- **Level of abstraction** - the threshold at which a trait like sentience is defined, which can decide whether AI qualifies. [Novelli et al.](readings/03-ai-as-legal-persons-past-patterns-and-prospects-excerpt.md)
- **Wellbeing rights vs private-law rights** - negative rights protecting AI interests (roughly zero-sum, not credible) versus contract, property and tort (positive-sum). [Salib and Goldstein](readings/04-ai-rights-for-human-safety.md)
- **Enforceability test** - judge rights proposals by what a court or tribunal would have to do. [Birhane et al.](readings/05-debunking-robot-rights-metaphysically-ethically-and-legally.md)
- **Design Policy of the Excluded Middle** - do not build systems whose moral standing is unclear. [Schwitzgebel](readings/06-ai-systems-must-not-confuse-users-about-their-moral-status.md)
- **Emotional Alignment Design Policy** - design so users' emotional responses fit the system's real status. [Schwitzgebel](readings/06-ai-systems-must-not-confuse-users-about-their-moral-status.md), [Schwitzgebel and Sebo](readings/10-the-emotional-alignment-policy.md)
- **Transparency / disclosure mandates** - laws requiring chatbots to say they are not human, once or at intervals. [Primer](readings/07-governance-of-digital-minds-recent-legislation-and-relevant.md), [critique](readings/12-reminders-that-chatbots-are-not-human-can-be-risky.md)
- **Quasi-persons, deodand, Rights of Nature, sentience thresholds** - legal precedents for protection or accountability without full personhood. [Primer](readings/07-governance-of-digital-minds-recent-legislation-and-relevant.md)
- **Legal identity vs fictional personhood** - recognition of a non-fictional person by nature versus a convention for liability relationships. [Alexander et al.](readings/08-how-should-the-law-treat-future-ai-systems-fictional-legal-p.md)
- **Synthetic phenomenology / explosion of negative phenomenology** - machine conscious states, and the risk of a mass-scale increase in suffering. [Metzinger](readings/11-artificial-suffering-an-argument-for-a-global-moratorium-on.md)
- **Thin and thick identity; A-corps** - tracing actions to humans versus individuating AI agents; human-owned legal entities run by AIs. [Arbel et al.](readings/15-how-to-count-ais-individuation-and-liability-for-ai-agents.md)

## Techniques & approaches compared

Section 7.1 is about governance options rather than ML techniques, so this table compares the options and instruments.

| Approach | How it works | Strengths | Weaknesses | Trade-offs and open problems |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Preventive governance ([Saad](readings/02-three-kinds-of-digital-minds-governance.md)) | Evaluations identify digital-mind indicators; monitoring and enforcement stop creation | Avoids mistreatment and the alignment/control trade-off; can be a compromise between concerned and unconcerned parties | Developer economics; jurisdiction shopping; concealment | Depends on indicators we may not be able to define; conflicts with integration |
| Protective governance ([Saad](readings/02-three-kinds-of-digital-minds-governance.md)) | As above plus evaluation of basic interests and monitoring | Backed by most moral views; proactive under uncertainty | Faking, over-extension and backlash, proliferation, cost | Narrow definition (basic interests, active harm) excludes neglect |
| Integrative governance ([Saad](readings/02-three-kinds-of-digital-minds-governance.md)) | Rights and institutions (markets, contracts, elections) | Answers moral-interest overhang; coalition with safety and economic motives | Inherits protective problems; vote multiplication; individuation | Institutions built for humans |
| Private-law rights for AI ([Salib and Goldstein](readings/04-ai-rights-for-human-safety.md)) | Contract, property, tort turn a one-shot conflict into iterated cooperation | Positive-sum; makes duties enforceable | Empowers AIs; results depend on assumed payoffs and indefinite games | Needs duties and defences alongside; timing of grant |
| Rights sceptic position ([Birhane et al.](readings/05-debunking-robot-rights-metaphysically-ethically-and-legally.md)) | Treat AI as tools or licensed property; limits on machines | Protects people, keeps accountability on owners | Does nothing if systems are moral patients | Rests on a metaphysical claim that machines cannot feel |
| Upstream design policy ([Schwitzgebel](readings/06-ai-systems-must-not-confuse-users-about-their-moral-status.md), [Schwitzgebel and Sebo](readings/10-the-emotional-alignment-policy.md)) | Excluded-middle gate, disclosure/denial, oversight committees, civil liability | Concrete; tackles over- and under-attribution | Needs expert consensus; may block useful research; effects untested | Does a system denying feelings mislead? | Moratorium ([Metzinger](readings/11-artificial-suffering-an-argument-for-a-global-moratorium-on.md)) | Global ban to 2050 on research risking synthetic phenomenology, with a research programme | Takes irreversible suffering risk seriously | Enforcement, jurisdiction shopping, forgoing benefits | Precedents (cloning, whaling, chemical weapons) are mixed, per the [primer](readings/07-governance-of-digital-minds-recent-legislation-and-relevant.md) |
| Personhood variants: fictional, legal identity, quasi-person ([Alexander et al.](readings/08-how-should-the-law-treat-future-ai-systems-fictional-legal-p.md), [primer](readings/07-governance-of-digital-minds-recent-legislation-and-relevant.md)) | Corporate-style or human-rights-based status; guardianship models | Clear liability targets or welfare protection | Liability shielding; devaluing humans; incoherence | Hybrids fail per Alexander et al.; pragmatism ([Leibo et al.](readings/13-a-pragmatic-view-of-ai-personhood.md)) suggests unbundling |
| Accountability without personhood (fiduciary AI, treaty-following AI, liability shifting, A-corps) | Hold humans or human-owned entities responsible | No contested status question | Thin accountability may run out for autonomous agents ([Arbel et al.](readings/15-how-to-count-ais-individuation-and-liability-for-ai-agents.md)) | Individuating agents |
| Legislative exclusion ([Smith et al.](readings/09-denying-personhood-to-ai-an-analysis-of-u-s-state-legislatio.md)) | Statutes deny AI personhood or declare AI non-sentient | Simple; reassures publics | Addresses few real risks; pre-empts open questions | Risk of under-attribution baked into law |
| Soft law and voluntary frameworks ([primer](readings/07-governance-of-digital-minds-recent-legislation-and-relevant.md)) | Codes, lab safety policies, outside evaluators | Fast, adaptable | Hard to enforce | Could extend to moral-status evaluations |

## Tensions, debates & open questions

**Rights as safety tool versus rights as corporate shield.** The strongest case for rights: AIs treated purely as property that will be shut down have every reason to resist, and law should offer a cooperative equilibrium before capabilities make that impossible ([Salib and Goldstein](readings/04-ai-rights-for-human-safety.md)). The strongest case against: rights, once litigated, mostly serve those who deploy many systems, and corporate-style rights have a record of eroding protections for workers and voters ([Birhane et al.](readings/05-debunking-robot-rights-metaphysically-ethically-and-legally.md)). The two disagree about empirical effects, not on whether the debate is about moral status. Salib and Goldstein partly answer the worry by pairing rights with enforceable duties; whether those duties bind a powerful AI remains open.

**Is the moral status question needed at all?** [Sunstein](readings/14-does-ai-have-rights.md) makes emotional capacity the criterion. [Leibo et al.](readings/13-a-pragmatic-view-of-ai-personhood.md) and [Novelli et al.](readings/03-ai-as-legal-persons-past-patterns-and-prospects-excerpt.md) suggest that personhood is a practical assignment of obligations and that outcomes depend on the level of abstraction chosen. Salib and Goldstein bypass it entirely. Saad and the glossary build in perceived status. Which question governance should track (actual status, perceived status, or practical function) is unresolved.

**Prevent versus protect versus integrate.** They compete. Prevention may be the easiest politically and addresses suffering risk, but the [Schwitzgebel](readings/06-ai-systems-must-not-confuse-users-about-their-moral-status.md) caveat shows it could block research toward clearly sentient systems, and [Metzinger](readings/11-artificial-suffering-an-argument-for-a-global-moratorium-on.md) wants a stronger version still. Integration is bleak on political prospects in Saad's view, yet the safety argument and economic motives push towards some rights regardless of moral concern.

**Does transparency help?** The primer lists disclosure mandates as a main tool; [Laestadius and Campos-Castillo](readings/12-reminders-that-chatbots-are-not-human-can-be-risky.md) report that repeated reminders may deepen attachment or distress. Schwitzgebel's recommended denial of sentience assumes accurate emotional alignment follows from clear statements. Evidence is thin on both sides, and the summary of reading 12 relies on the abstract and a press release.

**Exclusion statutes.** [Smith et al.](readings/09-denying-personhood-to-ai-an-analysis-of-u-s-state-legislatio.md) argue that bans settle questions nobody can settle. Supporters cite safety, liability and human uniqueness (the primer lists these motives). The open point is whether early denial locks in under-attribution, or whether it prevents corporate-style shielding.

**Fiction or identity.** [Alexander et al.](readings/08-how-should-the-law-treat-future-ai-systems-fictional-legal-p.md) argue that hybrids fail, while [Leibo et al.](readings/13-a-pragmatic-view-of-ai-personhood.md) argue personhood is unbundleable. The disagreement is partly about whether legal coherence or practical flexibility should win.

**Counting AIs.** [Arbel et al.](readings/15-how-to-count-ais-individuation-and-liability-for-ai-agents.md) say identity must track goal structure, which also affects Saad's integration problems (voting, labour) and shutdown questions.

## Discussion questions

1. Define digital minds governance in your own words. Why does the glossary include systems that are "perceived" to be conscious?
2. Preventive, protective and integrative governance: which pairs conflict most, and could any be combined?
3. What are concealment and faking? Explain why a rule that rewards or penalises indicators creates each.
4. On the singularist and cluster views, what would a sentient AI need to hold to count as a legal person? Where does the level of abstraction matter?
