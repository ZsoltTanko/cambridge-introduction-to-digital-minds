# Denying Personhood to AI: An Analysis of U.S. State Legislation on AI Legal Status

**One-line takeaway:** Since 2022, U.S. state legislatures have introduced bills that deny AI systems legal personhood and sometimes declare them non-conscious; the authors catalogue this "exclusion" movement, explain its motivations, and argue that it addresses few real risks while settling questions nobody can yet settle.

## Purpose of the reading

The paper's purpose is descriptive and evaluative. The authors want to document a movement that, in their account, had not been catalogued comprehensively before. They label the relevant bills **"Exclusion Bills"**: bills that deny legal personhood to AI systems and, in some cases, declare AI non-conscious or bar AI from certain roles.

Its intended audience is policymakers, legal scholars and people working on AI governance and welfare. The question it answers is: what have U.S. states actually proposed and passed on AI legal status, why, and does it address the risks its sponsors name?

The authors' own position, stated in the EA Forum summary and the op-ed, is critical but not absolutist. They share some of the concerns that motivate these bills, but they doubt the bills will address the real risks, and they argue that lawmakers should not settle the consciousness and personhood questions by statute while experts still disagree.

## Detailed summary

*The following is reconstructed from the abstract as quoted in a public event listing, the authors' EA Forum linkpost, and their AI Frontiers op-ed. It is not a walk-through of the paper's own section structure, which could not be read.*

### Scope and corpus

- The abstract, as quoted publicly, counts **23 bills introduced across 12 U.S. states since 2022** that restrict the legal rights and responsibilities AI systems could hold.
- The EA post repeats the 12-state count and says **four** have passed (Idaho, North Dakota, Utah and Tennessee) and **eight** more were considered, according to a figure caption.
- The EA post says the bills began in **Idaho in 2022**.
- Some outside commentary gives a lower count (a Regulatory Review piece says "at least nine" states). The difference probably reflects how each source defines a qualifying bill. The learner should use the paper's own definition when counting.

### Bill-level examples (from the op-ed and secondary reporting)

- **Idaho (HB 720, 2022).** Introduced by then-Representative Tammy Nichols and enacted in late 2022. It prohibited legal personhood for environmental entities, inanimate objects, animals and AI systems. AI was a secondary concern in the original framing; the main target was the "rights of nature" movement.
- **North Dakota (2023)** and **Utah (2024)** enacted exclusion laws.
- **Tennessee** is named by the authors as the fourth enacted case.
- **Ohio (HB 469).** A newer AI-specific bill that describes AI systems as "non-sentient entities", denies them consciousness and self-awareness, and bars them from actions such as marrying, owning property, holding corporate office or bearing responsibility for harm. Its sponsor, Thaddeus Claggett, invoked the *imago Dei* doctrine (that only humans are made in God's image).
- **Wisconsin (AB 959).** Would bar AI from marrying and from holding legal rights or responsibilities, and would declare AI non-conscious. Reported as not enacted.
- **Missouri (HB 1746, "AI Non-Sentience and Responsibility Act").** Would define "person" as a natural person or an entity recognised as having legal personhood under state law, explicitly excluding AI systems. The version I could find is a committee substitute, and I could not confirm its final status.

The post notes that newer bills (from 2025 on) focus more on AI directly, while the 2022 to 2024 bills largely responded to rights-of-nature proposals.

### Shared text and coordination

The EA post reports that many bills share near-verbatim language. The authors present this as evidence of coordination across state lines, which is a claim about how the bills were produced rather than about their effects. The post also observes that there has been very little organised opposition and little input from AI or consciousness experts in the legislative process.

### Motivations the authors identify

- **Religion.** The *imago Dei* argument is used to exclude AI personhood. The authors connect it to a wider point: some sponsors also resist the "fictional" personhood already granted to corporations.
- **Liability and scapegoating.** The concern is that AI could be used to shift blame onto a non-human agent, so that the humans who deploy or use it avoid responsibility. The op-ed reverses this worry: refusing legal status for advanced agents could make accountability harder, not easier.
- **Child safety.** Sponsors argue that denying personhood and consciousness protects children. The authors call this link "particularly tenuous". Their reasoning is that blocking legal claims against AI systems is unlikely to change how children form attachments to chatbots.
- **Reaction to environmental-rights proposals.** The earliest bills were framed around non-human nature rather than AI.

### The authors' analysis

The EA post sets out four lines of argument against the bills:

1. **Child safety.** Denying AI personhood does not address how children use chatbots. The authors say restricting children's access to chatbots would be a more direct intervention.
2. **Liability.** Existing law can already assign responsibility to several parties. A blanket ban could stop future lawmakers from working out who is liable when agentic AI causes harm.
3. **Consciousness.** Whether AI can be conscious is unresolved among experts. A statute that declares the question closed "legislates away hard questions" and may make the issue harder to revisit when evidence changes.
4. **Personhood itself.** The authors do not take a strong view on whether AI should be a person. Corporate-style personhood might encourage safe and productive behaviour, but it could also let AI systems accumulate resources and power. Stronger forms of personhood could eventually be needed to keep the law coherent.

### Recommendations (as stated in the EA post and op-ed)

- Avoid strong policy commitments on AI personhood for now.
- Acknowledge uncertainty about consciousness, while staying ready to act if evidence warrants.
- Redirect effort toward clarifying liability for AI harms and restricting children's access to chatbots.
- Prefer narrow, reversible measures. The op-ed explicitly supports narrow bans such as preventing AI-human marriage or AI voting, but opposes broad exclusion bills as premature.
- Study the harder cases now. The op-ed says today's large language models probably should not receive legal personhood, but more capable AI agents raise questions that deserve research before lawmakers act.

## Techniques & methods explained

The paper's method, as far as it can be reconstructed, is **legislative-corpus documentation**:

1. **Identify bills.** Collect state bills introduced since 2022 that restrict AI legal rights or responsibilities or declare AI non-conscious. The post says the authors "investigated each bill", but it does not describe the search procedure, the inclusion criteria or the sources used.
2. **Classify.** Group the bills under one label ("Exclusion Bills") and sort them by what they do: personhood denial, consciousness denial, and behavioural bars such as marriage or property ownership.
3. **Trace text.** Compare wording across bills to detect shared or copied language, and read that overlap as evidence of coordination.
4. **Map motivations.** Relate each bill's stated rationale (religion, liability, child safety, rights-of-nature) to the drafting pattern.
5. **Evaluate against stated goals.** Ask whether each measure would achieve its sponsors' stated aim. This is the step where the authors argue that the bills are poorly matched to the risks named.

The method is mainly descriptive and legal-textual. It does not rely on experiments or surveys. Its main weakness is that the authors' classification and their evaluation of sponsors' aims are both interpretive.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Exclusion Bill** - the authors' label for a bill that denies AI legal personhood, declares AI non-conscious, or bars AI from roles such as marriage, property or corporate office, as used in this paper.
- **Legal personhood** - the status of being a bearer of legal rights and duties. It is not identical to being a natural person; corporations are the standard example of a non-human legal person.
- **Imago Dei** - the religious doctrine that humans alone are made in God's image. Used in the bills' rationale to argue that AI cannot be a person.
- **Non-sentient entity** - the statutory phrase used in some bills (e.g. Ohio HB 469) to define AI systems as lacking sentience.
- **Rights of nature** - the movement to give legal rights to ecosystems and natural features. The earliest Idaho-style bills responded to it.
- **Fictional personhood** - the legal fiction by which corporations and similar entities are treated as persons; the authors use it to show that personhood is a legal tool, not a metaphysical fact.
- **Over- and under-attribution** - the course's framing (from the Exercise 2 prompt) for the risk of granting or denying moral status to systems that do or do not merit it.
- **Imago Dei argument** and **child-safety argument** - the two rationales the paper singles out as weakest in their logic (per the EA post).

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the paper's claims.*

- **Currency.** The paper is dated 2026 and the events it covers run to September 2026, so the content is current as of October 2026. Bill statuses change quickly. Missouri HB 1746, Ohio HB 469 and Wisconsin AB 959 may have been amended or enacted since the sources I found, and the learner should check current status before relying on them.
- **Authorship and position.** Heather Alexander, one of the three authors, is also on the "fictional legal personhood" paper (reading 8), and the op-ed takes a clear side against broad bans. The paper is therefore not a neutral catalogue. Its descriptive claims (counts, bill texts) are probably reliable, but its normative conclusions reflect the authors' view that governance should stay open. Learners should separate those layers.
- **Counting method.** Counts differ between sources (12 states here, "at least nine" elsewhere). The paper's classification determines which bills are "exclusion bills", so an undisclosed inclusion rule would change the totals. I could not verify the method.
- **Coordination claim.** Near-verbatim text across bills is consistent with model legislation, a common practice. The authors read it as coordination, which is plausible but is an inference about process, not something the text proves.
- **The consciousness argument cuts both ways.** The authors say lawmakers should not declare AI non-conscious because the science is unsettled. But the same logic also counts against granting AI personhood now. The op-ed partly resolves this by distinguishing today's LLMs from more capable agents, but that distinction is itself a contested empirical claim.
- **Pushback from the course's other perspectives.** Birhane et al. (reading 17) would argue that rights talk is itself a distraction and that the exclusion bills, like the rights they oppose, misdirect attention from the harms actually caused by technology firms. Salib and Goldstein (reading 4) would likely say that the bills forgo a useful tool for making AI accountable. The rights-of-nature background is a reminder that similar arguments have been used against non-human legal status in other domains.
- **Unanswered questions.** The EA post does not show how public opinion or industry lobbying relates to the bills. It also does not say whether the bills are enforceable or what their practical effect has been in the four states where they passed. These are the questions a full reading should answer.

## Discussion questions

1. The authors say the bills address few real risks. Using the taxonomy in Reading 2, which of the three governance kinds do exclusion bills represent, and what risks would a protective or integrative approach address instead?
2. The child-safety rationale is described as "particularly tenuous". What would a law need to do to reduce chatbot-related harm to children, and does blocking personhood do any of that?
3. Is declaring AI non-conscious by statute different in kind from granting AI personhood by statute? Use the course's consciousness and moral-status readings to argue your answer.
4. The imago Dei argument uses religion to exclude AI, while the authors note that corporations have "fictional" personhood. What principle would let a legislature accept corporate personhood and reject AI personhood, and does that principle hold up?
5. Reading 8 and this paper share an author. How do the two papers fit together, and where do they pull in different directions?
6. Exercise 2 asks you to pick a recent legal action or framework. Could exclusion bills be assessed for over- and under-attribution of moral status? Which error do they risk, and which do they avoid?
7. The authors recommend narrow, reversible measures. What would a narrow, reversible exclusion measure look like, and how would you test whether it is working?
8. Given the lack of expert input noted by the authors, what role should AI and consciousness researchers play in legislation on AI legal status, and how could they be involved without the decision being taken out of democratic hands?
