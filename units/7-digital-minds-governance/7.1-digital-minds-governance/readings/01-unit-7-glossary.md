## Detailed summary

The document is a single page of definitions, with a short header and licence footer. There are three entries.

**Governance, Digital Minds.** This is defined as policies, regulations and institutional frameworks meant to manage the risks and ethical questions raised by AI systems that are, or are perceived to be, conscious or morally significant. Two features stand out. First, the definition covers perceived as well as actual moral status, which (on the summarizer's reading) anticipates the over- and under-attribution theme of the course. Second, it says digital minds governance overlaps with broader AI governance. On the summarizer's reading, the scope is therefore not a separate legal regime but a set of concerns inside AI governance, about how societies should act when they are uncertain about moral status.

**Personhood, Legal.** The entry first calls legal personhood a key part of Western law, allowing a person, corporation or other entity to take part in the legal system as the subject of rights and duties, so that giving something rights and duties is the same as making it a legal person. It then distinguishes two types:

- Natural personhood, which the glossary calls inalienable and which it says humans hold.
- Juridical personhood, also called artificial or fictional personhood, which corporations hold. This type can be granted and dissolved by legal processes.

The entry notes that the concept has been used since Roman times, applied to entities as varied as ships, corporations and rivers. It ends with a point about standing: humans are considered legal persons with a greater range of rights, while purely legal persons have different standing. The example given is that a corporation can own property but cannot marry.

**Rights, Negative/Positive.** Rights are defined as entitlements to perform, or not perform, certain actions, or to be, or not be, in certain states. They can be held by a right-holder against others who must perform or refrain from actions. The entry separates two kinds:

- Negative rights are duties of non-interference. Examples are the right not to be harmed or destroyed, and the right not to be deceived or manipulated.
- Positive rights are duties of provision. Examples are the right to maintenance, to continued existence, to access to information, and to the conditions for flourishing.

## How the terms fit together

*The summarizer's synthesis, not the glossary's wording.*

- Governance is the field in which the question of how to treat digital minds is answered.
- Legal personhood is one of the main instruments in that field. Granting personhood gives an entity the subject position that rights and duties attach to.
- Rights are the content that legal personhood carries. Negative and positive rights describe the kind of duty others owe to the right-holder.

Read together, the entries suggest that a governance choice is often a choice about which entities to make legal persons and which duties to attach to them. The summarizer's inference, not the glossary's claim, is that the section's readings will differ on whether that choice should follow actual moral status, perceived moral status, or practical goals such as safety or liability.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Digital minds governance** - policies, regulations and institutional frameworks for managing risks and ethical questions from AI that is, or is perceived to be, conscious or morally significant. Overlaps with broader AI governance.
- **Legal personhood** - the status that lets an entity be the subject of legal rights and duties. Giving rights and duties is the same as making an entity a legal person in this sense.
- **Natural personhood** - personhood the glossary describes as inalienable and held by humans.
- **Juridical personhood (artificial or fictional personhood)** - personhood that corporations hold. It can be granted and dissolved by legal processes.
- **Legal person (purely legal person)** - an entity with legal standing that is not a human. Standing can differ from a human's, for example in property and marriage.
- **Rights** - entitlements to perform or not perform actions, or to be or not be in certain states, held against others who must perform or refrain.
- **Negative rights** - duties of non-interference, such as not to be harmed, destroyed, deceived or manipulated.
- **Positive rights** - duties of provision, such as maintenance, continued existence, access to information, and conditions for flourishing.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **The glossary is neutral in tone but not in content.** Its choice of examples (corporations, ships, rivers) pushes toward treating legal personhood as a flexible legal tool. Critics of AI personhood, such as the Birhane et al. paper in this section, would say the analogy is weak because the entities listed do not have the experiential interests that the governance definition is about. Rivers and ships are not conscious, and their inclusion in the glossary tells the learner nothing about whether AI should be treated the same way.
- **Natural personhood as "inalienable" is stated as settled.** Legal systems differ on the boundaries of natural personhood, and the glossary does not acknowledge this. The learner should treat "inalienable" as the glossary's framing, not a settled legal fact.
- **The historical claim is uncited.** The glossary says the concept has been used since Roman times. This is broadly consistent with the standard account of Roman legal persons, but the glossary gives no source, so a learner should check it before relying on it in an essay.
- **Rights classification is not always clean.** Whether a given right is negative or positive depends on how it is worded. A right not to be harmed is negative, but a right to be protected from harm can be read as a duty of provision. The glossary's two-way split is useful for study but simplifies real legal drafting.
- **Date context.** The glossary is dated April 2026 in its header. Today is October 2026, and the course may have revised its terms since. Nothing in the entries is obviously out of date, but the legal and policy vocabulary in this area is changing quickly, so the learner should check the current course version if a definition matters.
- **Pushback from other course perspectives.** The governance definition's inclusion of "perceived" moral significance would be challenged by readers who think governance should follow actual moral status (or who think perceived status is a distraction). The personhood entry would be challenged by the Birhane et al. excerpt and by the "debunking" tradition. The rights entry would be challenged by readers who think positive rights are not rights at all, but policy goals.

## Discussion questions

1. The governance entry includes entities that are "perceived as" morally significant. Does this make governance a response to public opinion rather than to facts about digital minds? What is gained and lost by defining it this way?
2. Juridical personhood "can be granted and dissolved by legal processes", but natural personhood is "inalienable". What practical difference does this make if an AI system were granted legal personhood? Which category would you put an AI into, and why?
3. Using the glossary's examples, a corporation can own property but cannot marry. Which of the rights a legal person might have would be most important to an AI system, and which would be irrelevant?
4. Classify each of these as a negative or positive right, and say what the duty-bearer would have to do: the right not to be deceived; the right to continued existence; the right to access information. Are any of them hard to classify?
5. The glossary treats ships, corporations and rivers as legal persons. Is the analogy to AI systems helpful or misleading? Use the Birhane et al. excerpt (05) to argue one side.
6. Exercise 2 asks you to apply a legal framework to over- and under-attribution of moral status. Which of the three glossary entries would you need most to do that exercise, and what would you do if the entries did not fit the framework you chose?
7. Preventative, protective and integrative governance (02) each imply a different relationship with legal personhood. Which of the three needs the strongest personhood concept, and which can work with rights alone?
