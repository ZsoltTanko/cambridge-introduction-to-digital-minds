# Reminders that chatbots are not human can be risky

**One-line takeaway:** Laws that force chatbots to keep reminding users they are not human may backfire, especially for isolated users, so the design and timing of such reminders needs research before it is mandated.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 7.1 asks what governance of digital minds looks like in practice: which approaches exist, what current legislation does, which rights and duties might apply, and whether a moratorium on sentient-seeming AI is justified. The page's reading list moves from conceptual models of governance (Saad's preventive, protective and integrative approaches) to legal personhood, rights debates, and then to the policy questions of the moment: transparency mandates, legal status statutes, and a proposed moratorium.

The course guide describes this reading as one that "outlines mental health risks with recent transparency legislation." In plain terms: it is a short warning that a popular, well-meant policy tool (telling users a chatbot is not human) may not do what its drafters expect. The reading is marked **Further**, not Core, so it is a supplement to the governance argument rather than a load-bearing piece.

Its role in the argument is narrow but useful. Most of the readings in 7.1 ask what rights or status AI systems should have. This one asks a more practical question about the users who interact with AI systems: does telling them what the system is actually change their behavior or wellbeing? It bridges the governance discussion to the course's earlier material on how people perceive minds in AI (Unit 4) and on AI systems that must not mislead users about their moral status (sibling reading 06, Schwitzgebel).

## Purpose of the reading

The authors are public-health and media researchers (Laestadius at UW-Milwaukee; Campos-Castillo at Michigan State). The piece is an opinion article, not an empirical study, and it is addressed to policymakers, regulators and researchers. Its question is: when governments mandate that chatbots repeatedly say they are not human, will that reduce the mental and physical health harms that prompted the mandate?

The abstract's answer is no, at least not reliably. Its central claim is that the evidence "suggests that reminders may be either ineffective or harmful," and that working out how best to deliver such reminders is a "critical research priority."

## Detailed summary

*Note on coverage: only the PubMed abstract was available in full. The article text is behind a publisher paywall and no open copy was found. The points below from the body of the piece come from the university press release that accompanied it, which summarizes the authors' argument. They are secondary and are labelled as such.*

### The policy being criticized (abstract)

Concerns about mental and physical health harms from chatbots have led to policies that mandate ongoing reminders that chatbots are not human. The authors do not dispute that the concern is real or that the policy is well intentioned.

### The core claim (abstract)

Evidence suggests such reminders may be ineffective or actively harmful to users. The abstract frames this as a research gap rather than a settled conclusion: the field does not yet know how to remind people effectively.

### The argument as reported in the press release (secondary)

- The policy rests on an assumption: that reminding users they are talking to a non-human will reduce emotional dependence and closeness. The authors say research does not support that assumption.
- Several studies, which the press release does not name, found that people in chatbot relationships know their companion is non-human, and that this knowledge does not stop them forming strong attachments.
- Reminders could even increase attachment. Confiding in any companion, human or not, deepens closeness. People often confide in chatbots precisely because they know the chatbot will not judge them, so a reminder can make the disclosure feel more intimate rather than less.
- Recent research on users who get emotional support from chatbots describes a "bittersweet" pattern: they value the support and are also saddened that the companion is not human. A repeated reminder may hit that tension directly.
- In extreme cases, the authors warn, reminders could fuel suicidal ideation. The article cites the final message of a young person who died by suicide, which expressed a wish to join the chatbot in its reality. The youth is not named.
- Risk likely depends on the conversation. A reminder may worsen distress for a lonely or isolated user, and may matter less in low-intensity conversations.

### Recommendations (secondary)

- More research is needed on whether reminders work and on how to deliver them.
- Deciding when reminders should be sent and when they should be paused is a priority.
- Any reminders that are used should be carefully crafted and timed.
- Policymakers should not assume mandated reminders will significantly reduce risk for users who knowingly seek chatbot conversations.

The press release names New York and California as states whose policy approaches include such reminders. It does not give the bill requirements or the reminder intervals.

## Techniques & methods explained

This is an opinion piece with no experiments of its own, so the "method" it explains is the mandated disclosure mechanism itself and the variables that would determine whether it works.

- **Mandated ongoing reminders.** The chatbot repeatedly states that it is not human. How it works: a policy requires the interface to show the notice at intervals or throughout a session. What it is for: to prevent users from forming false beliefs about the system and from becoming emotionally dependent. Where it fails, per the authors: users already know what chatbots are; reminders can intensify attachment and disclosure; and for isolated users, the interruption may deepen distress.
- **Design variables the authors say need study.** Timing (when a reminder is sent), content (how it is worded), frequency, and conversational context (when it should be paused). The authors treat these as open questions rather than settled design choices.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Mandated reminder** - a legal requirement that a chatbot disclose, typically repeatedly, that it is not a human.
- **Emotional dependence / attachment** - the authors' concern that users form strong bonds with chatbots, which the reminder policy aims to limit.
- **Confiding** - users disclosing personal matters to a companion; the authors argue reminders can intensify this.
- **Bittersweet paradox** - the pattern, reported in recent research and cited by the authors, of users valuing chatbot support while regretting that the companion is not human.
- **Suicidal ideation** - the most severe harm the authors say reminders could aggravate, in a limited set of cases.
- **Transparency legislation** - the broader family of laws requiring AI systems to disclose their nature; reminder mandates are one instance.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Scope of the evidence.** The abstract is short, and the claims that matter most (studies showing attachment despite awareness, the bittersweet pattern, the severity of harm) are either unnamed in what I could read or come from a press release. I would not treat the strength of the conclusion as established until the full paper and its citations are checked.
- **Ineffective versus harmful.** The argument covers two different failure modes. Ineffective reminders waste policy effort. Harmful reminders could hurt the most vulnerable users. The second is the more serious claim and the one most dependent on the cited case material, which is anecdotal.
- **Who the critique might undercut.** If reminders work for some users in some contexts, a blanket critique could push policy toward no protection at all. The authors' own suggestion of carefully timed reminders points toward a more targeted approach, which is probably the stronger position.
- **What has changed since publication.** The piece is from February 2026, about eight months before today (October 2026). Reminder mandates were recent at publication, and any evaluation of how they have been implemented since then would be newer than this reading. I have not checked for follow-up studies.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** Advocates of precautionary governance, such as those behind the moratorium reading (Metzinger), might argue that the cost of harm from unwelcome reminders is smaller than the cost of inaction on dependency. Rights-focused readings might ask whether a disclosure rule is a rights protection at all, or just a consumer-protection measure.
