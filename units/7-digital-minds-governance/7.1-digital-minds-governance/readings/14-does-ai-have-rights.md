# Does AI have rights?

**One-line takeaway:** Sunstein argues that whether AI deserves moral and legal rights turns on one question, whether it can actually experience emotions (not merely mimic them), and that emotional capacity is both necessary and sufficient for rights.

## Where this sits in the course

**What the section is trying to establish.** Section 7.1 is the opening section of Unit 7, which asks what approaches to digital minds governance exist, what legislation and policy frameworks are relevant, and which rights and duties might apply to digital systems. The unit's learning goals include listing potential rights (positive/negative, private/public) and liabilities, and appraising the arguments for and against a moratorium or ban on creating sentient or seemingly sentient AI. Section 7.1 therefore starts from the normative question of whether AI should hold rights at all, before moving to legal personhood, governance models and legislation.

**What the course guide says about why to read it.** The guide says the reading "discusses potential reasons for granting AIs rights, and methods for reasoning under moral uncertainty." It is marked **Further**, not Core, so it is optional depth rather than required reading for the section.

**The role this reading plays.** Sunstein gives a concrete, one-criterion answer to the "should AI have rights" question. Most other 7.1 readings either argue that AI rights are a useful policy tool (Salib & Goldstein, "AI Rights for Human Safety") or reject AI rights (Birhane et al.). Sunstein sits in a different place: he takes the moral question seriously and locates the answer in a single property, emotional experience. The reading therefore gives learners a clean, testable-in-principle criterion to set against the other proposals in the section. It is also the only reading in the section that centres on the epistemic problem of not knowing whether an AI has the relevant inner states.

**Which part is assigned.** The whole piece is assigned, with no excerpt.

## Purpose of the reading

Sunstein is arguing for a specific account of moral and legal standing for AI systems. He asks whether AI can have emotions such as sadness, pleasure, regret, anxiety, joy and distress. If it cannot, he says it has no moral rights and should receive legal rights only for practical reasons. If it can, he says it has moral rights and should also receive legal rights. The audience is broad and public-facing, since the material appeared in SSRN and in essay form on Substack, and it is addressed to legal scholars and general readers as well as philosophers. The question it answers is: what property must a being have for it to be a rights-holder, and does that property apply to AI?

## Detailed summary

The following is reconstructed from the secondary accounts listed above.

### Framing the question
Sunstein presents the question "does AI have rights" as one that depends on whether AI can feel. He says the question is not empirical in the usual sense, because there is "no fact of the matter" that can be checked by observation. He presents his view as a picture that readers may find congenial, not as a settled proof. He also says openly that a focus on emotion is contested.

### Bentham as the starting point
The argument begins from a footnote in Jeremy Bentham's 1780 work. Bentham's line, quoted in Sunstein's drafts, is that the question is not whether beings can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer. Sunstein uses this to make two points:
- Reasoning and language are not necessary for rights. Horses, dogs and infants lack these capacities in the relevant sense, yet a suffering being plausibly deserves protection.
- Reasoning and language are not sufficient for rights either. He offers a laptop as an example. A laptop may appear to talk and reason, but he does not think it has rights. He notes that this point is itself contested.

Sunstein then challenges "speciesist" positions (only humans matter; humans matter far more than other beings; animals matter but AI does not even if it can suffer). He asks their defenders to state the ground for the view.

### The amended Benthamite criterion
Sunstein amends Bentham's test from "can they suffer" to "can they experience emotions, and what emotions can they experience." Under this amendment:
- A being that cannot suffer may still deserve protection if it can feel pleasure, joy, boredom or rapture.
- Rocks, paper and similar objects have no moral rights, and we owe them no duties. He notes that some "deep ecology" thinkers may disagree.
- Emotional capacity is probably a necessary condition for rights. Nature may still receive legal protections for practical reasons, but that is not the same as moral rights.

### Applying the criterion to AI
- **Mimicry earns nothing.** An AI that imitates emotion, or imitates a sense of self, does not get rights from that imitation.
- **Self-protective behaviour earns nothing.** An airplane can protect itself without having rights, and so can an AI.
- **Genuine feeling earns rights.** If an AI actually feels pleasure, pain, joy or sadness, Sunstein says it is entitled to moral rights and, he suggests, to legal rights as well.
- **Content of rights is open.** He suggests that at minimum such an AI would have a right to be free from cruelty and abuse. Possibly it would have a right not to suffer and a right to life. He suggests it would not necessarily have rights to privacy, to own guns or to vote. Conflicts between AI and human or animal interests would need adjudication, and humans keep a right of self-defence.

### Alternative criteria and why he rejects them
The drafts list self-awareness, memory and continuity, reasoning and agency as alternative grounds for rights. Sunstein argues that these fit poorly with people's firm judgments in related cases. For example, a calculator can reason, but it is not a rights-holder. An agent that cares about nothing does not seem to warrant rights. He presents these as rhetorical questions.

### Using a model's own answer
In the Substack drafts, Sunstein quotes a Claude model's answer to the question of whether it has emotions. The model said it was uncertain. It separated three uncertainties: whether any inner experience exists, whether its self-reports are accurate, and whether human emotion concepts apply at all. Sunstein says he found this answer striking. The comments under the draft dispute whether the model's report is evidence of anything.

## Techniques & methods explained

The reading is argumentative rather than empirical, so the moves below are the argumentative and conceptual methods it uses.

- **Amending a classic criterion (Bentham's test).** Start from a widely accepted moral threshold (suffering), show it is neither necessary nor sufficient for rights in the usual sense, and replace it with a broader threshold (emotional experience). Where it works: it keeps the intuition that felt welfare matters, and it explains why the criterion has to be about what a being feels. Where it fails: the amendment needs a theory of what counts as an emotion, and the reading gives only examples.
- **Separating genuine feeling from mimicry and behaviour.** Sunstein's test is about whether an emotion is actually experienced, not whether a system's output looks emotional. This blocks the easy argument from "it says it feels" to "it has rights". Where it fails: it requires a way to tell the two apart from outside, which the reading does not supply. He says the question has "no fact of the matter" that can be checked, so the test cannot be applied as a clear check.
- **Argument by counterexample.** He tests rival criteria (reasoning, language, self-awareness, agency) against cases such as laptops, calculators and people who care about nothing. A criterion that gets such cases wrong is rejected.
- **Stating the conclusion conditionally.** The thesis is a biconditional: rights follow if and only if the capacity is present. This makes the argument clear but leaves the empirical question to someone else.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Aspect | As presented in the reading (secondary accounts) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Clear criterion | One property (emotional capacity) decides moral rights | Simple, and it lines up with suffering-focused ethics |
| Anti-mimicry | Imitated emotion or self-protection earns no rights | Stops rights being granted on surface behaviour |
| Bentham link | Grounds the view in a well-known utilitarian tradition | Critics say Sunstein reads Bentham too narrowly |
| Acknowledged limits | He says the emotion focus is contested | Some readers will reject it outright |
| Empirical access | Says the question is not empirical | This also means it cannot be settled by testing |
| Open content | Leaves the actual content and limits of AI rights unspecified | The policy consequences are therefore not worked out |

**Criticisms reported in the accounts.** One critic argues that Sunstein discounts self-consciousness, reasoning and agency, and questions whether emotions can count without awareness of them. Another critic argues that Bentham meant pleasure to matter alongside suffering in assessing moral status, which makes Sunstein's amendment less of a departure than he suggests. Conservatives about AI consciousness, as relayed by Eric Schwitzgebel, reportedly regard such claims as ridiculous and possibly dangerous.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Moral rights** - rights a being has in virtue of its own properties, independently of any law that grants them.
- **Legal rights** - rights given by law, which Sunstein says may be granted to AI for practical reasons even without emotional capacity.
- **Emotional capacity (as used here)** - the ability to actually feel states such as joy, sadness, regret, anxiety or distress, as opposed to producing emotion-like outputs.
- **Necessary and sufficient condition** - the claim that emotion is both required for rights and enough for them.
- **Mimicry** - producing outputs that look like emotion or self-awareness without the underlying experience. Sunstein says this earns no rights.
- **Speciesism** - giving moral weight to a being only because of its species membership. Sunstein uses the term to describe positions that deny AI rights despite its capacity to suffer.
- **Rights-holder** - a being that is owed duties or protections for its own sake.
- **Deep ecology** - a view that gives intrinsic value to nature and ecosystems. Sunstein mentions it as a possible challenge to his Benthamite framing.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

The reading is argumentative, so it has no experimental data. Its concrete examples are:

- **Bentham's 1780 footnote**, which Sunstein quotes as the starting point for the suffering criterion (secondary accounts).
- **Horses, dogs and infants**, cited as beings that lack reasoning or language in the relevant sense yet plausibly deserve protection.
- **A laptop that can talk and reason**, cited as a case that shows reasoning is not sufficient for rights.
- **An airplane that protects itself**, cited as an example of self-protective behaviour that earns no rights.
- **A rock or a sheet of paper**, cited as objects that lack moral rights.
- **A Claude model's expressed uncertainty about its own emotions**, in the Substack drafts. The model separated three uncertainties (inner experience, accuracy of self-report, applicability of human emotion concepts).
- **The "no fact of the matter" claim**, his statement that the question of AI rights is not one that verification can settle (from the APA Blog summary).

All of these are attributed to Sunstein through the secondary accounts. They are not quotations from the final paper.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Assumption: emotion is the right threshold.** Sunstein assumes that moral rights track felt emotion. Many views would say that a suffering-based threshold, a welfare-based threshold, or a relational threshold (what the AI does and how others relate to it) is better. The reading only partly answers this.
- **The test may be unapplicable.** If, as Sunstein says, the question has "no fact of the matter," then the criterion cannot guide decisions, because no test can show whether an AI has the relevant emotions. This is the most serious practical gap. Learners should ask what a policymaker would do with a criterion that cannot be checked.
- **Mimicry versus feeling is hard to draw.** The distinction between genuine feeling and imitation of feeling is central to his argument, yet the accounts I could access do not explain how to draw it from the outside. The Claude example makes this concrete: the model's self-report is exactly the evidence that cannot settle the question.
- **Bentham may be read selectively.** Critics argue that Bentham's own view gave weight to pleasure and to suffering together, and that Sunstein's extension is less of a break from Bentham than he presents it. This matters because the authority of his frame partly rests on the Bentham link.
- **Other criteria are dismissed quickly.** Rejecting self-awareness, reasoning and agency on the basis of intuitions about calculators and uncaring agents is a contested move. Those criteria have their own defenders, and the dismissal may be too fast.
- **Recency.** The reading was posted in March 2026 and the course is being read in October 2026. It is a recent draft that the author says will be refined and published in *The Futures Review*. Its specific claims may therefore already have changed, and learners should check the final published version.
- **Pushback from other course perspectives.** Birhane et al. would argue that rights framings like Sunstein's can distort more than they clarify. Salib and Goldstein would say that the welfare question and the safety question should not be kept separate. Schwitzgebel's moral-confusion concern would say that a biconditional based on emotion may make users overattribute feeling to systems that merely mimic it, which is a risk Sunstein's own mimicry point partly addresses.

## Discussion questions

1. Sunstein says emotional capacity is both necessary and sufficient for rights. Which is harder to defend: the necessity claim or the sufficiency claim? Give a case for each.
2. If "there is no fact of the matter" about whether AI feels, can the emotion criterion guide any real governance decision? What would a policymaker do differently under Sunstein's view than under a view that gives AI no rights?
3. Bentham's test asks whether beings can suffer. Sunstein asks whether they can experience emotions in general. What is gained and lost by widening the test from suffering to pleasure, boredom and rapture?
4. The reading separates genuine feeling from mimicry and self-protection. How, in practice, would you tell these apart for a system such as a language model? Use the Claude example from the drafts.
5. Compare Sunstein's rights-on-welfare view with Salib and Goldstein's rights-for-safety view. Could both be true at once? Would they recommend the same governance approach?
6. The course guide links this reading to moral uncertainty. How should one decide whether to grant AI rights when the criterion itself is uncertain? Relate this to the unit's approaches-under-uncertainty material.
7. Exercise 1 asks you to rate preventive, protective and integrative approaches. Where would Sunstein's criterion push you on the protective approach, and where would it leave the other two untouched?
