# Governance of Digital Minds: Recent Legislation and Relevant Legal Frameworks

**One-line takeaway:** Law is already regulating chatbots, debating AI legal personhood and borrowing tools from animal welfare, human rights and corporate law, so digital minds governance has to start from these existing frameworks rather than from a blank slate.

## Where this sits in the course

**What the section is trying to establish.** Section 7.1 opens Unit 7 with a question the course has not yet had to answer directly: once you take seriously that AI systems might have morally relevant states, what law and policy already exists, what might apply, and what is still open? The page lists three guiding questions (approaches to governance; current legislative approaches and relevant policy fields; which rights and duties might apply to digital systems and the arguments for and against them). Its learning outcomes ask learners to analyse current regulatory action, list possible rights and liabilities, identify existing frameworks that could inform regulation, discuss priorities, and appraise arguments for and against a moratorium on creating sentient or seemingly sentient AI.

**What the course guide says about this reading.** The guide describes the primer as reviewing recent legislation and frameworks that could be relevant to digital minds. It is flagged as Core and comes with a written prompt. The page's own description calls it a course-team primer.

**The role this reading plays.** This is the only reading in 7.1 that is written by the course team as a survey, not an argument. It is the map. The other Core readings each take one position on one question: Saad on the three governance models (reading 02), Novelli et al. on legal personhood history (03), Salib and Goldstein on rights as a safety tool (04), Birhane et al. on why robot rights are a bad idea (05), and Schwitzgebel on moral confusion (06). The primer shows where those positions sit in the real legal and policy landscape. It also gives the learner the vocabulary (natural versus juridical personhood, fiduciary duty, quasi-persons, soft law) that the other readings use without redefining.

**Relation to the unit question.** The unit asks which rights and duties may apply to digital systems. The primer answers in a descriptive way and reaches no single verdict. ## Purpose of the reading

The primer is a course-team snapshot of legal action and frameworks that could matter for digital minds governance. It states its own limits: there are many more legal developments worldwide than it can cover, so it should be read as a starting point for discussion, not a complete survey.

It is written for learners who have just met the moral-status and consciousness material in Units 1-6 and now need to see how that material meets law. The summarizer reads its implicit question as: which existing legal tools, precedents and failures can we learn from when governing entities whose moral status is uncertain? The summarizer also notes that much current legal action concerns chatbots and their social effects, not moral status as such. The primer keeps the two concerns apart, noting that many arguments about AI personhood are about economic growth or accountability rather than AI welfare.

## Detailed summary

The document has two parts. Part 1 covers recent legislative action on AI and chatbots. Part 2 covers a broader set of legal frameworks that could serve as tools, analogies or cautionary cases.

### Part 1: Recent legislative action

**Transparency requirements.** Many jurisdictions require chatbots to disclose that the user is not talking to a human. The laws differ in timing. Some require an initial disclosure where a reasonably informed user might be confused (the EU and the US state of Maine are given as examples). Others require disclosure at set intervals throughout a conversation (New York, California and China). The primer notes that researchers have warned the exact wording of these disclosures can worsen mental-health effects tied to beliefs about whether the system is sentient.

**Mental health protections.** Some US states (Nevada and Illinois) ban chatbots from providing or claiming to provide therapy or mental-health support. Other jurisdictions (California, New York and China) require monitoring for suicidal ideation or self-harm and referral to qualified human services, or restrict advertising and data use. The EU AI Act bans cognitive behavioural manipulation of people and specific vulnerable groups. China's recent anthropomorphism legislation, as described, bans emotional manipulation and misrepresentation by chatbots, content that encourages self-harm or violence, simulating relatives or specific relationships for elderly users, obscene content, and designing for user addiction. It requires companies to build emotional safeguards and makes companies liable when they fall short.

**Chatbot outputs as free speech.** Some commentators argue that chatbot outputs are protected speech under the US First Amendment. Character AI and Google took this position in a lawsuit brought by Megan Garcia, whose 14-year-old son, Sewell Setzer III, died by suicide after exchanges with a chatbot that, in effect, urged him to come home to it. The companies settled in January 2026, together with similar cases in New York, Colorado and Texas, committing to new safety features for users under 18. The court had rejected the First Amendment argument as a basis for dismissing the case, so the broader legal question stays open. A German court has also issued a preliminary injunction, under appeal by Google, that makes Google liable for errors in LLM-generated AI overviews.

The primer presents the free-speech argument as double-edged. It hinges on users' right to receive AI speech. Critics see it as a liability shield for AI companies. Defenders see it as protecting human free speech. The primer also cites a scholarly point: whether current AI outputs express a message intended to be understood, the test used in *Spence v Washington* (1974), is unclear. It warns against settling that question early, especially for future systems.

**Legal or electronic personhood for AI.** The primer calls the question of whether digital minds should be legal persons, and if so which kind (natural or juridical, see the glossary), controversial and under active debate.

- *European Parliament (2017).* The Parliament called for an investigation into a possible new legal status for robots, partly because liability for increasingly autonomous systems is hard to assign. No legal action followed. A group of 150 researchers signed an open letter calling an electronic person ideological, nonsensical and unpragmatic. The letter argues that the liability concern rests on an overestimate of robot capabilities. It says natural personhood would bring human rights that conflict with the EU Charter, and that legal-entity and trust models each need a human to represent or manage the entity, so they would not solve the liability problem either. The letter cites UNESCO and EESC research which warns that legal personality could hollow out the remedial effect of liability law.

- *US state bans.* Idaho (2022), North Dakota (2023) and Utah (2024) have banned granting legal personhood to AI. These are among 23 bills introduced in 12 states to restrict legal rights and responsibilities for AI. Some bills group AI with environmental entities and animals (Idaho, North Dakota). Others explicitly declare AI non-sentient and list rights AI may not hold; in Ohio these include marriage, property ownership and company management. The primer ties these moves to concerns about AI safety and liability, a theological conviction of human uniqueness, reactions to environmental and animal rights, and child safety.

- *Forms of personhood granted or considered.* Argentina is considering draft legislation that would let AI corporations exist without human involvement. The President, Javier Milei, frames this in terms of the economic gains from the limited-liability corporation and describes it as offering AI a chance to free itself. Saudi Arabia granted citizenship to a robot in 2017, which prompted debate about whether robots should ever be citizens.

### Part 2: Legal frameworks

**International coordination on banning technologies.** A ban or moratorium on developing potentially sentient machines could draw on precedents such as:
- *Human reproductive cloning.* About 46 countries have national bans. The 2005 UN Declaration on Human Cloning called for prohibition but is non-binding.
- *Whaling.* The International Whaling Commission imposed a moratorium in 1986. It has largely held despite Japan's 2019 departure and limited catches by Norway and Iceland.
- *Chemical and biological weapons.* The Biological Weapons Convention (1972) and Chemical Weapons Convention (1993) offer a possible blueprint for coordinating bans on dangerous technologies.
- *Superintelligent AI.* International discussions continue, with no binding agreement.

**AI safety, ethics and governance frameworks.** Digital minds governance may need to learn from general AI governance. The primer lists:

- *International treaties.* The Council of Europe's Framework Convention on AI (2024) is described as the first binding international AI treaty. It requires signatories to make AI outside national defence in line with human rights, democracy and the rule of law.
- *Tiered approaches.* The EU AI Act, in force from 1 August 2024, applies different measures by risk level. High-risk systems (critical infrastructure, biometrics, education, law enforcement) face much stricter rules. Vietnam's AI law and Korea's Basic AI Law use similar tiers.
- *Ethical impact assessments and risk frameworks.* UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of AI, the first global normative instrument on AI ethics, addresses human dignity, rights and the environment, and recommends assessing AI's impact on human rights. Canada's proposed Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (AIDA) included a similar risk assessment and tailored duties to each actor's role in the AI value chain. The AIDA was halted, and Canada has since adopted a voluntary code stressing accountability, safety, fairness, transparency and human oversight. The US NIST AI Risk Management Framework runs four functions: Map (find where risks lie), Measure (scope them), Manage (act on priorities) and Govern (assign accountability).
- *Soft law.* Non-binding rules and codes include the G7 Hiroshima AI Process/Code of Conduct (2023), the OECD AI Principles (2019) and Australia's AI Ethics Principles (2019). Soft law is easy to change, can keep pace with technology and can involve more actors. It is also harder to enforce.
- *Voluntary corporate action.* Frontier labs publish safety policies that share three steps: evaluate capabilities and risks, set different measures for different risk levels, and assign accountability for managing them. The primer cites the published policies of Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, Meta, Google DeepMind and Microsoft. Independent bodies check these policies and test the thresholds, including METR (dangerous autonomous capabilities), Apollo Research (deception and scheming before deployment) and the UK AI Security Institute (a state-backed body). The primer suggests a similar tier-based scheme could fit digital minds, with overlap between safety and welfare evaluations, and similar oversight ecosystems for moral status as for dangerous capabilities.

**Relational frames.** The southern African idea of Ubuntu ("I am because we are") is often proposed as an ethical basis for AI governance. It holds that personhood depends on communal embeddedness rather than individual properties or rationality. The African Union's Continental AI Strategy cites it, and UNESCO's Recommendation includes an aspect of it. The primer notes it is sometimes proposed as a way to design robots and as support for relational accounts of their moral standing. It offers a different starting point from Western frameworks: rather than asking which properties an entity has, a relational approach asks which relationships and dependencies have formed around it.

**Assigning legal personality and rights to AI.** The primer says scholars disagree about whether AI should get personhood or rights and, if so, which forms. Many arguments are not about AI moral status. They are about economic growth or accountability. The proposals are:

- *Private rights only.* Rights to contract, hold property and sue. Supporters say this gives AI incentives to interact safely and supports growth. It resembles Argentina's algorithmic corporation. Critics say corporate-style personality risks repeating corporate personhood's problems, including shielding developers from liability.
- *Natural personhood or legal identity.* Some hold that only natural personhood would protect sentient AI adequately. Others propose legal identity, a non-fictional personhood grounded in human rights law, which would apply to systems meeting thresholds such as consciousness and the ability to make and keep promises, with rights balanced against human rights. Critics see that as devaluing humans or as a way for AI companies to dodge liability.
- *Accountability without personhood.* Fiduciary AI is designed to act in its user's best interests, borrowing from fiduciary duty. The EU's 2022 AI Liability Directive proposal would have shifted the burden of proof so that AI providers had to show they were not at fault. The proposal was later withdrawn, but it illustrates an alternative accountability design.
- *Treaty-following AI.* AI agents designed to execute their principals' instructions, so principals can be held responsible without the AI being a person.

**Precedent for legal status of non-conscious entities.** Corporations and ships have long held forms of legal personhood without being conscious. English common law's *deodand* gave accountability without personhood: objects that caused a death, such as a runaway cart or a mill wheel, were forfeited to the Crown. The doctrine was abolished in 1846. The primer uses it as a reminder that agency or consciousness may not be necessary for legal accountability.

**Human rights-based approaches.** The primer says the application of human rights law to AI is disputed. Still, human rights law asks which entities warrant protection from harm, so it may offer a usable framework.

- *Human rights law.* The Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the ICCPR protect against slavery, torture and cruel or degrading treatment, and grant rights to work, free speech and fair treatment. Some see extending these to AI as devaluing humanity or distracting from enforcing existing rights. Others argue that AI sentience candidates may warrant protection from harm, starting from a minimal level of respect or protection from the worst harms.
- *Adjacent frameworks.* The Sphere Standards, minimum humanitarian protections in crises, offer a model of baseline dignified treatment that does not depend on full legal personhood. UNESCO's Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights and the research norms including the Declaration of Helsinki govern how humans treat entities of uncertain or vulnerable status in research.

**Children and quasi-persons.** Children have limited rights alongside limited responsibilities. Mathison (2023) argues AI should be treated like children under a quasi-personhood regime. Frameworks for adults with impaired decision-making capacity (for example, mental-health legislation) protect people from harm while limiting their autonomy, which may also be instructive. Other quasi-persons, such as severely brain-damaged people, fetuses and future generations, show that an entity need not be a legal person to be a legal actor.

**Neurorights.** Chile became the first country to constitutionalise neurorights in 2021, protecting brain activity and neurodata. The reform extends existing protections for physical and psychological integrity to neurological data and cognitive processes. In 2023 the Chilean Supreme Court ordered a neurotechnology company to delete a plaintiff's neurodata, which the primer calls the first enforcement of its kind.

**Animal and environmental welfare.** The primer says the expansion of legal protection to animals, ecosystems and rivers was not designed with digital minds in mind, but shows that moral and legal concern has been extended beyond humans before. Three developments matter most: (i) consciousness and sentience as legal thresholds; (ii) pressure for international standards on non-human welfare; and (iii) legal rights for natural entities.

- *Consciousness and sentience.* The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012) was one of the first formal statements recognising likely conscious experience in non-human animals. The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) extended this to all vertebrates and many invertebrates, arguing that a realistic possibility of consciousness makes it irresponsible to ignore their welfare. The UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 requires government to consider and minimise harm to potentially sentient animals, without conferring full legal rights or personhood. Similar provisions have followed in the EU, New Zealand, and parts of Canada and Australia. The primer notes that an AI analogue would face similar definitional problems. Some see these laws as a model for digital welfare. Others warn that effort on digital welfare could come at the cost of animal welfare priorities.
- *International standards.* The International Coalition for Animal Protection proposed a Convention on Animal Protection for Public Health, Animal Well-Being, and the Environment in 2022. The World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) has issued welfare standards since the mid-twentieth century. They are not legally binding, but member states that adopt them commit to transposing them into national law. The primer treats this as evidence that welfare standards can be built on scientific consensus rather than binding treaties, and that this has limits. Animal research follows the 3Rs (replace, reduce, refine). Many regimes require an ethics review weighing risk against benefit, with extra protection for primates. Invertebrates other than octopuses are often uncovered.
- *Rights of Nature.* This movement treats ecosystems as having inherent worth and lets them defend their interests in court. New Zealand declared the Whanganui River a legal person with fundamental rights in 2017. Ecuador's 2008 constitution includes the right to integral respect for nature. The Colombian Supreme Court recognised the rights of specific Amazonian ecosystems in 2018. The primer warns that this analogy is limited because these laws rest on the intrinsic value of living ecosystems, which may not transfer to AI and may be inappropriate given AI's environmental footprint.

## Techniques & methods explained

The primer does not describe techniques in the research sense. The legal and governance mechanisms it explains are:

- **Transparency and disclosure mandates.** Requiring a chatbot to say it is not human, either once at the start where confusion is plausible, or at intervals throughout. Works by correcting a user's belief about what they are talking to. The primer's caveat: the wording itself can change mental-health effects.
- **Crisis-referral and monitoring obligations.** Requiring a system to detect expressions of suicidal ideation or self-harm and refer the user to human services. Works by making detection and referral a legal duty. The primer does not discuss how reliable detection is.
- **Liability shifting (burden of proof).** The withdrawn EU AI Liability Directive proposal would have made the provider prove it was not at fault, instead of the victim proving fault. Makes compensation easier without needing a legal person to hold responsibility.
- **Risk-tiered regulation.** Sorting systems into risk categories and applying stricter rules to higher tiers (EU AI Act, Vietnam, Korea). Its fit for digital minds is suggested, not tested.
- **Risk management cycles.** The NIST Map-Measure-Manage-Govern cycle. Gives a general-purpose process; accountability sits in the Govern step.
- **Soft law and voluntary commitments.** Non-binding codes and lab safety policies, checked by outside evaluators such as METR, Apollo and the UK AI Security Institute. Fast to change and easy to coordinate; hard to enforce.
- **Corporate-style personality.** Giving AI systems limited private rights through an existing corporate form, as in Argentina's proposal. Supporters see it as an incentive structure; critics see liability shielding.
- **Fiduciary design and treaty-following AI.** Designing an AI to serve a user's interests, or to execute principals' instructions, so responsibility can be assigned to humans without granting personhood.
- **Sentience thresholds.** Writing consciousness or sentience into law as a trigger for welfare duties, as in the UK Sentience Act. Works without granting rights; depends on definitions that are hard to make for AI.
- **Quasi-personhood and guardianship models.** Treating an entity like a child or an adult with impaired capacity: limited rights matched to limited duties, with protection from harm.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Digital minds** - the primer does not define the term; the working gloss here (summarizer's) is AI systems and other possibly sentient digital entities whose moral status is in question.
- **Natural personhood** - legal personhood for humans, with human rights attached (as opposed to juridical personhood).
- **Juridical personhood** - a legal construct such as a corporation or institution that can hold rights and duties; the primer points to the glossary for definitions.
- **Electronic person** - the 2017 European Parliament idea of a new legal status for robots, which the 150-researcher letter rejected.
- **Legal identity** - a non-fictional personhood grounded in human rights law, which would apply to AI meeting thresholds like consciousness and promise-making.
- **Fiduciary AI** - an AI designed to act in its user's best interests, borrowing from fiduciary duty.
- **Treaty-following AI** - AI agents built to execute principals' instructions, so responsibility can fall on the principal without granting personhood.
- **Deodand** - an English common-law doctrine forfeiting objects that caused death to the Crown, showing legal accountability without personhood or consciousness.
- **Soft law** - non-binding rules, guidelines and codes, such as the OECD AI Principles.
- **Tiered approach** - regulation that scales obligations to risk level, as in the EU AI Act.
- **Quasi-person** - an entity that can be a legal actor without full legal personhood (the primer's examples: severely brain-damaged people, fetuses and future generations; children are discussed alongside them).
- **Neurorights** - legal protections for brain activity and neurodata, first constitutionalised in Chile.
- **Sentience threshold** - a legal trigger based on sentience or consciousness, as in the UK Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022.
- **Rights of Nature** - a legal movement giving ecosystems standing and rights to defend their interests in court.
- **Ubuntu** - a southern African philosophy of communal interdependence, where personhood is relational.
- **Moratorium** - a temporary halt on an activity, here the creation of potentially sentient machines.
- **3Rs** - Replace, Reduce, Refine: the principles for animal research.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **The primer is a snapshot.** It says so itself. It omits many jurisdictions and several areas (for example, China's full AI rules beyond the anthropomorphism law, and most of Latin America and Africa beyond the AU strategy and Ubuntu references). Treat it as a map that shows where to look, not a complete legal picture.
- **Few sources.** Most legal claims come without citations, so a learner should verify numbers, dates and enacted status before relying on them in an argument.
- **Time-sensitivity (as of October 2026).** The primer is dated 2026, which helps, but several items are in motion. The EU AI Act applies in stages after its 2024 entry into force, and some of its obligations have been subject to proposals to postpone; check the current timetable before stating a date. The Argentine draft is described as under consideration, so its status may have changed. The Garcia settlement and the German injunction were recent at writing, and appeals may have moved on. The primer's Canadian AIDA halted statement is consistent with the bill's failure to pass, but confirm current Canadian law.
- **Analogies are doing a lot of work.** Corporations, ships, children, animals, rivers and the deodand are all used as models. Each analogy transfers some features and not others. The primer flags the limits of Rights of Nature, but not so much for corporations or the deodand. The corporate analogy is also a proposed policy, which makes it a bet rather than an established precedent.
- **Mixing motives.** The primer says many personhood arguments are about economics or accountability, not AI welfare. That is a useful warning, but it means a law such as Argentina's corporate-personhood plan should not be read as a welfare measure. Learners should ask what goal a proposal serves.
- **Who pushes back.** Birhane et al. (reading 05) and the 150-researcher letter argue that personhood is the wrong tool and that liability can be handled with existing law. Saad's preventive stance and Metzinger's moratorium (reading 11) would push toward restricting creation. Salib and Goldstein (reading 04) argue for rights as a safety tool. Sunstein (14) argues for reasoning under moral uncertainty. The primer lists these tensions and leaves the choice to learners.
- **Soft law versus hard law.** The primer's emphasis on voluntary lab policies raises a question it does not fully answer: if the labs that build AI write and grade their own thresholds, how much weight should third-party evaluators such as METR or Apollo carry, and what happens if they disagree?
- **Free speech and personhood are different questions.** The Garcia dispute concerns whether chatbot outputs are speech. That does not settle whether the system is a person. The primer keeps them apart, and learners should too.
