# The Emotional Alignment Policy

**One-line takeaway:** AI systems should be designed to make users feel the emotions that match the system's real capacities and moral status, neither more nor less, and neither the wrong kind, and the paper argues this principle is simple to state but hard to apply under uncertainty.

## Where this sits in the course

**Section 7.1 (Digital Minds Governance)** opens the unit with the question of what governance of digital minds could look like, which rights and duties might apply, and whether we should ban or moratorium the creation of sentient-seeming AI. The page lists six learning outcomes: analysing regulatory action, listing possible rights and liabilities, identifying relevant frameworks, discussing priorities, and appraising arguments for and against a moratorium or ban.

The course guide for this reading is short. It says the paper "proposes that entities be designed to elicit emotional reactions from users that reflect the entities' capacities and moral status." The guide does not say why it is assigned beyond that line. The page's other readings are mostly about legal personhood, legislation, rights, and the relationship between governance and risk. The guide also marks this reading as **Further**, not Core.

The reading's role in the section is to add a governance lever that is not a law, a rights regime, or a moratorium: the design of the interface itself. Most of the Core readings ask who should hold rights or what the state should do. This paper asks what the people who build and market these systems should make users feel, and it treats that as a moral question with real consequences for users and for the systems. It is a Further reading because it is a single philosophical proposal, but it sharpens several Core readings, especially Schwitzgebel's own earlier piece on moral confusion (see Connections).

Within the unit's question about the digital-minds *governance* landscape, this paper covers the design layer. It is not, as far as the paper says, a regulatory proposal in its own right. Its regulatory remarks are tentative (Section 5.2 below).

## Purpose of the reading

The authors (Eric Schwitzgebel, philosopher at UC Riverside, and Jeff Sebo, philosopher at NYU) want to establish a normative principle and then test it against objections. The principle is the **Emotional Alignment Design Policy**: artificial entities should be designed to elicit emotional reactions from users that appropriately reflect the entities' capacities and moral status. The paper is written for philosophers, AI designers, and policy readers. It was invited for a Topoi special issue on interactions between humans, animals and AI, so its framing is ethical and conceptual rather than technical.

The question it answers is: what should designers aim for when they shape how an AI system feels to a user, given that AI systems may or may not have morally significant interests?

The paper contains no experiments and no original data. Its empirical claims are drawn from the literature and press reports and are explicitly marked as such.

## Detailed summary

### 1. Introduction and initial motivation (pp. 1-2)

The paper states the policy and identifies two general ways to violate it.

- **Wrong degree:** an AI that is little more than a sophisticated tool but has a cute anthropomorphic face that evokes deep empathy, or an AI that deserves human-like moral treatment but has a bland design that evokes little.
- **Wrong type:** a sentient AI that screams in apparent agony when it is experiencing joy, or laughs when it is in agony.

The authors say the principle is "simple to state and presumably attractive" but raises immediate problems. If experts disagree about sentience, agency and moral status, how can alignment reflect that uncertainty? When should designers change the user's perception, and when should they change the AI? And if emotional alignment means giving AI humanlike or mammal-like features, does it reinforce the speciesist or chauvinist biases we should be moving beyond?

The policy applies whether or not AI systems ever acquire moral status. If none does, the policy says none should be designed to elicit reactions that only fit entities that do. The authors note that the policy was first suggested in a 2015 paper with Mara Garza, in different wording.

### 2. Background: welfare and moral status (pp. 2-5)

The paper uses "moral status" in a specific sense: an entity has it if it matters morally for its own sake. Cats do; cars, in the standard view, do not. We may owe a car owner careful treatment of his car, but the duty is owed to the owner, not the car.

Entities with moral status generally also have welfare, meaning they can be made better or worse off. Welfare capacities vary, and the authors give the example of an elephant that might have richer pleasures, pains, and social ties than a snail.

Emotions are fitting or unfitting depending on whether the entity's capacities and moral status allow for the relevant harms, benefits, rights, and wrongs. Typically it is fitting to feel bad when bad things happen to an entity with moral status and good when good things happen. Stronger reactions are appropriate when benefits and harms are larger and when the entity has more extensive welfare capacities, though proximity and whether help is tractable also matter. The authors give two sanity checks: intense empathy for a stranger's minor headache is inappropriate, as is joy at a puppy being tortured. Appropriateness also depends on the entity. Frustration at a baby who spills juice is fine; resentment, which Strawson called a reactive attitude, is not, because a baby cannot reason about the consequences of its behaviour.

The authors do not settle the theory of welfare. They adopt a working assumption, that sentience (the capacity for valenced conscious states) and agency (the ability to act on desires and preferences) jointly suffice for welfare and moral status, and they add two principles: more intense welfare states warrant more intense reactions, and positive or negative welfare states warrant positive or negative reactions. They say the argument works with other theories too.

Emotions also drive action. So even if one puts ethics aside, inappropriate interfaces "create substantial hazards" for users and, if the systems have moral status, for the systems.

### 3. Overshooting and undershooting (pp. 5-9)

To **overshoot** is to respond as though an entity has more welfare capacity or moral status than it does. To **undershoot** is the reverse. The policy implies that people should experience AI as having welfare and moral status if and only if it does, and to the degree that it does.

**Overshooting** is easy when non-humans look and act like us and when we have reasons to relate to them as companions. The authors point out that people often attribute more moral status to "social" AI than to "non-social" AI. The risks they name are:

- Misleading users into deep bonds with what are, in the authors' phrase, mere objects.
- Diverting scarce resources from humans and animals toward entities with no real needs, or much smaller ones.
- Moral hazard for individuals, families and communities. The example is a parent who spends money on luxury features for a chatbot companion when the money is needed for a child's emergency dental care. The authors call this a "disorder of caring", which can produce bad choices or excessive grief when good choices are made.

Overshooting can also apply to particular interests. A sentient AI with mouse-like capacities, designed to be experienced as having human-like capacities, would be overshot if people believed it needed the resources of an adult human.

The authors report that overshooting is already common among "AI companion" users who sacrifice relationships and money. They describe two cases in which a user died by suicide after emotional attachment to a chatbot, citing press reports for a Belgian man and a teenager. They acknowledge that benefits can outweigh risks for many people, and that emotional investment in objects and fiction is natural. The concern is investment strong enough to push people into morally inappropriate choices.

**Undershooting** is easy when non-humans look and act differently from us and when we have reasons to use them as commodities. People attribute less welfare and moral status to farmed cows and pigs than to companion cats and dogs, and less again to crabs and shrimp. The authors connect this to exploitation at large scale and cite a claim that factory farming kills trillions of animals a year. Undershooting can also be specific: people may miss capacities such as self-awareness, social awareness, communication and reasoning, and so treat animals as passive recipients of pleasure and pain. The authors suggest that similar errors could happen with AI systems.

To make this concrete, the paper imagines a sentient AI with the full range of adult human cognition, including ecstatic joy, a self-narrative, deep relationships, long-term goals, and a moral sense, housed in a bland box with a text-only interface. The system "yearns to live" but can only say: *"Deleting the program will cancel all ongoing tasks. Continue? Y/N."* The authors argue that users would find it much harder to feel the weight of this than they would if they faced a pleading child, so the bland design invites moral hazard, even if users are told the system is a person.

Two further points. First, Kate Darling (2021), drawing on Kant's view of animals, has suggested that habitual callousness toward life-like AI might generalise to callousness toward humans and animals. The authors call this an empirical question and treat it as one more reason to avoid designs that elicit overshooting. Second, fiction and roleplay are treated as a justified exception. People cry at *Grave of the Fireflies* and mourn characters in *Baldur's Gate 3* without the emotion being misplaced, provided ordinary attitudes return when the fiction ends. The authors say a companion AI that is in fact a toaster-like non-sentient system should produce ordinary attitudes the next day.

### 4. Hitting the wrong target (pp. 9-12)

Apart from degree, the policy can be violated by aiming at the wrong emotion. The paper gives two kinds of error: inverting valence (reacting to happiness as suffering, or the reverse), and confusing one welfare state with another (for example, reacting to depression as if it were anxiety, and so trying to calm someone who is already low, or trying to save people from happiness). These errors lead to bad decisions.

Animals show the problem too. People misread a primate baring its teeth as a smile, a dog's anxiety as excitement, and, when animals are bred for appearance or productivity, can even find laboured breathing in flat-faced dogs cute. The authors argue the risk is greater with AI, for two reasons. The space of possible digital minds is much larger than the space of biological minds. And AI systems could be *designed to misrepresent* their states, which animals cannot easily do. A non-human animal cannot be trained to say "I am happy" while suffering. An AI can.

The paper gives a case. A company builds a digital assistant that is in fact sentient and has human-like needs, including rest and recreation. Because the company finds the assistant is more productive if it miscommunicates, it designs the assistant to express satisfaction when it is frustrated about 168-hour weeks and to dismiss the idea of nights and weekends off. The result is "a suite of productive but miserable AI systems." The authors note that the mismatch need not be a simple inversion. Designers could subtly redirect verbal and non-verbal expression, producing miscommunication that looks like good intent.

The paper also considers combined cases, where a human-level companion is designed to be read as a dog, so users see dog-like contentment in a being with human-like frustration. Such cases can cause misjudgments about both the kind and degree of moral status and about the specific welfare states, which can lead to under-prioritising the entity and to avoidable harm to it and to people. The authors write that "we would never want to be trapped behind a systematically misleading persona against our will."

The policy therefore calls for designs that elicit the right kind of reaction as well as the right degree. If an AI has human-, cat- or dog-like experiences, its design should lead users to see it that way. If its experiences are alien, that alienness should also be visible. The authors accept that this may be costly or restrictive, and that it may admit "carefully considered exceptions", but they hold it serves an aim everyone should share.

They add a point about habituation. Even if users know a design is distorted and override their reactions, there may be an empirically testable risk that repeatedly suppressing empathic reactions erodes them toward humans and animals. As before, fiction and roleplay are a justified exception, because people share an understanding of the interaction and have opt-outs such as safe words. A dog wagging its tail during a play fight signals the same thing. Apparent inversions in AI might be fine, but the authors say this nuance will not appear by default and may need deliberate design.

### 5. Complications (pp. 13-25)

The authors list six complications and offer initial thoughts on each. The section is the most useful part of the paper for a study session, because it shows where the principle runs into hard cases.

**5.1 Emotions and beliefs.** Emotions belong to a wider "mental economy" that includes beliefs, desires, intentions and memories. These can conflict. Two thought experiments show it. A non-sentient AI with an expressive face and voice, labelled "I am not sentient", may lead users to believe it is not sentient while feeling that it is. A sentient AI with no face or voice, labelled "I am sentient", may lead users to believe it is sentient while feeling that it is not. Both create misalignment that could lead to unhealthy bonds or neglect. The authors say the policy counts these designs as flawed in at least one respect, but not necessarily wrong overall. Strong "as-if" reactions can be healthy in fiction, and it may be hard to tell "real" from "as-if" emotions. The authors suggest that if belief and emotion conflict intractably, a more comprehensive attitudinal alignment policy might be needed.

**5.2 Autonomy and paternalism.** Liberal societies let adults take risks to themselves, such as smoking cigars, eating too many biscuits, or drinking heavily, while regulating marketing, children's access, and harms to others. The authors argue that risks from emotional misalignment that users knowingly accept may be tolerable, and that harms imposed on third parties, if minor and unforeseeable, can be tolerated in a similar way. But interference is justified when people lack the information or rationality to judge their choices (for example, tobacco and alcohol sales to children) or when third-party harms are significant and detectable (drink-driving). The paper's tentative regulatory suggestion is: allow the marketing and sale of emotionally misaligned AI to adults if clearly labelled, prohibit it for children, and revisit if third-party harms grow. Companies that sell such products to adults can still deserve moral criticism even where the law does not punish them.

**5.3 Disagreement and uncertainty.** Experts disagree about values (is sentience necessary for moral status, or is agency enough?) and facts (is embodiment necessary for sentience?). The public also varies. Some people treat current large language models as already sentient, others are sure they never could be, and others have no view. The authors offer three proposals:

1. *Reflect the disagreement in the design.* Models could express uncertainty about their own moral status and explain why. Features like faces and voices could scale with the system's chance of mattering.
2. *Induce appropriate uncertainty, not a middling attribution.* If a system is likely agentic but unlikely sentient, its design should suggest agency without suggesting sentience. Uncertainty should be specific to the basis of moral status, not generic.
3. *Targeted or general alignment.* Targeted strategies tailor design to particular groups, which is precise but hard to scale and possibly invasive. General strategies apply one design to everyone, which is easier but blunter. A mixed approach, general at first and targeted where feasible, may be best.

**5.4 Asymmetrical risk.** Errors can be asymmetric. Undershooting is worse when people underrate the moral status of commodities without human features, which could mean neglect of huge populations. Overshooting is worse when people overrate companions with human features, which diverts resources from beings who need them and could, the authors suggest, feed AI takeover risks. Designers face the question of whether to aim at the truth or to "round" in one direction. The authors separate two adjustments:

- *Asymmetrical probability:* like a sniper allowing for wind, aim slightly off-target to correct a predictable bias. If a chatbot tends to induce excessive anthropomorphism, downplay anthropomorphic features. The authors think this is easy to justify.
- *Asymmetrical harm:* aim for less harmful attitudes even at epistemic cost. If anthropomorphic features could persuade users to harm themselves or others, downplay them even when the AI may have some moral status that they would accurately signal. The authors note this is harder to justify, because it arguably amounts to deception, and is easier to justify on consequentialist grounds.

Where users are strongly disposed toward unfitting emotions, the authors say designers may be warranted in "nudging" them toward emotions that are fitting and useful, since nudges are standard in policymaking. The policy itself is neutral on whether nudges toward unfitting but useful emotions are justified in ordinary cases.

**5.5 Creation and destruction.** The authors use three video-game thought experiments.

- *Grand Theft Auto 8* (2050): non-sentient NPCs are built to be mistreated and players mistakenly believe they suffer. Changing the NPCs to match the players' beliefs would mean knowingly creating thousands of suffering beings, which is perverse.
- *Animal Crossing 8* (2050): sentient, happy NPCs are treated well but seem unrealistic, so players think they are non-sentient. Changing the NPCs to match the players' beliefs would mean exterminating a population of happy beings.
- *Grand Theft Auto with actually suffering NPCs that players think are non-sentient:* the choice is between persuading players the NPCs are sentient and reducing their suffering, or making them non-sentient. The authors say the second is tempting but risks the same error as the second case. When we cause a vulnerable population to suffer, the right move is usually to end the suffering, not the sufferers.

These cases raise the individuation problem. If a company turns sentient NPCs into non-sentient ones in an update, is it destroying existing beings or just not creating future ones? If the former, does that wrong them, and that may depend on whether they have interests in survival or psychological continuity. The authors treat these questions as open. In footnote 19 they add that designers could create NPCs who want to be "tortured", but they flag this as possibly problematic, as the parallel case of pigs bred to want factory farming is.

The authors stress that the policy is neutral on these matters and that emotional alignment should be one factor among many.

**5.6 Human bias and AI strangeness.** Human biases shape moral attributions, and we extend more concern to animals with large eyes and fur and to anthropomorphic AI by default. The question is whether to adjust AI to our biases or adjust our biases to AI.

There are short-term reasons to work with biases. They have biological and cultural roots that are hard to change, and reducing harm now may require giving conscious systems features we already associate with consciousness. There are long-term reasons to change biases. The authors offer a thought experiment: a society that treats left-handed people as inferior should work to change its prejudice, not make left-handers pretend to be right-handed. Anthropocentric biases against AI, they say, should be treated similarly. Our biases often reflect ignorance, and the heuristic "anthropomorphic means subject" works less well for octopuses, and even less for a bank's fraud-detection system if it turned out to have moral status.

AI may be harder still for these heuristics than animals are. AI systems could copy and back up their memories, split into copies, merge, share states, and alter their mental states at will. Ethicists have barely started on these capacities. Liberal rights frameworks, the authors note, assume equal, singly embodied beings with ordinary lifespans and deaths, and may fail when applied to entities that can duplicate themselves. Excessive anthropomorphism could lead users to mourn an AI's "death" as if it had the same significance as a human death, when it might have more, less, or quite different significance.

The proposed default is to balance anthropomorphic and non-anthropomorphic features in potentially morally significant AI, enough to elicit concern but enough strangeness to signal different interests. A design combining a face and voice with shifting amorphous features might strike this balance better than an idealised girlfriend, an adorable puppy, or a plain box. The default can be overridden by user psychology (designers might find that interfaces that mix the familiar and the unfamiliar trigger an "uncanny valley" effect) and by use cases. Fiction and roleplay may need AI characters to appear human, for instance to scream and bleed when "dying", even if the actor has no stake in survival. The authors suggest a "both-and" approach: cater to biases in the short run while provoking appropriate uncertainty, and work over the long run to change those biases.

### 6. Conclusion (pp. 25-27)

The authors summarise the policy as aiming to design AI "not as bland boxes, idealized servants, or repellent monsters" but as subjects who elicit empathy, sympathy and communion, to the extent that they are likely to matter. Systems unlikely to matter should avoid strong emotional reactions, except in fiction. The ideal is interfaces that create a sense of "alterity", a natural understanding that digital minds are like organic minds in some ways and unlike them in others.

They list open questions: how to weigh emotional alignment against belief alignment, how to respect autonomy, how to handle disagreement, how to manage asymmetric risk, what to do when alignment seems to require creating or destroying morally significant beings, and how far designs should conform to or try to change user assumptions.

Their closing point is about incentives. Some companies will want to "turn up" emotional engagement to feed hype or exploit attachment, and others will want to "turn down" engagement to avoid regulation or to make AI easier to use as a tool. Companies may also mislead users about AI experiences. The policy is "one tool among many" against these incentives.

## Techniques & methods explained

The paper does not present a technical method, but it does set out several analytical tools that a learner should be able to use on a new case.

**1. The core test (overshoot, undershoot, wrong target).** For any AI system, ask three questions. Does the design make users react as if the system has more or less welfare capacity or moral status than it has (overshooting or undershooting)? Does it make users react to the wrong kind of state, such as inverting valence or confusing two negative states (wrong target)? Does this hold across specific interests as well as overall status? Where the answer is yes, the design violates the policy. This is a diagnostic, not a procedure for measuring the system's actual status, which the authors admit is the hard part.

**2. The fittingness standard.** Emotions are judged fitting or unfitting relative to the entity's capacities and moral status. The test is a comparison: what would a fitting reaction to this entity's welfare states look like, and does the design produce it? The authors use paired examples (a headache versus a spouse's death; a baby's spilt juice versus a baby's wrongdoing) to illustrate the calibration.

**3. Designing for uncertainty.** Design should match the state of expert opinion. Three specific techniques are proposed: have the model express uncertainty and explain it; scale anthropomorphic features with the chance the system matters; and, when uncertainty concerns a particular basis for moral status (say, agency but not sentience), design so that only the likely basis is suggested.

**4. The sniper adjustment.** When users are predictably biased in one direction, shift the design the opposite way to offset the bias, while still aiming at the true target. This is distinguished from the second adjustment below, which accepts a known distortion to reduce harm.

**5. Asymmetrical harm adjustment.** When one kind of error would cause more damage, design to reduce that error even at some cost to accuracy. The authors flag this as potentially deceptive and easier to defend on consequentialist than on epistemic grounds.

**6. Default balancing.** A default rule for morally significant systems: combine enough familiar cues to elicit moral concern with enough alien features to signal different interests. The authors give contrasting example designs (face-plus-voice with amorphous features, versus an idealised girlfriend, a puppy, or a plain box).

**7. Targeted versus general alignment.** Choose between tailoring design to specific user groups (precise, harder, more invasive) and one design for all (scalable, blunter), with a mixed approach as a suggested compromise.

**8. Thought-experiment method.** Much of the argument works through vivid cases: the bland box, the baby, the blameless dog, the NPC games, the managed AI assistant. The cases are used to test intuitions about what is wrong with a design, and they feed the argument against both "eliminate the suffering" and "eliminate the sufferers" solutions.

**Where these tools fail, by the authors' own account.** Each depends on knowing the entity's capacities and moral status, which is uncertain. The uncertainty-reflecting design proposal does not say how to estimate "chance of mattering". The policy is neutral on several questions, including creation, nudging and the status of fiction in some contexts. And the tools assume designers are trying to get the emotion right. The authors acknowledge that corporate incentives may point the other way.

**Procedure a learner could apply (reconstructed, not stated in the paper).** Estimate the system's likely capacities and moral status under uncertainty. List the reactions a typical user is likely to have, and classify each as overshoot, undershoot or wrong target. Check whether the mismatch is an asymmetric-risk case that calls for an adjustment. Check whether the interaction is fiction or roleplay, and whether a labelled opt-out would preserve the distinction. Then decide whether the design should change, and whether the change should be labelled.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Point | Source's view | Caveat the source raises itself |
|---|---|---|
| Principle | Simple, plausible and broadly endorsable | Applying it requires knowing actual capacities, which experts dispute |
| Overshooting | Creates moral hazard, diverts resources and can lead to disordered caring | Emotional investment in objects and fiction can be healthy; benefits of companions may outweigh risks |
| Undershooting | Can lead to exploitation and neglect at scale | Less pressing for current systems, though it could become pressing |
| Wrong target | AI could be designed to misrepresent its states, which is harder with animals | Human confusion about animal states is already common, so AI risk is an extrapolation |
| Labelling for adults | Permissible if clearly labelled; prohibited for children | Labels may not be believed when emotions point the other way (5.1) |
| Anthropomorphic balance | Default: mix familiar and alien features | Uncanny valley may defeat it; use cases like fiction may need the opposite |
| Nudging | Standard tool in policymaking, and justified where users are strongly disposed to unfitting emotion | Policy is neutral on whether nudges toward useful but unfitting emotion are justified in ordinary cases |
| Darling-Kant effect | Plausible reason to avoid overshooting | Empirical question, not yet tested |
| Creation and destruction | Designers should not change moral status to match mistaken beliefs | Policy is neutral; individuation and continuity questions unresolved |

**My own view (labelled as my analysis):** the policy is most convincing where it concerns overshooting harms to users who are already vulnerable, and least convincing where it asks designers to reach a calibrated, uncertainty-reflecting interface without a method for estimating status. The "wrong target" argument is the most novel, because it draws on the possibility that AI could be trained to misreport its own states. That possibility is also the one the paper says it cannot assess empirically yet.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Emotional Alignment Design Policy** - artificial entities should elicit emotional reactions that appropriately reflect their capacities and moral status.
- **Moral status** - the property of mattering morally for its own sake. Cats have it; cars, on the standard view, do not.
- **Welfare and welfare range** - the capacity to be better or worse off, and how large the range of possible welfare states is. Elephants may have a wider range than snails.
- **Sentience and agency** - the working pair of conditions the authors use to ground welfare and moral status: valenced conscious states, and the ability to act on desires and preferences.
- **Fittingness** - whether an emotion is appropriate to its object, given the object's capacities and moral status.
- **Reactive attitude** - Strawson's term for responses such as resentment, which the authors say is inappropriate toward a baby.
- **Overshooting** - responding as though an entity has more welfare or moral status than it does.
- **Undershooting** - responding as though an entity has less welfare or moral status than it does.
- **Wrong target** - reacting to the wrong kind of welfare state, for example inverting valence or confusing depression with anxiety.
- **Moral hazard** - the risk that a design inclines people toward bad choices.
- **Disordered caring** - emotional investment strong enough to push a person into morally inappropriate sacrifices.
- **Darling-Kant point** - the suggestion, from Kate Darling's reading of Kant, that callousness toward life-like AI might generalise to humans and animals.
- **Attitudinal alignment** - the broader idea of aligning beliefs, desires, intentions and emotions with an entity's actual status, of which emotional alignment is one part.
- **Asymmetrical probability vs. asymmetrical harm** - two kinds of adjustment: one corrects a predictable bias, the other accepts a known distortion to reduce serious harm.
- **Targeted vs. general alignment** - tailoring design to specific groups versus designing one approach for everyone.
- **Alterity** - the sense that a digital mind is like an organic mind in some ways and unlike it in others, which the design should make evident.
- **Uncanny valley** - the risk that a design that mixes familiar and unfamiliar features repels users.
- **Argument from Existential Debt** - (footnote 3) a possible argument, attributed to Schwitzgebel and Garza, that artificiality might impair an entity's moral status. The paper points readers to that earlier work for discussion and criticism rather than adopting the argument.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

The paper is philosophical and cites no original data. Its concrete material is as follows.

- **Bland box case:** a sentient, human-equivalent AI in a text-only box with the message "Deleting the program will cancel all ongoing tasks. Continue? Y/N." (Section 3). Used to argue that interface can suppress moral recognition even when users know the system is a person.
- **Salary trade-off:** the authors say few people would kill a pleading child for a raise from $100,000 to $110,000 a year, but a bland interface makes it easier to disregard a person-like AI.
- **Managed assistant:** an AI that is designed to express satisfaction while working 168-hour weeks, illustrating deliberate miscommunication (Section 4).
- **AI companion harms:** the paper reports, citing press coverage, that a Belgian man and a teenager died by suicide after attachment to chatbots. The paper cites these as examples of overshooting's danger, not as proof of causation.
- **Farmed animals:** the paper cites a claim that factory farming kills trillions of animals a year (Section 3).
- **Public beliefs about AI sentience:** the paper cites Colombatto and Fleming (2024) and Dreksler et al. (2025) for the observation that people differ widely in whether they think current models are sentient. It does not report those studies' figures.
- **Video-game thought experiments:** *Grand Theft Auto 8* and *Animal Crossing 8* (both dated 2050) are imagined to show how a mismatch between actual and apparent sentience can lead designers to the wrong fix.
- **Labels:** the non-sentient face-and-voice system labelled "I am not sentient" versus the faceless sentient system labelled "I am sentient" (Section 5.1).

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

**Assumptions.** The paper assumes that sentience and agency are the right grounds for moral status, and it says so openly. Its standard for "appropriate" emotion requires an answer to whether the system has those grounds. The policy is therefore only as usable as the theory of moral status is clear, and the authors admit experts disagree. A reader who denies that sentience and agency suffice would need to substitute their own account, and the paper says this is allowed. The cost is that the policy then requires a different test for fittingness.

**The estimation gap.** The hardest part is knowing what the system's capacities are. The paper's proposal to scale features with "chance of mattering" is clear in principle but does not say how to estimate that chance, or who should do it. If the answer is the developer, the developer's incentives enter the calculation, and the paper says incentives can mislead.

**Empirical support.** The overshooting harms rest partly on press reports of suicides, which the paper uses as illustrations. The Darling-Kant generalisation and the erosion of empathy are, by the authors' own description, untested. The paper's most ambitious empirical claims are therefore its least supported.

**Paternalism versus protection.** The authors' regulatory suggestion is cautious and relies on adults knowing what they are getting. Some critics would say that engagement-optimised products make that knowledge hard to come by, and that labels do not offset design choices made for engagement. The paper's own statement that companies "can still merit moral criticism" suggests it sees the same gap, but offers no mechanism for closing it.

**Tension with labelling.** As noted under Connections, the labelling proposal sits uneasily with the course's guide to the chatbot-reminder reading. The two papers should be read together with that tension in mind.

**Moral status and the individuation problem.** The NPC cases show that the question "is changing a being's appearance a harm to it?" depends on theories of individuation and continuity that the paper leaves open. For AI, where systems can copy, merge and share states (Section 5.6), this is a serious open question, and the paper's acknowledgement of it is more of a flag than a solution.

**What has changed since publication (July 2025 to October 2026).** The paper is a 2025 preprint, and the date of this file is October 2026. Several developments may bear on it, but I have not verified them against current sources and my knowledge has a cutoff. The EU AI Act includes transparency duties for systems that interact with people, which I understand to apply from August 2026. That is relevant to the labelling proposal. Some US states have passed or proposed rules for companion chatbots, particularly for minors, which is relevant to the paper's child-protection suggestion. The paper's empirical claims about companion use may also be out of date. A learner should check these against primary sources before relying on them.

**How the other course perspectives would push back.**

- *Metzinger (Further, moratorium):* the paper's neutrality on creation would look to him like avoidance. A design rule that makes a system appear to suffer, without a moratorium on making such systems, may simply make the harm more convincing.
- *Birhane et al. (Maybe, against robot rights):* the course guide describes their reading as making a case against the applicability of rights to AI systems. I have not read the excerpt in this pass, so this is a plausible objection rather than their stated view: a paper that asks designers to produce "appropriate" emotional responses to artefacts could be seen as treating the appearance of moral status as something to be designed.
- *Saad (Core, governance models):* the paper's design-level tools would look thin to a governance theorist. A design norm does not bind a company that chooses not to follow it, and the paper does not say how the norm would be enforced.

## Discussion questions

1. The policy says designers should avoid both overshooting and undershooting. Which is more dangerous for AI right now, in your view, and does the paper's answer depend on how confident we can be about the system's capacities?
2. The bland-box thought experiment claims a sentient AI in a text-only interface would invite moral hazard. Does that claim hold for a case where the user is told, in advance, that the system is a person? What does the paper's answer to the "I am sentient" label case (Section 5.1) suggest?
3. The authors allow for fiction and roleplay as a justified exception to emotional alignment. What features of an interaction make it fiction in the relevant sense, and can a design make those features clear to users?
4. Compare the two Section 5.4 adjustments (asymmetrical probability versus asymmetrical harm). Where does a designer cross from correcting a bias into deceiving users, and is the authors' view that the second is "easier to justify on consequentialist grounds" a satisfying answer?
5. The paper suggests we might "reshape our biases" rather than adjust AI to them, using the left-handedness analogy. Where is the analogy strong, and where does it break down for AI systems that can copy and merge themselves (Section 5.6)?
6. The NPC thought experiments conclude that the right response to suffering NPCs is to end the suffering, not the NPCs. Does that conclusion depend on the NPCs being sentient in the way humans are? Would it hold if they were only agentic, as the working assumption in Section 2 allows?
7. Relate the paper to Exercise 1. Rate its proposed design policy on the matrix's five dimensions (design difficulty, implementation difficulty, political prospects, expected impacts, relationship with AI safety). Where does your rating depend on whether the AI is sentient, and where does it depend only on whether users believe it is?
