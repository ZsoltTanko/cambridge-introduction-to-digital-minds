# Debunking Robot Rights Metaphysically, Ethically, and Legally

**One-line takeaway:** Birhane, van Dijk and Pasquale argue that legal rights for AI systems would mostly shield their corporate owners from accountability, so the assigned Section 8 tests robot-rights ideas by asking what a court would actually have to do with them.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors are a critical technology scholar (Birhane, Mozilla Foundation and Trinity College Dublin), a philosopher of technology (van Dijk, University of Twente) and a law professor (Pasquale, Cornell). Their paper targets the robot-rights literature in AI ethics and legal theory. Its three-part argument is metaphysical (machines are not the kinds of things that can be granted or denied rights), ethical (limits on machines should be the priority, given harms to marginalised people), and legal (the best analogy is corporate rights, not human rights).

Section 8 is the legal-practice part of that argument. Its question is: if robot rights were recognised by courts, what would they do? The section's target readers are people who treat rights as a low-cost, abstract add-on to AI ethics, and legal scholars who have written modest proposals for robot rights. The section tests those proposals against concrete scenarios and concludes that even modest ones have troubling outcomes.

## Detailed summary

### Opening: rights must "cash out"

The section begins by separating legal rights talk from ethical and metaphysical rights talk. The authors say that for a pragmatic attorney, rights claims must "cash out" in an actionable claim in a court or administrative tribunal. The question is therefore how a right would redistribute resources and respect in society. They say this exercise shows problems even in the most modest proposals. Several advocates describe robot rights as expanding the circle of moral concern from persons to the natural environment and then to the built environment. The authors argue that the built-environment step is the one that causes most harm.

### Case 1: police robots and surveillance

The first example is a police robot. The authors say that even the idea of heightened concern for a robotic police force is disturbing to advocates for heavily surveilled minority communities. They quote US Congressman Jamaal Bowman, who responded to a robot police dog used by the New York Police Department. The paper describes that department as having been widely condemned for racist practices. Bowman argued that the money should go to public health and public safety, and he dismissed the robot dogs as a RoboCop-style spectacle.

The authors then imagine a robot-rights advocate arguing with Bowman. The advocate might say that respecting the robot will grow our moral concern for everyone. Or the advocate might say that a person should not feel entitled to break a leg off a policing robot that subdues them, even if they are innocent, because that would be like animal abuse. The authors call such replies condescending at best and cruelly indifferent at worst. Their point is that the moral commitments behind robot rights, applied to a police robot, make a victim's resistance look like abuse of the machine.

### Case 2: speech rights for bots

The second example is free expression. The authors cite lawyer John Frank Weaver (2018), who argued that the constitutional speech protections humans have in the US should extend to AI, robots and Twitterbots. The authors respond that bots and generative AI output already overwhelm and drown out human speech. They cite work on spam-like hashtags, amplified stereotypes and disrupted collective opinion formation (Bianchi et al., 2022). The purposes of free expression, in their view, are human autonomy and democratic self-government. Those purposes are not served by giving speech rights to bots, which are often built by already-powerful entities.

The authors extend the example to small drones that could project images, films or speech to individuals in public spaces. The right of people to peaceful enjoyment of public space could be lost, because people would be constantly targeted by messages.

### Case 3: recording rights, drones and privacy

The authors then turn to rights to record. Courts have granted people a right to record police and other public actors. The example is Glik v. Cunniffe (US First Circuit, 2011). The authors say these rights rest on a human limit: the common-sense assumption that few people will be creepy and persistent enough to stalk others with a phone camera. Attach the same camera to a drone and the usual limits no longer hold, and privacy may be destroyed. The authors cite Nourbakhsh (2013) and Véliz (2020).

### Case 4: what does a robot's "interest" add?

The authors ask what constitutional rights for a robot would add to the discussion. They ask what the robot's interest is. A robot might be programmed to want to maximise the number of people who see its ads. It might then "feel" something like discomfort. But the authors note that it could be reprogrammed with a different optimisation function (Pasquale, 2020).

They then address the consciousness question. Mosakas (2021) argues that there is no evidence in robotics of anything like the fixed consciousness of animals and humans that would demand moral concern. The authors call this a conclusive argument against a position, attributed to Jowitt (2021), that basing legal rights on human embodiment is arbitrary. (This is a strong claim, discussed in the Critical perspective section.) They also argue that a non-discrimination principle between humans and machines would most likely shift influence toward the large firms that are best placed to deploy robots and AI at scale, and to use them to manipulate judicial, political and communications systems.

The authors acknowledge that the idea of robot rights seems open-minded and cosmopolitan in the abstract. They say rights come with costs: responsibilities land on natural persons, while powerful bodies behind the robots are relieved of responsibility and accountability.

### Case 5: the public sphere and corporate power

The authors describe the logical endpoint of an unregulated automated public sphere (Pasquale, 2017). Robot armies compete for attention, and the firms with the most money, power and influence win. Those firms micro-target populations with whatever works, whether truths, half-truths or manipulative lies. The public sphere fragments into millions of private ones. The authors cite Herman and Chomsky (2010) and Lippmann (1925) to describe what remains: the manufactured consent of a phantom public. They also argue that undisclosed bots counterfeit the reputational currency people earn through their embodied presence in the world (Pasquale, 2020).

### Case 6: exclusion analogies

The authors take up Gunkel's (2013) argument that a focus on human-like capabilities has been used to exclude others. Gunkel argues that anthropocentric criteria marginalise machines and have also been used to exclude women, people of colour and animals. The authors agree that human-centred criteria have been used to exclude people, but they say the comparison should not be used to make robot exclusion look equivalent to those injustices.

They offer a sharper example. Some promoters of robot rights compare a person's right to marry a robot with the emancipatory project of same-sex marriage. The authors call this a trivialisation of the equality struggle. They note that the religious right has previously used the same flattening rhetoric, comparing gay marriage to a person marrying a dog or a box turtle. The authors say the line between post-human and dehumanising rhetoric is easily blurred.

### Case 7: the right not to be harmed

The authors then address protection from harm. Kate Darling (2016) has argued that treating robots more like animals could discourage harmful human behaviour, since kicking a robotic turtle might be an early sign of sociopathy. The authors place this under the human-welfare argument already covered in Section 6. Their concern is that complicating the hierarchy of machine, animal and human could also produce inhumane results.

As an example, they describe a US animal shelter that bought a K5 Knightscope security robot to patrol its grounds, partly to keep homeless people away. Campers tied a tarp around the robot, covered its sensors with barbecue sauce and knocked it over. The shelter's spokeswoman complained about the cost of security, and the complaint drew a wave of online derision. The authors say the shelter had not considered whether constant robotic surveillance could be cruel to people.

The authors then consider whether a robot might deserve animal-cruelty protections. They answer no, for two reasons. The types of interest protected by animal welfare laws do not exist in the robot case, because the robot cannot feel and has no phenomenological engagement with the world. Robots are better classified as tools and property that need a licence, not as partners. The authors note that the San Francisco Department of Public Works warned that unauthorised operation of robots on sidewalks risks a daily fine (Cuthbertson, 2017), and that this is correct: a person can feel pain if run over by a robot, but a robot cannot be harmed by being switched off or scrapped.

### Closing: a hermeneutics of suspicion

The section ends with three moves. First, it recalls Solum's (1992) warnings and an open letter from more than 280 European experts, which the authors quote as calling the idea of an electronic legal person ideological, nonsensical and non-pragmatic (Nevejans et al., 2018). Second, it argues that because robots can be used to surveil, oppress and marginalise people, we should read efforts to build a general moral duty to respect robot rights with suspicion. Third, it returns to Danaher's "Andromedan" thought experiment from Section 7 and says that the composition, origin and intent of an entity matter to its reception as a moral agent or patient. The authors say an "ethics of relationality" cannot erase those considerations.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

The rest of the paper is not assigned. Briefly:

- **Abstract and Introduction:** The metaphysical, ethical and legal claims are stated. The authors argue that robot rights would act as a smoke screen for corporate interests, and they say the "robot" in debate is always a human-machine socio-technical system.
- **Section 2 (robot rights debate):** Robot personhood has been a fringe but long-running position. The authors separate arguments about how robots shape human virtue from arguments about robots' own qualities.
- **Section 3 (the robots at issue):** The authors list kinds of robots and argue that current systems depend on hidden human labour, from microwork and content moderation to data collection. They discuss training-data consent and environmental costs, and they use dataset tables from Birhane and Prabhu (2021).
- **Section 4 (the machines-like-us argument):** The "map is not the territory" critique targets the idea that a human-like machine should get rights. The authors criticise Dawkins' and Kurzweil's arguments and say rights are not granted because a being proves it is human.
- **Section 5 (embodied and enactive cognition):** Human beings are described as self-sustaining, situated and relational processes. Machines are described as closed, fully specified artifacts.
- **Section 6 (posthumanism):** The authors separate Latour-style posthumanism from transhumanism. They argue that ecological posthumanism does not support robot rights, and they describe the material and energy costs of AI.
- **Section 7 (legal perspective):** The authors separate "freedom from damage" from alleged positive rights to act. They discuss Froomkin and Colangelo's work on self-defence against drones, Massaro and Norton's free-speech argument, and Google's First Amendment claims in Volokh and Falk (2011).
- **Section 9 (the enduring irresponsibility of AI rights talk):** The authors name two errors: focusing on relationships without regard to facts, and treating machines as entities that can be harmed or discriminated against. They end by calling for accountability legislation for powerful corporations.

## Techniques & methods explained

These are the argumentative moves Section 8 uses. The paper does not run experiments.

- **The "cash out" test.** Ask what a claimed right would require a court or tribunal to do. If an abstract right has no clear enforceable content, its effects will fall on whoever has to enforce or pay for it. Here, the test shows that rights for robots would mainly make human bystanders and marginalised people bear the burden of protecting a machine's owner.
- **Scenario testing.** The authors take a proposal and walk it into a concrete setting: a police dog, a campus robot, a speech-protected bot, a drone with a camera. They then check whether the proposal still looks sound. The method is strong for showing side effects. It is weak for showing that a scenario is typical.
- **Owner-versus-robot tracing.** The authors ask who actually gains or loses when a right is granted to a robot. Their answer is that the rights accrue to the owner or manufacturer, not to the robot. This turns a question about the machine's moral status into a question about who holds power.
- **Interest audit.** Before granting a right, ask what interest is being protected. The authors ask whether the robot has an interest that survives reprogramming, and they argue that a robot's apparent "discomfort" depends entirely on its designers' optimisation target.
- **Analogy stress-testing.** The authors test robot rights against other rights movements (gun owners' rights, same-sex marriage, animal welfare, corporate speech) and argue that the robot analogy either trivialises those movements or lets corporations hide behind them.
- **Hermeneutics of suspicion.** When a moral duty seems to arise from emotional responses or simulated relationships, the authors ask who designed the response and for what purpose. This shifts the burden of proof onto the people who want the duty recognised.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

The table separates the case for robot rights (as the authors describe it) from the authors' response. The authors' view is not balanced.

| Claimed benefit of robot rights (as described) | Authors' response in Section 8 |
|---|---|
| Expands the circle of moral concern beyond persons to environments and built objects | Attention to robots may crowd out concern for people in marginalised communities. Rights for police robots would make resistance look like abuse of property. |
| Protects free expression, including speech by AI systems | Bots and generative AI already drown out human speech. Expression rights for bots serve the firms that build them. |
| Protects the right to record and to observe | Recording rights rest on human limits. Attaching them to drones or swarms of robots removes those limits and erodes privacy. |
| Discourages cruelty and shapes human behaviour (Darling's point) | Complicating the hierarchy of machine, animal and human can cause inhumane outcomes, as the shelter example shows. |
| Gives legal protection against harm to robots | Robots cannot be harmed in the morally relevant sense. Protections fall on owners, and the owners are powerful firms. |
| Offers a modest, relational or pragmatic path to moral consideration (Coeckelbergh-style) | Relational duties really concern the people in the relationship, so they are a human-rights argument in disguise. |
| Non-discrimination between humans and machines is fair | Non-discrimination would tip influence to large firms that can deploy robots at scale. |

**Trade-offs the authors name explicitly:** rights for robots shift responsibilities onto natural persons and away from the powerful bodies behind robots. The authors judge this trade-off to be a net loss.

**Trade-off the authors largely leave unexamined:** the case in which a future system really does have morally relevant experience. The section says that current robots cannot feel, and it treats that as settled for the purposes of the argument. It does not explore what would follow if that were false.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Robot rights** - claims that machines should hold legal or moral rights, either positive (to act, speak, vote, marry) or negative (freedom from damage). The authors use the term broadly, to include LLMs, chatbots, drones and social bots.
- **Cash out (rights talk)** - translating a right into an actionable legal claim before a court or tribunal. The authors' test for whether a rights proposal means anything.
- **Corporate rights** - rights of corporations, such as speech or property protections, which the authors treat as the closest legal analogy to robot rights and as a source of harm to workers, consumers and voters.
- **Ethics of relationality** - an approach that grounds moral consideration in relationships with others. The authors say it cannot replace questions about how an entity was built, what it is made of and what it is for.
- **Phantom public** - Lippmann's term, used by the authors for a public whose opinions have been manufactured by media and bots rather than formed freely.
- **Manufactured consent** - Herman and Chomsky's idea, cited by the authors, that media systems shape public opinion in the interests of those who control them.
- **Automated public sphere** - the online space in which algorithms and bots compete for attention, which the authors say favours the best-funded firms (Pasquale, 2017).
- **Reputational currency** - the social trust people earn through embodied, accountable presence. The authors argue that undisclosed bots counterfeit it.
- **Hermeneutics of suspicion** - the authors' method of reading claims about robot moral status with scepticism about who benefits and why.
- **Freedom from vs freedom to** - the paper's distinction between a protection against damage and a positive permission to act. Section 8 focuses on the positive claims.
- **Anthropocentric criteria** - Gunkel's term for human-centred tests of moral status, which the authors say have excluded women, people of colour and animals.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

No original study is reported. The evidence is anecdotal, legal and secondary.

- **NYPD robot police dog (Bowman, 2021):** a congressman criticised the department's robot dog as "RoboCop" nonsense. The authors cite it as an example of why robot rights for police robots would be disturbing to advocates for surveilled communities.
- **Glik v. Cunniffe (US First Circuit, 2011):** a court recognised a right to record police. The authors argue that the rationale depends on human limits that drones would remove.
- **K5 Knightscope robot at an animal shelter (US, undated in the paper):** campers tarped the robot, covered its sensors with barbecue sauce and knocked it over. The shelter's security costs became a public controversy.
- **San Francisco Department of Public Works warning (Cuthbertson, 2017):** unauthorised sidewalk robot operation risks a daily fine. The authors cite it as evidence that robots are treated as property.
- **Open letter from over 280 European experts (Nevejans et al., 2018):** the authors quote it as rejecting an electronic legal person as ideological and non-pragmatic.
- **Bianchi et al. (2022):** cited for the claim that bots and generative AI already spam hashtags, amplify stereotypes and impede collective will formation. See the Critical perspective section about the reference's title.
- **Danaher's Andromedan spouse (Danaher, 2020):** a thought experiment about moral status through behaviour, which the authors reject in Section 7 and recall in Section 8's closing.
- **Gunkel (2013) on exclusion:** anthropocentric criteria have excluded women, people of colour and animals.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

**What the section does well.** It forces the reader to ask what a right does in practice and who holds it. Its examples are concrete, and several of them (the surveillance robot, the bot-speech problem, the drone and recording-rights point) identify real side effects that a purely philosophical rights debate can miss. The point that rights for systems may shield their owners from liability is a serious governance concern, and it is independent of whether machines can ever be moral patients.

**Where the argument is weaker.**

- **It answers a narrower question than the one it claims.** Much of the section argues against rights for current, deployed systems: police dogs, chatbots, delivery robots. The question the course cares about is whether a future system might have morally relevant states that create claims of its own. The section's replies about the shelter robot and the sidewalk-permit example do not address that question, because they assume the robot cannot feel. That assumption is the very thing under dispute in Units 2 and 3.
- **Some claims are overstated.** The authors present Mosakas (2021) as conclusive against Jowitt's view that basing legal rights on embodiment is arbitrary. A single article on the consciousness criterion does not settle a contested legal-philosophical question, and the authors give little reason for the strength of the claim.
- **The examples are anecdotes.** The shelter robot, the police dog and the Glik case are vivid, but they do not establish that robot rights would produce these outcomes generally. The authors often move from "could" to "would". The argument is stronger for the claim that rights can be used this way than for the claim that they inevitably will be.
- **The citation for bots is doubtful.** The reference list entry cited for the claim that bots and generative AI overwhelm human speech (Bianchi et al., 2022) has the title "Easily accessible text-to-image generation amplifies demographic stereotypes at large scale". The title does not obviously support that claim. Check the original before relying on it.
- **Some references are in the wrong place or date.** The Solum (1992) entry in the reference list repeats the citation of Solum (1991), with the same journal details. The Weaver (2018) reference has the Newsweek URL for the Bowman story in the source. These are housekeeping issues, but they affect how easily the claims can be checked.
- **Dated evidence.** The paper's data anchors come from 2021 to 2022: the dataset consent table, the Sophia robot grant (2018), the K5 shelter incident and the energy estimates. Some numbers may have moved, and I have not verified current figures.

**What has changed since 2024 (October 2026).** The paper's targets were mainly chatbots, LLMs and social bots, and it was written in the early 2020s, when the robot-rights debate was still mostly academic. Two things have moved. First, the course's own 2026 readings suggest that the debate has become concrete. The title of sibling reading 09 (Smith, Caviola and Alexander, an analysis of US state legislation on AI legal status) points to state-level measures denying AI personhood, which matches the paper's policy preference. I have not read that reading or Sunstein's 2026 paper for this file, so I cannot say which measures were enacted. Second, the AI welfare discussion has moved to welfare-based protections under uncertainty rather than full rights. The paper's objections are aimed at rights and legal personhood. They do not directly address welfare-based precautions that can be adopted without any rights claim, which is a gap in the paper's framing rather than a rebuttal of it.

**How other course perspectives would push back.**

- The **Salib and Goldstein** reading would argue that some rights for AI may reduce catastrophic risk, so Section 8's concern about shielding owners is a reason to design rights carefully, not to refuse all rights.
- **Sunstein's** approach to moral uncertainty would say that refusing rights because consciousness is uncertain is itself a choice with costs, and that uncertainty cuts both ways.
- The **consciousness units (2 and 3)** would say that the authors' premise that robots cannot feel needs evidence, not assertion.
- **Coeckelbergh's** relational approach, which the authors criticise, would say that the paper's distinction between robot welfare and human welfare is not as sharp as the authors suggest in their discussion of human-robot relationships.

## Discussion questions

1. The section says rights talk must "cash out" in an actionable claim. Pick one of the paper's examples (the police robot, the speech bot, the drone, the shelter robot) and say what the rights claim would require a court to do. Who would bear the cost?
2. The authors argue that granting robots the right to record or speak mainly benefits their owners. Is that always true? Can you imagine a case where a rights claim for a machine protects a person without benefiting an owner?
3. The authors' case against animal-style protections rests on the premise that robots cannot feel. If that premise were uncertain, how would the argument change? What evidence would you need before accepting or rejecting it?
4. Compare this section with Salib and Goldstein's "AI Rights for Human Safety". Can rights be designed to limit corporate power rather than to increase it? What would a rights regime look like if it were built for accountability?
5. The authors use analogies to gun-owner rights, corporate speech and same-sex marriage. Which analogy do you find most useful for thinking about AI rights, and which one (if any) distorts the question?
6. Exercise 2 asks you to pick a legal action or framework and note over- and under-attribution of moral status. Apply the "cash out" test to one of the course's legal readings (Novelli et al., or the state-level personhood analysis). Where does the over-attribution risk show up?
7. The section warns that rights for robots could shift responsibility from powerful firms to natural persons. What accountability mechanisms, short of rights, would prevent that outcome? Are they enough?
8. The paper's strongest claim is that robots are tools. At what point, if any, would a system stop being a tool in the sense the authors need, and who should decide?
