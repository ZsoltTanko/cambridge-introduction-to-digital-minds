## Purpose of the reading

The authors (Novelli, Floridi and Sartor, 2025) write a critical literature review of the AI legal personhood debate. Their stated aim is to trace where the debate has been and where it is heading, arguing that it is shaped by a set of "path dependencies" - earlier choices and frames that constrain later ones. The excerpt does not name an intended readership; from its legal-theory register, I infer legal scholars, philosophers and policy-minded readers. The question the excerpt answers is: what does it mean to give something legal personhood, and what conceptual options exist for deciding whether AI qualifies?

## Detailed summary

### Framing from the introduction (in the file, not in the assignment)

The authors name four strands of path dependency that shape the debate:

1. **Prevailing views of legal personhood** - singularist or cluster (Section 2).
2. **Technological advancement and accessibility** - as AI masters new domains, expectations are recalibrated (Section 3).
3. **Existing regulation** - data privacy, liability and cybersecurity rules, which can either help or hinder recognition, depending on whether they are consistent with one another (Section 4).
4. **Historical case law** - precedent for extending personhood to corporations and other entities (Section 4).

They argue that strands 1 and 2 move together. In periods of technological optimism and commercial accessibility the debate leans toward the cluster view, with arguments that AI should get a broad set of human- or corporate-like rights and duties. In periods of scepticism it leans singularist, asking whether AI should get narrow rights of the kind given to animals or environmental entities.

Two further claims in the introduction are important for the rest of the unit. First, the authors say that EU frameworks tend to constrain rather than catalyse expansion of AI personhood, forming a cohesive resistance to change. Second, they predict that courts will be hesitant to recognise AI personhood without supportive legislation, given the limited empirical evidence available. Recognition is unlikely unless AI first achieves *de facto* legitimacy, meaning it exercises rights and fulfils societal roles over an extended period. The introduction ends by previewing prospects over 5-20 years (incremental advances in generative models and autonomous agents) and a more distant scenario in which AI is integrated with human cognition through brain-machine interfaces.

### 2.1 Where the idea of a "persona" comes from

The section opens with etymology. The word persona first meant the mask an actor wore in Roman theatre, then the roles a person plays in law - as debtor or creditor in substantive law, as plaintiff or defendant in procedure - and finally the abstract ability to take part in legal relations at all.

In Roman law, as presented through Gaius's *Institutes*, the term applied to every human. But a more selective notion also existed: the concrete ability to take on legal roles. Enslaved people were excluded from it, treated as things that could be sold rather than active participants. Over the late empire slavery was progressively abolished and the principle spread that all humans are, in principle, legal persons. The idea was then extended to collective entities such as companies and associations, which act through their members and employees, called organs. Modern law therefore divides persons into **natural persons** (human beings) and **juridical persons** (collective entities).

The authors' point is that personhood has always been a set of roles granted to entities, not a fixed property, and that exclusion has been part of its history.

### 2.2 The singularist view

Before deciding whether AI qualifies, the authors ask what legal personhood means, i.e. which roles (rights, duties, powers and combinations of these) a person can hold and which non-persons, such as things, cannot.

The **singularist view** is the minimal answer: an entity is a legal person if it holds, or can hold, a single right or duty. This fits the idea that a right is a legally protected interest, and it lets law protect entities that cannot act on their own. If norms protect non-human animals, foetuses, trees or rivers, the singularist concludes that those entities also have legal personhood. The footnote on this paragraph adds that the position conflicts with practice, since these entities are generally not regarded as legal persons in ordinary legal life (citing Kurki, 2019).

Applied to AI, the singularist question is whether AI has features that justify a single right or duty of the kind given to other non-human entities. The example given is sentience: if specific AI systems were sentient, there would be an argument for extending the mistreatment protections currently given to animals.

### 2.3 The cluster view

The **cluster view** (associated with Kurki 2019, Naffine 2009 and Ohlin 2005) is broader. Legal personhood is the vesting, or capacity to vest, of a large cluster of roles. In private law these include making contracts, owning property, protection against harm by others, the power to start legal proceedings, and liability for one's own wrongdoing.

The cluster is typically attributed to all adult human beings (footnote: adult citizens of sound mind). But some roles can be limited:

- Children and incapacitated adults are persons in law but cannot start contracts or lawsuits on their own; guardians act for them.
- Corporations can contract and sue through their administrators but are excluded from family roles (spouse, child) and from some fundamental and data-protection rights under EU data protection law.

Personhood is also restricted through minority, incapacity, and in some legal systems through features such as gender or social position. The authors note that some groups have been stripped of all personhood, denied the "right to have rights" (citing Arendt, 1966, ch. 9).

On this view the statuses are unified by a **typical case**, the default cluster for adult humans. Every other entity is a variation from that standard. On my reading, this makes the question for AI "how far from the typical case?" rather than "is it a person or not?". The excerpt itself frames the cluster statuses as variations from the typical case.

### 2.4 Applying the two views to AI

The authors set out the debate in terms of the two views:

- **Singularist:** can AI's features be assimilated to those that justify single rights for other non-human entities (mainly benefit-rights)?
- **Cluster:** should specific AI systems hold legal roles that overlap substantially with those of humans or juridical persons, possibly with restrictions?

Within the cluster approach there are two templates:

- **Minor or incapacitated person model:** AI gets protection against harm and eligibility for some benefits but no independent capacity to contract or litigate.
- **Corporate model:** AI gets limited protections but can be given a general capacity to contract and bring lawsuits.

The authors argue that neither template is easy to defend. They set aside the case where AI becomes indistinguishable from humans or from human-run corporations, since that would dissolve the debate rather than settle it. In every other case there is no uncontroversial criterion for deciding whether AI resembles humans or corporations. The relevant traits form a continuum.

The sharpest point in this part is about **level of abstraction**. Take rationality narrowly, as the ability to do elementary planning or pattern recognition, and sentience narrowly, as reacting appropriately to wanted or unwanted inputs. Then one can reasonably argue that some AI systems and humans share these capacities. Demanding definitions of the same concepts lead to a different answer. So, on the authors' argument, the conclusion turns on the level of abstraction chosen, which determines which traits count as relevant.

### 2.5 Two perspectives: de lege lata and de lege ferenda

The debate also depends on whether one asks about existing law or about reform:

- **De lege lata** (what the law is): does current law already recognise, or could it reasonably be interpreted to recognise, some degree of legal personhood for AI?
- **De lege ferenda** (what the law should be): should new laws be enacted to grant AI legal personhood explicitly?

Both perspectives need normative criteria to justify or reject AI personhood.

### 2.6 Non-instrumental and instrumental rationales

The last part of Section 2 sorts the reasons for granting personhood into two kinds:

- **Non-instrumental:** some AI systems deserve personhood because of their intrinsic features. The authors frame this in Kantian terms, as beings that are "ends in themselves". Examples: AI capable of rational thought should get the protections given to rational beings; AI capable of suffering should be protected like other sentient entities.
- **Instrumental:** AI should get personhood for its benefits to humans, such as the people who depend on AI in business or society (citing Novelli, 2023). Here personhood is a tool serving human goals rather than recognition of value in the AI itself.

On my reading, the distinction is the most useful tool the excerpt offers for later readings in this section. The excerpt itself does not discuss other sources; my own classification is that Salib and Goldstein's work on rights as a tool against catastrophic risk is an instrumental argument.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

The excerpt file continues a little into Section 3, "The Trends of the Debate", which is beyond the guide's stop point. The authors open with ancient myths of artificial humans (Pygmalion and Galatea) and then turn to the legal literature:

- **Tuchler (1978), "Man-Made Man and the Law":** introduces the fabricated-man concept, a term partly inspired by science fiction and applied to children conceived with reproductive technology, embryos grown outside the womb, cyborgs and robots. Tuchler warns that denying personhood to such beings could open a gap between law and public moral intuitions, leading to cynicism, disobedience or weaker respect for legal norms. He also warns that extending rights and duties could undermine humanity's sense of its own inherent value.
- **Shapiro (1982), "Of Robots, Persons, and the Protection of Religious Beliefs":** argues by analogy that a person can undergo such profound change that they stop being a person in the relevant sense and become robot-like, losing the capacity for autonomous decision-making.

## Techniques & methods explained

1. **Etymological and historical reconstruction.** The authors trace "persona" from theatre masks to the roles of Roman law, and from the excluded enslaved person to the corporation. *What for:* to show that personhood has always been a granted set of roles with a shifting boundary. *Where it fails:* it can suggest that legal history determines present answers, when it is only one of the path dependencies the authors list.

2. **Decomposition into role clusters.** The entity is treated as a bundle of rights, duties, powers and capacities, and each can be assessed separately. *How it works:* list the roles in question (contracts, property, protection, litigation, liability), then check which entities hold which subset. *What for:* to explain how children, incapacitated adults and corporations can all be persons while holding different subsets. *Where it fails:* it presupposes that the roles can be listed in a stable way, and it inherits the exclusions the authors note (gender, social position).

3. **Singularist test.** Find any single right or duty that an entity holds, and the entity counts as a person. *How it works:* look for protective norms that apply to the entity, as with animals or rivers. *What for:* to justify narrow protections for entities that cannot act for themselves. *Where it fails:* the authors say it does not match practice, and it may make personhood so cheap that the concept loses force.

4. **The typical case as a reference point.** The adult human is the default cluster, and other entities are variations. *How it works:* ask how far an entity departs from the standard case and in which roles. *What for:* to turn "is it a person?" into a question of degree. *Where it fails:* the standard may silently encode what counts as normal, and the excerpt does not say how to pick the typical case when humans themselves differ.

5. **Analogy templates for AI.** The minor or incapacitated person model and the corporate model. *How it works:* map AI onto a known category and borrow its roles. *What for:* to propose concrete legal designs. *Where it fails:* neither model is a clean fit, and each implies a different mix of protection and autonomy, which the authors do not resolve.

6. **Level-of-abstraction analysis.** The same concept (rationality, sentience) is assessed at different thresholds. *How it works:* state the concept at a chosen level, identify observable traits relevant at that level, and check whether AI has them. *What for:* to show that many AI-personhood conclusions depend on the chosen definition. *Where it fails:* it can be used to argue for any conclusion by choosing a threshold, so the chosen threshold has to be justified separately.

7. **Two framing axes.** De lege lata versus de lege ferenda, and non-instrumental versus instrumental rationale. *How it works:* classify each argument on both axes. *What for:* to keep the existing-law question apart from the reform question, and the intrinsic-value argument apart from the usefulness argument. *Where it fails:* the axes are not exclusive, and an argument can mix them.

8. **Path-dependency analysis** (from the introduction). The debate is shaped by prior views, technological expectations, regulation and precedent, which interact over time. *What for:* to explain why the debate oscillates. *Where it fails:* the excerpt presents this as an interpretive lens, not as a tested model.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Legal personhood** - the capacity to hold legal roles (rights, duties, powers); not the same as being human.
- **Natural person** - a human being in law.
- **Juridical person** - a collective entity, such as a company or association, that acts through its organs.
- **Persona** - in Roman usage, first the actor's mask and then the legal role, and finally the ability to take part in legal relations.
- **Singularist view** - an entity is a legal person if it holds or can hold just one right or duty.
- **Cluster view** - legal personhood is a bundle of roles, typically attributed to adult humans and varied for other entities.
- **Typical case** - the default cluster of roles for adult humans, against which other entities are measured.
- **De lege lata** - the question of whether existing law already recognises or could recognise AI personhood.
- **De lege ferenda** - the question of whether new law should grant AI personhood.
- **Non-instrumental rationale** - personhood justified by intrinsic features, in Kantian terms "ends in themselves".
- **Instrumental rationale** - personhood justified by its benefits to humans.
- **Level of abstraction** - the threshold at which a concept such as rationality or sentience is defined, which determines which traits count.
- **Path dependency** - the way earlier views and choices constrain later ones; the authors name four strands.
- **De facto legitimacy** - the authors' term for recognition that comes from AI exercising rights and fulfilling roles over time.
- **Fabricated man** - Tuchler's term (1978) for human-like beings produced by technology.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

The excerpt contains no experiments or statistics. Its concrete material is historical, doctrinal and illustrative:

- **Roman law (Gaius's *Institutes*):** personhood applied to all humans, but enslaved people were excluded from the ability to take legal roles.
- **Corporate personhood:** companies and associations were given personhood through their members and organs.
- **EU data protection law:** corporations are excluded from some data-protection rights, which the authors use to show that personhood is partial.
- **Children and incapacitated adults:** persons in law who need guardians to act for them.
- **Arendt (1966, ch. 9):** the "right to have rights" as a warning that personhood can be taken away.
- **Tuchler (1978):** the fabricated-man concept and the concern about a gap between law and moral intuitions.
- **Shapiro (1982):** the argument that a person can become "robot-like" through transformation.
- **Prospects:** the authors frame near-term AI development as roughly 5-20 years of incremental progress, separate from a more distant brain-machine interface scenario.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's view.*

- **Historical analogies carry the argument.** The reading leans heavily on Roman law, corporate personhood and the treatment of minors. These analogies are useful for showing that personhood is a set of roles, but they do not show which roles AI should have. The corporate analogy in particular raises the question of whether AI should be treated as an agent with interests or as an instrument run by others.
- **The singularist objection rests on practice, and practice is contested.** The footnote says the singularist view conflicts with what legal systems actually do. Some jurisdictions have granted legal personhood to rivers, notably the Whanganui River in New Zealand (2017) and the Atrato River in Colombia (2016). That undercuts the claim that such entities are, in practice, not treated as persons, and it suggests the singularist position is less absurd in practice than the reading implies. This comes from my own knowledge, not from the excerpt.
- **The level-of-abstraction point is the strongest contribution.** It shows that the AI-personhood question cannot be settled by looking at AI systems alone, because the criteria come first. That is a serious point. The weakness is that the reading gives no principled way to choose a threshold. Each side will choose the threshold that suits its conclusion.
- **The "de facto legitimacy" prediction is a claim about social process.** The authors predict that courts will not act without legislation and that recognition requires AI to exercise rights for some time. This is plausible but untested in the excerpt, and it is not clear what evidence would count for or against it.
- **Things that have changed since publication.** The paper is from 2025, and the date is now October 2026. The EU AI Act, adopted in 2024 and applied in stages since then, regulates AI systems by risk and does not grant them personhood, which fits the authors' claim that EU frameworks resist expansion. Reading 09 reports that several US states have enacted or proposed laws denying AI legal status, which supports the authors' view that the de lege ferenda debate is moving toward denial in at least some places. The paper's 5-20-year horizon for incremental AI progress is still running, so its claims about agents and generative models can be checked against what has happened since.
- **How other course perspectives would push back.** Birhane et al. would argue that the legal rationales for robot rights are dangerous and that the cluster view imports human categories that do not fit machines. Metzinger would argue for a moratorium on research that risks synthetic phenomenology, which makes the whole personhood debate secondary. Salib and Goldstein would accept the instrumental rationale and push it further, using rights as a safety tool. Schwitzgebel would warn that legal roles may add to public moral confusion.
