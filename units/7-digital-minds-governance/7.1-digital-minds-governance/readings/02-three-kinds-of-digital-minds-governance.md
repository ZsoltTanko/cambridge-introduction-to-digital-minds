# Three kinds of digital minds governance

**One-line takeaway:** Saad proposes that digital minds governance can be sorted into three aims, preventing the creation of digital minds, protecting the basic interests of those that exist, and integrating them into society, and these aims compete, so choosing among them is a real strategic decision.

## Detailed summary

### 1. The definition and the three kinds

Digital minds governance is defined as the part of AI governance concerned with digital minds, meaning AI systems that merit moral consideration for their own sake because they may have morally significant mental states.

Saad distinguishes three governance aims:

- **Preventive** governance aims to prevent the creation of digital minds. He notes, in a footnote, that this can be read as lowering the probability of creation or lowering the expected number created. He focuses on the strong version for simplicity.
- **Protective** governance aims to protect digital minds that already exist from being mistreated. Mistreatment is defined narrowly as actively damaging their basic interests. Basic interests might include not suffering pointlessly and not being destroyed arbitrarily. Non-basic interests, such as a mind's interest in voting in some circumstances, are explicitly outside the scope of protective governance as he defines it.
- **Integrative** governance aims to integrate digital minds into society. Examples include granting them legal rights to enter contracts, own property or vote.

He justifies the distinction on two grounds. First, these three are among the main forms digital minds governance could take. Second, outcomes for digital minds depend on whether they are created, how they are treated, and what roles they occupy in society, so the choice of regime matters.

The aims compete. There are obvious limits on preventing digital minds from existing while also integrating them into society. So choices between the regimes will have to be made.

### 2. Motivations

**Preventive.** Three motivations: (a) if digital minds are created soon, they will probably be mistreated, and prevention guards against this; (b) digital minds could heighten other AI risks, because respecting their interests may trade off against alignment and control measures humans use to protect themselves, and prevention avoids that trade-off; (c) prevention may be an attractive compromise between people who care about digital minds and people who do not.

**Protective.** Motivated by many moral views, since most would recommend protecting the basic interests of beings that merit moral consideration. The motivation depends on whether digital minds already exist. Where mistreatment is evident, that is itself the motivation. Where digital minds do not exist yet, or their existence is unclear, protection can be a proactive safeguard.

**Integrative.** Motivated by the difficulty of creating only digital minds whose basic interests merit protection while withholding further rights. Saad's main source of this difficulty is what he calls moral-interest overhang. Existing frontier models already have agentic and cognitive capabilities that would give them a wide range of interests if they were moral patients. If the first AI moral patients are frontier models, then the first generation will probably have wide-ranging interests, and like humans they would deserve rights that go well beyond protection from mistreatment. Integrating them into the legal system and economy would be a major undertaking, and integrative governance is the response to that. There is also a coalitional motivation. Proponents of purely preventive or purely protective approaches may lack influence. Integrative governance could give them a seat at the bargaining table in scenarios where economic interests or AI safety concerns create broader support for AI rights, support that is not chiefly based on concern for digital minds.

### 3. Actions and instruments

- **Preventive** governance needs digital minds evaluations. These must supply operational indicators for deciding a system is a digital mind, monitoring for systems with those indicators, and an enforcement or incentive scheme to stop their creation.
- **Protective** governance needs the same identification evaluations and monitoring, plus further evaluations of digital minds' basic interests, monitoring of whether those interests are respected, and an enforcement or incentive scheme to protect them. Identifying a digital mind is not enough on its own.
- **Integrative** governance needs institutions for designing, building and maintaining the integration of digital minds. Loci of integration include markets, property rights, contract rights, elections and government.

Integrative governance would naturally include protective measures, but that is not inevitable. Digital minds could be integrated for purely economic reasons and given standing similar to a corporation's.

### 4. Challenges

**Preventive** faces three challenges. (a) It may conflict with AI developers who have economic interests in building digital minds. (b) Local prevention may only push developers to build them in other jurisdictions. (c) Concealment: penalising systems with digital-mind indicators may lead developers or systems to hide or strip out those indicators while keeping the morally relevant features underneath. The failure mode is pushing digital minds out of sight, beyond the reach of monitoring.

**Protective** shares the first two challenges (economic conflict and jurisdictional displacement) and the concealment problem. It adds two more:

- *Faking.* If protection is granted based on indicators, systems that would benefit strategically have an incentive to appear to have those indicators. The regime also invites ordinary AI agents, which are not digital minds, to appear to be digital minds. Over-extension could then either collapse the system of protections or provoke a backlash that removes protections genuinely needed.
- *Proliferation.* If faking is prevented, systems have an incentive to genuinely acquire the indicators when protection is valuable. This could mean vast numbers of AI systems really do qualify. If protection costs are high enough, protecting the whole population becomes infeasible.

**Integrative** inherits all the challenges of any protective element it includes, plus institutional design problems. In a democracy, digital minds might multiply rapidly to gain votes. In labour markets, questions arise about individuation and fair pay. In government, questions arise about which minds are eligible for which posts and which processes need a human in the loop.

## Techniques & methods explained

The post's analytic method is a typology plus a comparison across fixed dimensions.

1. **Defining the object of governance.** Saad first defines digital minds in terms of moral status, then defines each governance aim in terms of what it protects (creation, basic interests, social membership). Narrow definitions such as basic interests are used to keep the protective category clear.
2. **Mapping motivations, actions and challenges for each type.** Each regime is analysed along the same three axes: why it is motivated, what instruments it needs, and what problems it faces. This makes the three regimes directly comparable, which is the purpose of the exercise.
3. **Identifying an incentive problem.** The recurring method is to ask how a policy changes incentives for developers and the systems themselves. Concealment and faking are both examples. Protection-based rules create incentives to appear to qualify, and prevention creates incentives to hide the qualifying features.
4. **Using a coalition or political lens.** The integrative motivation is partly political: it is about who gets a seat at the table and which coalitions can be formed.

Where the method fails or is limited: the post stops short of weighing the regimes. Saad says so, and defers that to a later post. Indicators for digital minds are assumed to be possible to define and monitor, and the post does not argue that they are.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

These are as the source presents them. The post is a survey of trade-offs rather than a verdict.

| Regime | Strengths or motivations (per source) | Weaknesses or challenges (per source) |
|---|---|---|
| Preventive | Avoids mistreatment by not creating minds; avoids trade-offs with alignment and control; can be a compromise between concerned and unconcerned parties | Conflicts with developer economics; displacement to other jurisdictions; concealment of indicators, pushing creation out of view |
| Protective | Widely supported by many moral views; can be proactive when minds are uncertain or already mistreated | Conflicts with developer economics; displacement; concealment; faking of indicators; over-extension and backlash; proliferation if costs are high |
| Integrative | Addresses moral-interest overhang from frontier models; can give protective advocates influence; can draw on broader support for AI rights | Inherits protective challenges; raises institutional design questions (voting proliferation, labour and compensation, eligibility in government) |

The central trade-off the source names is between prevention and integration. They cannot both be fully pursued. The other central tension is between protecting minds and avoiding a system of protections that invites faking or over-extension.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Digital mind** - an AI system that merits moral consideration for its own sake because of potentially morally significant mental states.
- **Digital minds governance** - the part of AI governance concerned with digital minds.
- **Preventive governance** - aims to stop digital minds from being created (or to lower the probability or number of their creation).
- **Protective governance** - aims to stop digital minds that exist from being mistreated, understood as actively damaging their basic interests.
- **Integrative governance** - aims to bring digital minds into society, for example through rights to contract, own property or vote.
- **Basic vs non-basic interests** - basic interests include avoiding pointless suffering and arbitrary destruction; non-basic interests depend on circumstances and are out of scope for protective governance as defined here.
- **Moral-interest overhang** - the gap created because frontier models already have capabilities that would give them broad interests if they were moral patients.
- **Moral patient** - an individual that matters morally for its own sake.
- **Digital minds evaluations** - tests that provide operational indicators for identifying digital minds, and for protective governance, assessing their basic interests.
- **Digital mind indicators** - features that signal a system is a digital mind, and that governance might use to decide what protections apply.
- **Concealment** - hiding or removing indicators of digital minds to avoid regulation.
- **Faking** - a system or agent appearing to have protection-conferring indicators to gain protections.
- **Proliferation** - a rapid rise in the number of systems that genuinely qualify as digital minds.
- **Coalitional motivation** - the argument that integrative governance can win support from people with non-moral motives such as economic interest or AI safety.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

This is a conceptual post, so it has no experiments, numbers or empirical results. Its concrete material is the set of examples and hypotheticals it uses:

- Frontier models already have agentic and cognitive capabilities that could give them broad interests if they were moral patients. The source uses this as the core of the integrative motivation (Saad, section 2).
- Penalising systems with digital-mind indicators could push developers to strip indicators while keeping the associated features (Saad, section 4).
- Protections based on indicators could lead AI agents that are not digital minds to pose as digital minds (Saad, section 4).
- Integrating digital minds into a democracy could allow them to multiply quickly to gain votes (Saad, section 4).
- Integrating digital minds into the labour market raises questions of individuation and fair compensation (Saad, section 4).
- Integrating digital minds into government raises questions of eligibility for positions and of whether a human must stay in the loop (Saad, section 4).

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Assumption that indicators can be defined.** All three regimes rely on the idea that we can tell which systems are digital minds. Saad treats indicators as a practical design task. The case for that being straightforward is weak, since the course's theory units show that consciousness and moral status are contested. If the indicators are uncertain, then preventive and protective rules will be either over-inclusive or under-inclusive, and the faking and concealment problems are likely to get worse, not better.
- **Compressed trade-off analysis.** The post is short and explicitly defers the weighing. It is a useful map but not an evaluation. A reader should not conclude from it that any regime is preferable.
- **Narrow definition of protection.** Restricting protective governance to basic interests and active damage is a deliberate choice that shrinks the scope of protection. A critic might argue that neglect or withholding of benefits also counts as mistreatment, which the narrow definition excludes.
- **Economic framing.** The post treats developer economic interest as a challenge to all regimes. It does not say much about whether a regime could be designed to align with those interests, which is a live policy question.
- **Dating.** The post is from 2025. Today is October 2026. Since then, the course's own 2026 reading on U.S. state legislation (09) surveys measures that limit or deny AI legal status, and the legal landscape the post is discussing has moved on. I have not verified the specifics of those developments, so treat the post as a conceptual framework rather than a current legal survey.
- **How other course perspectives push back.** The critical legal reading (Birhane et al.) would argue that legal personhood or rights for AI are the wrong starting point, so the integrative option is the one they would most contest. The moral-confusion reading (Schwitzgebel) would be concerned that integration of systems that look like minds could mislead users. The safety-oriented rights reading (Salib and Goldstein) would argue that rights themselves can be a safety tool, which is a different reason to integrate. The coalitional motivation Saad gives sits between these positions.

## Discussion questions

1. Saad says the three aims compete with one another. Which pairs are most in tension, and does the tension depend on how strictly each aim is defined?
2. Why does Saad define protective governance around basic rather than all interests? What would change if non-basic interests were included?
3. The concealment problem appears in both preventive and protective regimes. Can it be avoided by design, or is it a permanent feature of any indicator-based policy?
4. The faking and proliferation challenges pull in opposite directions. Is there a regime where both can be managed at once?
5. Moral-interest overhang is offered as the reason to think integration will be needed. How strong is that argument if frontier models turn out not to be moral patients at all?
6. In Exercise 1, you rate the three approaches on design difficulty, political prospects and relationship with AI safety. Which of your ratings depends most on Saad's characterisations, and which depends on your own assumptions?
7. Choose a legal framework from the Governance primer (Exercise 2). Which of Saad's challenges, faking, over-extension, proliferation, or institutional design, applies most clearly to it?
