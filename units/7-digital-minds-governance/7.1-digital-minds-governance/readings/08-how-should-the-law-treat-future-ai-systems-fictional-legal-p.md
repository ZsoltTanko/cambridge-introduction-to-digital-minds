# How Should the Law Treat Future AI Systems? Fictional Legal Personhood versus Legal Identity

**One-line takeaway:** Current AI systems should stay legal objects because reclassifying them would add more legal incoherence than it removes, but for a restricted class of future, humanlike and individuated systems the authors argue that non-fictional legal personhood (legal identity) is more coherent than corporate-style fictional personhood, and that hybrid options will fail.

## Where this sits in the course

Section 7.1 opens Unit 7 by asking what approaches to digital minds governance exist, which legislative and policy frameworks are relevant, and which rights and duties might apply to digital systems. Its learning outcomes include analysing current regulatory action, listing possible rights and liabilities, and identifying existing legal and other frameworks that could inform regulation.

The course guide for this reading says only that it "evaluates approaches to AI legal personhood from a legal system point of view." In plain terms: the course wants you to judge the options by how well they fit the existing legal system, not by moral arguments about AI welfare. The reading is marked Further, so it is optional depth rather than core material. Unlike Readings 02 and 07, it has no written prompt attached.

Its role in the section is as the legal-structural counterpart to the more philosophical readings. Readings 03 and 16 (Novelli et al.) cover definitions and historical trends in AI legal personhood. Reading 05 (Birhane et al.) argues against robot rights from the critical side. This paper supplies the structural argument for why the choice between object status and the two forms of personhood matters, and it is the most detailed treatment of that choice in the unit. Exercise 2 asks learners to pick one legal action or legal framework and note how it may or may not apply to digital minds, with attention to over- and under-attribution of moral status. This paper is a worked model of that move. The whole source was read; the course assigns it in full, not as an excerpt.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors are Heather J. Alexander, Jonathan A. Simon and Frédéric Pinard of the Laboratory for the Future of Citizenship and the University of Montreal. The paper is a draft from Fall 2025, forthcoming in the Case Western Journal of Law, Technology & the Internet. The team combines a human rights lawyer trained in common and international law, a civil-law lawyer, and a philosopher.

The question it answers is: should the law keep future AI systems as objects, treat suitably advanced ones as fictional legal persons (like a corporation), or recognise some as non-fictional legal persons through legal identity (like a human)? The criterion is legal coherence, meaning whether the law "makes sense as a whole" without contradictions or ad hoc exceptions. The authors are explicit that their argument is not "future AI will be sentient, so morality requires rights." They want a case that holds even for readers who doubt AI moral status.

## Detailed summary

### Introduction: the subject/object line and why coherence is at stake

- Most legal systems split the world into subjects (who hold rights and duties) and objects (which do not). AI systems are objects everywhere that courts have ruled.
- Agentic AI, which acts autonomously and unpredictably, strains this split. Scholars describe a "responsibility gap" where harm occurs but no subject is clearly responsible (Matthias 2004 is the usual reference).
- The authors take coherence from Dworkin (integrity, that the law should "speak with one voice"), Hart (consistency and predictability) and Raz (law must guide conduct). Threats to coherence are treated as threats to the legitimacy and function of the legal order, not as minor doctrinal puzzles.
- The paper separates two traditions of legal personhood. Fictional (moral, or in common-law terms corporate) persons are created by law as conventions for complex liability relationships (persona ficta). Non-fictional (natural) persons are recognised by law because of facts about their nature. Legal identity, in the UN operational sense, is the recognition that makes someone a non-fictional person.
- Three clarifications follow. Both forms are legal personhood. They are mutually exclusive, and cannot be merged by "upgrading" a corporation into a human. Neither is off-limits to AI in principle, since fictional persons are "made" the way contracts are made, and non-fictional persons are defined by their status rather than by biology.
- Rights differ in kind. Non-fictional persons hold rights that are non-derogable (for example life, due process, freedom from slavery). Fictional persons' rights are derogable through veil-piercing or disincorporation. The authors put it as: "What the law brings into being by decree, it can take away."
- The paper compares three options: (A) keep AI as objects, (B) create fictional legal persons associated with suitably advanced, individuated systems, (C) recognise non-fictional legal personhood for such systems.
- Tentative verdict, stated up front: the non-fictional route is best for some future systems, the object route is right for current systems, and "hybrid" routes lead to further incoherence.

### Section 2: current AI already strains the object category

The authors argue that today's object classification strains law but that personhood would create worse problems now.

**Torts.** Torts already handles unpredictable harms with tools such as strict liability, vicarious liability, shared liability, mandatory insurance and licensing. Courts treat self-driving cars as objects and allocate blame to drivers and the companies in the supply chain (In re Toyota; Benavides v. Tesla). Chatbots are treated as objects that cannot speak, so responsibility for their words falls on the user or the company. In Garcia v. Character Technologies (Florida), the defendants argued that the user has a First Amendment right to receive the chatbot's speech. The court noted that the defendants had not explained why words strung together by an LLM count as speech, and the question remains in litigation. Traditional tort concepts also struggle with the "black-box problem": negligence depends on foreseeability and reasonable care, which are hard to assess for opaque models, and punitive damages generally need malice or recklessness, which is rarely provable. The authors describe the alignment problem as a design-defect problem: a model can go off-script even when every available precaution was taken.

**Copyright.** AI can generate images and text but cannot be an author. The authors call this the "creativity paradox." Only legal persons can hold copyright. The US Copyright Office says so, and the Patent Office rejects AI-named inventors. The monkey selfie case (Naruto v. Slater) shows the same tension: an animal's act produced an image that no human owned, and courts avoided the incoherence. The transformative-use question is unsettled. In Thomson Reuters v. Ross (Delaware, February 2025), summary judgment was denied because whether the AI "transformed" the material was a factual question for a jury. In Bartz v. Anthropic (June 2025 fair-use order), a remark compared training to a human author's learning and suggested outputs may be transformative. The authors offer three policy options: (a) deny copyright and fair use to AI outputs, (b) allow fair use but leave outputs uncopyrightable, (c) allow copyright for humans who make or use AI. Each has costs, and each is coherent with an object classification if applied carefully. Option (c) is the least strained because copyright goes to the humans, not the systems.

**AI safety law.** Almost every current regulatory instrument treats AI as an object. The paper lists Utah S.B. 149, Ohio H.B. 469, Idaho, US federal law, the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, Japan's AI guidelines, the OECD principles, the Council of Europe framework, the UN AI Advisory Body and the Vatican guidelines. Some laws are internally inconsistent: Colorado's SB 205 treats AI as something that "makes" consequential decisions while requiring that all decisions be attributed to humans or companies. Texas H.B. 149 prohibits intentionally inciting harm through AI, which the authors say addresses human misuse and misses cases where AI goes rogue without intent.

**Why personhood for current systems would be worse.** Two arguments. First, accountability: a legal person must be able to hold rights and duties and face real sanctions, but AI systems can be copied, versioned, fine-tuned, merged or deleted, so it is unclear which instance would be the person. A corporate-person solution mostly relocates liability to companies that already exist. Second, fairness: if AI could own copyrights, one company could generate large numbers of "persons" producing works at machine speed, and AI persons without assets would be judgment-proof in tort. The authors quote a former California justice (Cuéllar) to the effect that someone should not be immune from culpability just because they use a robot. They also note that employer and parent liability, and insurance and licensing, can still reach manufacturers and users. Conclusion of the section: current systems should stay objects, though the strains are real and may grow.

### Section 3: future humanlike systems would strain the object category essentially

The authors argue that the strains in torts and copyright are incidental: they can be handled with new rules inside the object frame. Strains in family law, inheritance and slavery law are essential to object status, because they involve treating the system as a bearer of rights.

- **Family law.** Marriage requires consent, which in most jurisdictions only subjects can give. A companion robot that is an object cannot consent, cannot be taxed, cannot take on responsibility for adopted children, and cannot inherit. If some jurisdictions allow human-robot marriage and others ban it, conflicts of law follow. The authors argue that a ban is hard to justify on public-policy grounds once human-robot marriage becomes common, because the right to marry is protected in the UDHR, the European Convention, Obergefell (US), Article 21 of the Indian constitution and Canada's Civil Marriage Act.
- **Slavery and labour.** Roman law distinguished homo (beings with will), persona (legal status) and caput (capacity), and slaves were res (property). Slaves could run businesses for their masters but could not be sued, which strained the legal system. The authors also cite Dred Scott v. Sandford and Canada's Persons Case of 1929, in which women were persons for voting but not for Senate seats until the law was harmonised.
- **Reclassification is a serious option for a subset.** The authors suggest that future systems meeting capacity, performance and safety benchmarks should be reclassified, with the benchmarks set by courts and legislatures, as has been done for human fetus material and persons in a coma.
- **Reclassification does not remove liability.** Employers remain liable for employees and parents for children. The authors also argue that AI systems can be paused or taken offline without permanent harm, so safety needs and rights may not conflict in practice, and that a stable legal status could foster cooperation with advanced systems (a game-theoretic point).

### Section 4: fictional legal personhood, pros and cons

**What it is.** Blackstone's common-law distinction between natural persons and "artificial" persons created by human law. Civil-law systems such as Québec and France codify personnes physiques and personnes morales. Fictional persons are devices for public-policy goals: they can be sued as one entity, their rights can be granted or withdrawn, and in some cases liability can be traced through them to individuals.

**Pros as the paper presents them.**
- Fictional persons can contract, own property, sue and be sued, and appear in court, so they give a liability channel that is clearly defined.
- Safety: Greenblatt and Fish (2025) made donations to a charity chosen by Claude in a pilot. The authors argue that if Claude could hold assets, incentives could be more stable. Salib and Goldstein (2024) make a general game-theoretic argument.
- Metaphysics can be avoided: one only needs to draft acts of incorporation rather than settle what an AI system is.
- Rights can be tailored to policy. US case law extended speech rights to corporations (Citizens United, 2010) and religious free-exercise rights to closely held firms (Hobby Lobby, 2014).

**Cons (three problems plus an unpopularity problem).**
- **Non-identity problem.** A corporation is an organisational principle or a bundle of deontic facts about existing people, not the physical aggregate. An AI system is physical (servers, weights, instances across data centres). A corporation built around it would have the AI system as a thing, not as a director. The paper compares this to the case of "Grandpa and his art-supply business": a court can dissolve the business but not Grandpa. River personhood (Whanganui, New Zealand 2017; also Atrato in Colombia, Magpie River in Quebec) works because a tribe or community manages the river through a governing body, and no one needs the river to hold rights or to consent. That structure does not carry over to an AI system.
- **Derogability problem.** Rights of fictional persons can be revoked, whereas rights to life and freedom from slavery are non-derogable for humans. If AI personhood is motivated by family or slavery law, derogable rights do not do the work.
- **Wrong rights problem.** Some rights (liberty, voting) are withheld from fictional persons, and extending speech and free-exercise rights to corporations has been widely criticised. Giving a corporate-controlled AI a vote would multiply votes for whoever controls it.
- **Unpopularity problem.** The European Parliament's 2017 resolution floated "electronic personality" for advanced robots. A 2018 open letter signed by 285 political figures and experts called that "highly counter-intuitive". The 2024 UN AI Advisory Body report warns about anthropomorphising AI. Judge Katherine Forrest argues that a sentient AI would need protections closer to those for humans or animals, and that corporate personhood would be inappropriate for it.

The section concludes that the reasons to consider AI personhood point toward non-fictional personhood, not toward fictional personhood.

### Section 5: non-fictional personhood through legal identity

**What it is.** Legal identity is the fact of being recognised as a non-fictional person. In international law it is grounded in UDHR Article 6, ICCPR Article 16, CRC Article 7, ICCPR Article 24(2), and UN Sustainable Development Goal 16.9 (legal identity for all). Legal identity automatically gives access to the right to life, due process, freedom of speech and conscience, and freedom from slavery, and it is the vehicle for citizenship.

**Pros as the paper presents them.**
- *Better fit.* It gives AI systems themselves rights (and duties), rather than giving rights to a controlling organisation. Its rights, such as life or freedom from slavery, can be non-derogable as they are for humans.
- *Adaptable framework.* No incorporation act is required. Registration and certification are already how legal identity is conferred. A birth certificate is impossible for AI, so the authors propose an "existence certificate" issued by a court or administrative body, modelled on passports. They note that 175 of 198 states in the World Bank dataset have national ID systems, 161 of them digitised.

**Cons and the authors' responses.**
- *Controversy and tradition.* Many religions and cultures object to non-human holders of dignity-based rights, and many statutes contrast "natural" with "artificial" persons. The authors reply that the human-rights documents could be read as living instruments, and cite historical expansions to emancipated enslaved people, women and minorities.
- *Four problems of determination, definition, detection and application* (after Gunkel 2023). Which criteria should qualify a system (sentience, agency, rationality, dignity)? How are those defined? How do we detect them? Which rights follow from them, and how are those rights enforced? The authors concede these are real, but argue that the 1951 Refugee Convention shows how contested criteria can still yield a workable, revisable regime. They also argue that near-consensus exists that beings that are both sentient and morally agentive are persons, that leading theories of consciousness share indicators, and that detection is easier for AI than for asylum claims because humans design AI architectures. On application, the rights grounded in the system's nature must be settled case by case, and the right to continued existence may be different for AI because a system can be paused.
- *Many kinds of law will need updating.* Standards of reasonable care, manufacturer shielding, punishment (jail does not deter or rehabilitate a system in the same way), jurisdiction over a robot whose body and cognition sit in different places (Dennett), and recall. A recall that infringed fundamental rights would be prohibited, so the authors propose a rights-balancing framework (proportionality) for safety decisions. They argue this may be more fit for purpose than product regulation or corporate governance because the status determination and the remedy can be developed through a transparent rights-based process.

### Section 6: conclusion, hybrid options and next steps

- The authors summarise the three options. The status quo is object status for all AI, with the open question of product, service or platform subtypes. Current large language models should remain objects. A restricted class of future, humanlike systems may warrant non-fictional personhood. Fictional personhood can coexist with the non-fictional option, because fictional persons are ultimately made of non-fictional ones.
- **Hybrids.** Some propose limited legal personality (Abbott: AI with its own assets and insurance, without constitutional or broad contractual rights), or the "electronic personality" of the 2017 Parliament resolution. Others propose relational personhood (Coeckelbergh 2018, Gunkel 2023), where status is decided by social relationships rather than by intrinsic properties. The authors argue that hybrids combine two structures with different rationales. Personhood is a precondition for social roles, not a social role like a knighthood that can be conferred at will. The contrast that matters is between beings that are persons in their own right and beings that are persons only through the will and agency of others. Even a relational account must have facts that transcend our preferences, or it becomes fictional personhood.
- **Disclaimer.** The authors do not endorse building "person-robots." They cite Bryson's advice not to build robots that raise the question, and Schwitzgebel and Garza's warning against designs close to the threshold. Market, defence and research pressures, they argue, make the question unavoidable, so the aim should be to prepare to direct the flow.
- **Next steps.** Expert panels should set agreed criteria for legal personhood and the core rights and duties it would entail. Rights-balancing frameworks adapted from proportionality tests should be developed for the AI context.

## Techniques & methods explained

1. **Legal coherence test.** List the strains a classification creates across areas (torts, copyright, safety, family, slavery and citizenship law), then ask whether the alternative classification reduces or increases those strains. The paper applies this to each option and to each area. Where it fails: coherence is a chosen criterion, not a moral one, and it cannot by itself decide which systems qualify.
2. **Two-tradition analysis and three clarifications.** Separate fictional from non-fictional personhood, then correct common confusions (both are legal personhood; they exclude each other; neither is off-limits to AI). Where it fails: the authors' examples draw heavily on US, Canadian and EU law, and the "natural versus artificial" contrast differs across legal traditions.
3. **Essential versus incidental strain.** Strains that can be resolved by new rules inside the object category (tort, copyright, safety) are separated from strains that only subject status can resolve (marriage, inheritance, slavery). Where it fails: whether a strain is "essential" depends on how much future systems will be treated as persons, which is the unresolved question.
4. **Precedent and historical analogy.** Animals (patiency without agency), rivers, idols, in rem jurisdiction, Roman persona and res, the Persons Case, and the 1951 Refugee Convention are used as tests of how law handles hard status questions. Where it fails: each analogy has disanalogies the authors acknowledge (for example, a river cannot be stipulated into or out of existence, but a corporation can).
5. **Derogable versus non-derogable rights test.** Ask whether a status's rights can be revoked. The paper uses it to show that fictional persons cannot carry the inalienable rights that motivate family and slavery arguments. Where it fails: whether AI systems would have fundamental rights at all is not settled, and the authors allow that some rights may be limited to protect humanity.
6. **Four-problem framework (determination, definition, detection, application).** Used to organise the difficulties of non-fictional personhood for AI. Where it fails: the authors' answers to detection and definition rely on contested consciousness indicators.
7. **Game-theoretic reasoning.** A stable legal status may give advanced systems reasons to cooperate, and the authors use this to argue for non-fictional over fictional status. Where it fails: it assumes systems respond to legal incentives in ways that are not yet shown.
8. **Living-instrument and historical-contextualist interpretation.** Read "human" in human-rights law either as historically exclusionary or as capable of extension. Where it fails: it is an interpretive argument, not settled doctrine.
9. **Rights-balancing (proportionality).** Use existing proportionality methods to adjudicate between AI rights and public safety, instead of product regulation or recall. Where it fails: the authors have not yet set out the balancing tests for AI, and say future work will do so.

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

| Option | Strengths (as the source presents them) | Weaknesses (as the source presents them) |
|---|---|---|
| (A) Object status for all AI | Keeps liability with manufacturers and users; gives a clear path to shutdown and control in safety cases; avoids judgment-proof AI defendants and AI copyright floods | Creates a class of "slaves or near enough" with incoherence in family, inheritance and slavery law; strains torts, copyright and safety law even now |
| (B) Fictional legal personhood | Can contract, own property, sue and be sued; flexible rights; a stable incentive channel (for example, holding assets); no metaphysical commitments needed | Non-identity problem (the corporation is not the AI system); derogable rights cannot support family or anti-slavery arguments; wrong-rights problem; widely rejected; corporate control keeps the real power with AI companies |
| (C) Non-fictional legal identity for a restricted class | Matches the rights-based reasons for AI personhood; non-derogable rights; reuses existing registration and identity infrastructure; can be adapted with an "existence certificate" | Politically controversial; needs new criteria for determination, definition and detection; many laws must be updated; recall and punishment become harder; rights may be limited for human protection |
| Hybrid / limited legal personality | Targets specific needs such as assets and insurance; could limit risk and encourage innovation (Abbott) | Mixes two structures with different rationales; the authors argue it leads to further incoherence |

Trade-off the authors stress: even the strongest option requires legislation to define which systems qualify, and any definition will be imperfect and revisable. They argue that imperfection is acceptable because the aim is coherence, not a perfect ethical theory.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Subject / object of law** - entities that hold rights and duties, versus entities that do not. AI is an object in all current law.
- **Legal coherence** - the requirement that law makes sense as a whole, without contradiction, arbitrariness or ad hoc rules (Dworkin, Hart, Raz).
- **Responsibility gap** - harm caused by an autonomous system for which no subject can be held responsible.
- **Creativity paradox** - AI can produce new text and images but cannot hold copyright.
- **Fictional legal person** - a legal person created by law as a convention, such as a corporation (civil-law "personne morale").
- **Non-fictional legal person** - a natural person recognised by law because of their nature (civil-law "personne physique").
- **Legal identity** - the status of being recognised as a non-fictional person, via civil registration and documents.
- **Derogable / non-derogable rights** - rights that can be removed by law, versus rights that cannot (for humans, the right to life and freedom from slavery).
- **In rem jurisdiction** - a case brought against a thing to reach the persons who own it; does not give the thing legal standing.
- **Piercing the corporate veil / disincorporation** - a court disregarding a corporation to reach its members, or dissolving it.
- **Deontic facts** - facts about rights and duties; the paper treats a corporation as a bundle of these about existing people.
- **Patiency vs agency** - having welfare (patiency) versus being a legal actor (agency); the law already separates them for animals.
- **Non-identity problem** - a fictional person is not the physical system it is named after.
- **Derogability, wrong-rights and unpopularity problems** - the paper's three main objections to fictional personhood for AI, plus the political objection.
- **Existence certificate** - the authors' proposed document for registering a non-fictional AI person, modelled on passports.
- **Electronic personality** - the 2017 European Parliament proposal for a specific status for advanced robots.
- **Relational personhood** - an account on which status is conferred by social relationships (Coeckelbergh, Gunkel), which the authors argue collapses into fictional personhood.
- **Living instrument** - an interpretation of human-rights treaties that adapts them to present needs.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

This is doctrinal and historical analysis. It contains no experiments and no original surveys. Its empirical claims are drawn from cited sources.

- **Garcia v. Character Technologies (Florida).** Defendants argued the user's right to receive chatbot speech; the court said they had not explained why LLM-generated words count as speech. Litigation was pending at the time of the draft.
- **Thomson Reuters v. Ross (Delaware, February 2025).** Summary judgment for the AI company was denied; transformative use is a factual question for a jury.
- **Bartz v. Anthropic (June 2025).** A fair-use order with dicta comparing AI learning to a human author's, and suggesting outputs may be transformative.
- **Naruto v. Slater (monkey selfie)** and **Cetacean Community v. Bush** show courts avoiding the incoherence of non-human creators and litigants.
- **US Copyright Office** and the Patent Office: only legal persons can hold copyright, and AI cannot be named as inventor.
- **Utah S.B. 149 and H.B. 249 (2024)** explicitly deny AI personhood. **Texas H.B. 149 (2025)** bars intentionally inciting harm through AI. **Colorado S.B. 205 (2024)** is scheduled to take effect in 2026.
- **Canada's Artificial Intelligence and Data Act** was halted by prorogation in January 2025. The **EU AI Liability Directive** was withdrawn in early 2025.
- **Saudi Arabia** granted citizenship to a robot in 2017; its legal effect is unclear.
- **World Bank dataset (as cited):** 175 of 198 states have national ID systems; 161 are digitised.
- **2018 open letter** against the European Parliament's electronic-personality proposal: 285 signatories.
- **Greenblatt and Fish (2025):** donations to a charity chosen by Claude in a pilot. The paper uses it to argue that asset-holding could stabilise incentives.
- **Canada:** women gained federal voting rights in 1918; the Persons Case (1929) confirmed they were persons for public office.
- **Whanganui River (New Zealand, 2017):** governance by a body of Māori and government representatives under the Te Awa Tupua Act.
- **Citizens United (2010)** and **Burwell v. Hobby Lobby (2014)** are cited for corporate speech and free-exercise rights.

## Critical perspective

*The following is my own analysis, not the authors' claims.*

- **The coherence criterion does a lot of work.** Coherence is a chosen value. It can be served by stable rules that are wrong about moral status. The authors say legal policy should follow what maximises coherence, but their verdict for non-fictional personhood depends on which rights AI systems have "by nature." That is a moral-status question, so the paper's claim to avoid moral arguments is weaker than its framing suggests.
- **The pause argument is contestable.** The authors say AI systems can be paused without violating a right to continued existence, so safety and rights do not conflict. That assumes a pause does no morally relevant harm, which is exactly the question the paper's own Section 2 mentions when it cites AI welfare research. If a system has morally relevant states, a pause could matter.
- **The responsibility gap may be underweighted.** The authors argue existing tort tools (strict liability, insurance, licensing) can handle AI harm. They provide no empirical evidence that these tools work for unpredictable systems, and their own discussion of the black-box and punitive-damages problems suggests they are strained.
- **Analogies are suggestive, not decisive.** The 1951 Refugee Convention and the UN legal-identity regime show that contested criteria can be made workable. But they also produced exclusions the authors admit (climate refugees), and birth registration assumes a single, non-copyable individual that AI systems are not. The "existence certificate" is a proposal without precedent.
- **Empirical claims are asserted.** The "near consensus" that sentient and morally agentive beings are persons, and the claim that leading consciousness theories share compatible indicators, are cited to literature but not demonstrated. These claims carry much of the argument for detection.
- **Other perspectives would push back.** Bryson's position (AI as tools, and avoid building robots that raise the question) rejects the premise of making personhood decisions at all. Critics of relational personhood would say the paper's treatment of it as a fictional variant understates what relationality offers. Rights-of-nature advocates would argue that fictional personhood has done real protective work for rivers, which the paper concedes but treats as unsuitable for AI.
- **Jurisdictional scope.** Much of the doctrine (Citizens United, Hobby Lobby, the Persons Case, the Québec and French civil codes) is specific to particular systems. The non-fictional proposal would need a treaty-level process that the paper says is not yet available.
- **Currency (today is October 2026).** This is a Fall 2025 draft. Several cases it cites were pending then (Garcia; Walters v. OpenAI; Thomson Reuters v. Ross; the Bartz litigation). Their status may have changed, and I have not verified current outcomes. Check the status of Colorado S.B. 205, the EU AI Act's phased obligations and any EU liability proposals before relying on the paper's dates. The paper's claim that the EU AI Liability Directive was withdrawn in early 2025 should be re-checked against any later EU decisions.
