# AI as legal persons: past, patterns and prospects

**One-line takeaway:** Whether AI can be a legal person depends first on which theory of legal personhood you hold (a minimal "single right" view or a broader "cluster of roles" view), and in practice on statute, the EU's product-based regulatory approach, and whether AI can show sustained social participation.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors are legal scholars and philosophers of technology. They aim to map the debate on AI legal personhood, to show that the debate is shaped by path dependencies (prevailing theories of personhood and the rise and fall of AI technology), and to argue that the legal system's resistance to AI personhood is currently strong, particularly in the EU.

The assigned section answers a preliminary question: before asking whether AI can be a legal person, what does "legal person" mean? The section asks what roles or rights a person in law holds, and then sets out two main theories of that concept and how each would apply to AI.

## Detailed summary (assigned excerpt: Section 2)

### The origin of "persona"

The section starts with the history of the word. "Persona" first meant a theatrical mask, then the roles a human could play in legal life (a debtor, creditor, owner, plaintiff or defendant), and finally the abstract ability to take part in legal relations at all.

In Roman law (the authors cite Gaius), the term applied in principle to every human being, but a second, selective sense also existed: the concrete ability to take on legal roles. Enslaved people were excluded from this second sense because they were treated as property rather than as active participants. Over time, as slavery was abolished, the idea that all humans have legal personhood in principle became dominant. The same concept was then extended to collective bodies such as companies and associations, which act through their members and officers (their "organs").

The result is the modern division between **natural persons** (human beings) and **juridical persons** (collective entities).

### The preliminary question: what does legal personhood mean?

To ask whether AI can be a person, the authors say, we first need to know what personhood consists of. Legal personhood is the ability to hold roles (rights, duties and powers) that are not available to non-persons, such as things. Two accounts of this follow.

### The singularist view

The singularist view is a minimal account. An entity is a legal person if it holds, or can hold, a single right or duty. The notion is tied to the idea that a right is a legally protected interest, which lets the law attribute personhood to beings that cannot take initiative themselves. On this account, if legal norms protect nonhuman animals, fetuses, trees or rivers, those entities count as legal persons as well.

The authors flag that this has an obvious cost. It does not match practice or common beliefs, because these entities are generally not treated as legal persons in practice.

### The cluster view

The cluster view, associated with Kurki, Naffine and Ohlin, treats personhood as a bundle of roles. In private law this bundle includes making contracts, owning property, protection against harm by others, the power to start legal proceedings, and liability for one's own wrongs. Adult humans typically hold the whole bundle. Limits are common:

- Children and incapacitated adults are persons in law but cannot contract or litigate on their own initiative. They need guardians.
- Corporations can contract and sue (through their administrators) but are excluded from family roles, such as being a spouse or child, and from some fundamental and data protection rights (the authors cite EU data protection law).
- Personhood can be restricted by minority, incapacity or, in some legal systems, by gender or social position. The authors note that some people have been stripped of almost all personhood, the "right to have rights" being the phrase they use.

On this view, all these statuses are variations from a standard case, the default cluster held by adult humans.

### Applying the two views to AI

The authors show how the debate forks depending on the view:

- **Singularist:** the question is whether AI features resemble those that justify single rights for other non-human entities. If an AI system were sentient, one could argue for extending anti-mistreatment protection in the way animals are protected.
- **Cluster:** the question is which roles, from the human or corporate cluster, an AI system should hold, possibly with restrictions. There are two templates:
  - Treat AI like a minor or an incapacitated person. It would get protection from harm and eligibility for some benefits, but no independent capacity to contract or sue.
  - Treat AI like a corporation. It would get limited protections but could receive general capacity to contract and initiate lawsuits.

Both templates face the same difficulty: there is no uncontroversial criterion for deciding whether AI should be assimilated to humans or corporations, or differentiated from them. The authors set aside the case where AI becomes indistinguishable from humans, because that would end the debate rather than resolve it.

### The role of abstraction

The authors make a subtle point. Traits such as rationality or sentience lie on a continuum, and what matters is the level of abstraction at which they are defined. If rationality means elementary planning or pattern recognition, and sentience means reacting appropriately to wanted or unwanted inputs, then humans and some AI systems plausibly share both. If the concepts are more demanding, the conclusion flips. The conclusion of any argument therefore depends on the definitions chosen.

### Two perspectives on the law

The debate also divides by method:

- **De lege lata** asks whether existing law already recognises, or could reasonably be read to recognise, some degree of personhood for AI.
- **De lege ferenda** asks whether new laws should be enacted to grant personhood explicitly.

Both use normative criteria to decide whether personhood is justified.

### Two rationales for personhood

The section distinguishes two kinds of justification:

- **Non-instrumental:** AI deserves personhood for its intrinsic features. In Kantian terms, it is an "end in itself". Examples: AI capable of rational thought should receive the protections given to rational beings, or AI capable of suffering should be protected like other sentient beings.
- **Instrumental:** AI deserves personhood for the benefit of human interests, such as people who rely on AI in business or in social life. Here personhood is a tool, not a recognition of value in the AI itself. The authors cite Novelli (2023) for this rationale.

The section ends with this distinction, which the later sections use to assess the trends and to argue that current legal thinking is mostly instrumental.

## Techniques & methods explained

The "methods" in this excerpt are argumentative and conceptual tools, not experiments.

| Tool | What it is | How it works | What it is for | Where it runs into trouble |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Singularist vs cluster distinction | Two theories of what personhood consists of | Singularist: one right or duty is enough. Cluster: a bundle of roles, with the adult human as the default case and everything else a variation | Sorting the debate into two questions: which single right fits AI, and which bundle AI should hold | The singularist view makes trees and rivers persons. The cluster view has no fixed content, and the bundle can be cut arbitrarily |
| Level-of-abstraction test | A way to decide whether AI and humans share a trait | Define the trait at a chosen level (for example "planning" or "reacting to inputs"). Check whether observable behaviour meets it. Repeat with more demanding definitions | Showing that conclusions depend on the definition used | It can make most traits look shared at a low level, and the choice of level is itself contested |
| Assimilation templates | Two analogies for the AI cluster | Compare AI to minors and incapacitated adults (protection, no capacity), or to corporations (limited protections, capacity to contract and sue) | Offering concrete models that the law already uses | Neither analogy fits AI in every respect, and the authors say both face difficulties |
| De lege lata / de lege ferenda | Two legal perspectives | Ask whether the current law already supports AI personhood, or whether a new law should create it | Separating what is possible now from what policy might change | The line between interpreting existing law and reforming it is contested |
| Instrumental vs non-instrumental rationale | Two justifications for personhood | Justify by intrinsic features (sentience, rationality) or by benefits to humans | Showing what kind of argument a proposal rests on | Intrinsic features are hard to establish, and instrumental personhood can be used as a shield for developers |
| Analogy from history (Roman law, corporations) | Reasoning from earlier extensions of personhood | Show how personhood was extended to slaves, collective bodies and others, and check whether the same criteria fit AI | Showing the law has extended personhood before | Historical analogies depend on which criteria are treated as the real reason for the extension |

## Pros, cons & trade-offs it surfaces

The section is mainly conceptual, so the trade-offs are about theories rather than policies. Source's view (as presented) is separated from the commentary below.

| Position or tool | Strength (as the source presents it) | Weakness or trade-off (as the source presents it) |
|---|---|---|
| Singularist view | Minimal and simple. Does not require AI to have a full set of human-like capacities | Too broad: it would make animals, fetuses, trees and rivers legal persons, which does not match practice |
| Cluster view | Matches how the law actually allocates roles to humans, children and corporations | Vague: there is no agreed core list, and the cluster can be shaped by restrictions based on features the law treats as relevant |
| Level-of-abstraction reasoning | Shows that conclusions depend on definitions, which clarifies disagreement | Leaves open which level is right, so it does not settle the dispute on its own |
| Instrumental rationale | Practical: personhood can enable contracts and liability for AI-using businesses | Can be a shield for developers and manufacturers, avoiding responsibility |
| Non-instrumental rationale | Ties personhood to intrinsic features, in line with Kantian thinking | Depends on contested claims about AI rationality or sentience |

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Legal personhood** - the ability to hold legal roles (rights, duties, powers) that things cannot hold, as used here.
- **Natural person** - a human being, treated as a legal person.
- **Juridical person** - a collective entity (company, association) treated as a legal person, acting through organs.
- **Persona** - originally a theatre mask, then a legal role, and finally the abstract capacity for legal interaction.
- **Singularist view** - a minimal account: holding one right or duty is enough for personhood.
- **Cluster view** - personhood as a bundle of roles, with the adult human as the default case.
- **Default cluster** - the full set of roles typically given to adult humans, against which other statuses are measured.
- **Level of abstraction** - the level at which a trait (such as rationality or sentience) is defined, which determines whether humans and AI share it.
- **De lege lata** - the law as it currently stands.
- **De lege ferenda** - the law as it should be reformed or enacted.
- **Non-instrumental rationale** - justifying personhood by the intrinsic characteristics of the entity (an "end in itself").
- **Instrumental rationale** - justifying personhood by the benefits it brings to humans.
- **Right to have rights** - the phrase (from Arendt) for the basic entitlement to any legal personhood, which the authors use to show how personhood can be stripped.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

The excerpt has few empirical data points. Its examples are conceptual and historical:

- **Roman law:** enslaved people were excluded from the active sense of personhood because they were treated as things (the authors' reading of Roman law).
- **Animals, fetuses, trees and rivers:** given as examples that the singularist view would count as persons, which the authors call an unwelcome consequence.
- **Children and incapacitated adults:** persons in law who need guardians to act, showing that the cluster view allows partial personhood.
- **Corporations:** persons that can contract and sue through administrators, but lack family roles and some fundamental and data protection rights (the authors cite EU data protection law).
- **Arendt:** the "right to have rights" as the threshold for any personhood.
- **Level of abstraction:** the authors' example that elementary planning and reacting to inputs may be shared by humans and some AI.

## Beyond the assigned excerpt

The source file also contains Sections 1 and 3-5. These are not assigned, but they show where Section 2 leads.

- **Introduction (Section 1):** opens with Schwarz's (1908) fable of ancient philosophers puzzled by a streetcar with no visible horse. The authors use it to show that legal personhood often grows from analogy and then becomes a practical matter of accountability. They also set out the two path dependencies (theory of personhood and technology).
- **Trends of the debate (Section 3):** traces the legal literature from Tuchler (1978) and Shapiro (1982), through Butler (1981) and Gemignani (1983) on copyright and liability, to Willick's proposal of "partial personality" (1983, 1985), Cole (1990) on tort liability, and Solum (1992), who uses trustee and constitutional-rights thought experiments. Section 3.1 covers the 1990s and 2000s, when statistical AI shifted the focus to concrete agents. The authors argue that the debate rises and falls with AI "summers" and "winters" and that policy events (about 2017) drove a further surge in publications after 2018.
- **Legislation and judicial uncertainty (Section 4):** Saudi Arabia's 2017 citizenship for the robot Sophia, which the authors call political choreography. The 2017 European Parliament resolution proposing "electronic personhood" failed to gain support. The European Commission's Expert Group opposed personhood and recommended strict liability for high-risk AI. The authors say the EU AI Act, the reformed Product Liability Directive and the proposed AI Liability Directive treat AI as a product. They predict that courts will move case by case. They draw on Banteka's (2020) US case-law study, which found that statute was the main condition for personhood (about 45%) and the right to sue and be sued was second (about 15%). They also note the circularity in courts' reasoning: rights such as suing are outcomes of personhood, not independent tests.
- **Prospects (Section 5):** near-term AI agents and generative models, the need for risk-based gradations of personhood, and long-term scenarios in which brain-machine interfaces might make AI an extension of the human legal person.

## Critical perspective

*The following is my own analysis, not the source's claims.*

- **Dated content:** the chapter was published in 2025, and today is 2026-10-08. Several EU points need checking. My understanding is that the Commission announced in 2025 that it would withdraw the proposed AI Liability Directive, which would weaken the chapter's claim that the directive is part of a settled "regulatory triad". I have not verified this against the official record, so treat it as something to check. The AI Act is in force with phased application, and the reformed Product Liability Directive is adopted but applies later. The chapter's broad conclusion (EU law treats AI as a product, not a person) still reads as accurate as of the date.
- **The singularist view is presented in a strong form.** The authors use trees and rivers to show it is too broad. The reductio is effective, but the chapter gives no supporting source for how often the view is actually held in that strong form, so the reader should check whether the target is a view anyone defends.
- **The cluster view leaves the content open.** The authors say it is a bundle of roles, but they do not say which roles are necessary or who decides. This makes the cluster view flexible, which is an advantage for comparison, and a weakness if you want a test that gives a yes or no answer.
- **Level of abstraction is a powerful but under-specified move.** It shows that conclusions depend on definitions, but it does not say how to pick the level. A critic could say the authors can make almost any AI trait look shared by choosing a low enough level.
- **Sentience and rationality are assumed to be measurable.** The non-instrumental rationale depends on criteria for intrinsic features, which the chapter does not supply. Unit 2 shows how disputed those criteria are.
- **Instrumental vs non-instrumental is a clean split that real cases may not respect.** Many proposals mix both, for example a legal form that protects a system (instrumental) because it might suffer (non-instrumental).
- **How the course's other perspectives would push back:** Birhane et al. (reading 05) would argue that the legal rationales for robot rights are the wrong frame, and that personhood might divert attention from accountability. Schwitzgebel and Sebo (reading 10) would say the cluster view must include the duty not to confuse users about AI status. Saad (reading 02) would ask whether the preventive, protective and integrative options are better framed than legal personhood alone.
