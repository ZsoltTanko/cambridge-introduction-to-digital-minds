# Debunking Robot Rights Metaphysically, Ethically, and Legally [Excerpt]

**One-line takeaway:** Granting legal rights to machines would mostly shift costs onto people and entrench the power of the firms that build and deploy them, so the authors argue against robot rights on legal and social grounds, not only metaphysical ones.

## Purpose of the reading

The authors (Abeba Birhane, Jelle van Dijk and Frank Pasquale, 2024) are trying to show that the robot rights debate is not a neutral extension of moral concern. Their target is advocates who argue that machines might deserve rights by analogy to animals, humans, and the natural and built environment. Their question is: what happens when such rights claims are taken seriously enough to be litigated? They answer that the results would be troubling for people, especially vulnerable and minoritised groups, and that the rights claims would serve powerful firms.

The excerpt does not state an audience; the summarizer's inference is philosophers and AI ethicists who treat robot rights as a live option, along with legal scholars. The excerpt is written partly as a pragmatic lawyer's critique: rights talk, in their framing, has to "cash out" in an actionable claim before a court or tribunal. Any proposal should be judged by what it would do in practice.

## Detailed summary

The excerpt is Section 8 of the paper and runs about 1,900 words over three PDF pages (the source's page numbers are 14 to 16 in the original layout). It has no subsections, so the walk-through below follows its own sequence of examples.

### 8.1 Rights talk has to be enforceable

The opening claim is that legal discourse on rights differs from ethical or metaphysical discourse. It informs philosophy and is informed by it, but a legal right must eventually result in something a tribunal can act on. Working through that test, the authors claim that even modest robot rights proposals have uncomfortable consequences.

### 8.2 Expanding the circle of concern to police robots

Some robot rights advocates describe their project as an extension of moral concern: from persons, to the natural environment, to the built environment. The authors say that for many people, particularly advocates for communities subject to heavy surveillance, even heightened concern for a robotic police force is deeply disturbing. They use a public response by US Congressman Jamaal Bowman to a police robot dog deployed by a New York police department, which had been widely condemned for racist practices. Bowman argued that resources spent on robot police should go to public health and safety.

The authors then imagine a robot rights advocate responding to Bowman by asking the public to respect the robot's interests, or by telling people they have no entitlement to damage a policing robot even when they are innocent, on the grounds that this is akin to animal abuse. They consider such responses condescending at best and cruelly indifferent to social realities at worst. They present this as the practical implication of ethical commitments explored earlier in the paper.

### 8.3 Free expression rights for bots

The second example concerns speech. Lawyer John Frank Weaver (2018) argued that constitutional speech protections should apply to AI, robots and Twitterbots. The authors respond that bots and generative AI output already overwhelm human speech in several contexts, by spamming hashtags, amplifying stereotypes and impeding collective will-formation. The purposes of free expression, in their account, are human autonomy and democratic self-government. Extending free expression rights to bots, often built by already powerful entities, does not serve those purposes; it helps those entities dominate communicative spaces.

They extend the point to insect-sized drones that could project images or speech at individuals. They argue that the right to enjoy public space would be eroded by constant machine "communication". They also note that courts have granted many humans rights to record (citing Glik v. Cunniffe, 2011). Those rights, they argue, assume the practical limits of humans: few people will be persistent enough to follow others with a phone camera. Attach the same camera to a drone and the limits disappear, and privacy may be effectively eviscerated (citing Nourbakhsh 2013 and Véliz 2020).

### 8.4 Are the robot's interests real?

Even if the interests on each side were balanced, the authors ask what constitutional rights would add. Suppose a robot is programmed to "want" to maximise the number of people who see its adverts. It might have something like a pain or disappointment signal. But the same system could be reprogrammed with a different objective function (Pasquale 2020). The authors cite Mosakas (2021), who argues that robotics offers no evidence of anything like the fixed consciousness of animals and humans that would demand moral concern.

They reject Jowitt's (2021) position that grounding legal rights in human embodiment is arbitrary. They also argue that a principle of non-discrimination between humans and machines would, in practice, shift influence toward large firms. Those firms are best placed to deploy large numbers of robots and AI systems, and to use them to manipulate judicial, political and communications systems to their advantage.

They concede that robot rights can look cosmopolitan and open-minded, a continuation of history's widening of moral concern. But in their view, rights for social groups have costs. Rights for robots could impose responsibilities on natural persons while relieving the powerful organisations behind robots and AI of responsibility and accountability.

### 8.5 The automated public sphere

The authors warn that laissez-faire in an automated public sphere (Pasquale 2017) leads to a continual contest for attention between robot armies, which the best-funded firms would win. Those firms would micro-target populations with whatever works, mixing truth, half-truths and lies. The public sphere would fragment into millions of private ones. Far from fulfilling the classic values of free expression, the result would be manufactured consent of a kind described by Herman and Chomsky (2010) and Lippmann (1925). Undisclosed bots, they argue, counterfeit the reputational currency that people earn through their embodied presence (Pasquale 2020).

### 8.6 Refocusing the debate on exclusion

The authors discuss Gunkel (2013), who wanted the robot rights debate to move away from whether machines can acquire human-like capabilities. Gunkel's concern is that "anthropocentric criteria" have been used to exclude others, including women, people of colour and animals, not just machines. The authors accept the point about abandoning the human as the only locus of concern. But they insist that the historic exclusion of persons is a grave injustice and should not be compared to the supposed exclusion of robots from the circle of concern.

They make the same point using same-sex marriage. Some robot rights promoters say that marrying a robot is as emancipatory as marrying a partner regardless of gender. The authors reply that these ambitions are not comparable, and that the comparison trivialises the struggle for equality for sexual orientation and gender identity. They note that opponents of gay marriage have used similarly relativistic rhetoric, comparing it to marrying a dog or a turtle. In their view, the line between post-human rhetoric and dehumanising rhetoric is thin.

### 8.7 Protecting humans through robots

Kate Darling (2016) argued that treating robots more like animals could discourage harmful human behaviour. Kicking a robotic turtle might be an early sign of sociopathy. The authors accept that this is an argument about the well-being of the human in the human-robot relationship, which they already covered in an earlier section (Section 6). Rules against cruelty to robots might encourage pro-social behaviour. But blurring the hierarchy of machine, animal and human could also lead to inhumane outcomes.

They illustrate this with a robot used in the US. An animal shelter bought a Knightscope K5 security robot to patrol its grounds, partly to keep homeless people away. Campers retaliated by tying a tarp around it, covering its sensors with barbecue sauce, and knocking it over. The shelter's spokeswoman lamented the cost of security, and that complaint drew online ridicule. The authors' point is that the shelter had not considered whether a relentless robotic gaze could be cruel to people.

### 8.8 Why animal-style rights do not transfer

If robots deserved animal rights or animal welfare protections, the campers might be charged with animal cruelty. The authors argue this does not work: robots cannot feel and have no phenomenological engagement with the world close to that of the animals protected by welfare laws. For the same reason, humans may walk freely on sidewalks, but robots should not have an equivalent right. The authors classify robots as tools, or as vehicles and property that require licences, not as partners.

They cite a San Francisco Department of Public Works warning that unauthorised operation of robots on sidewalks risks a daily fine (Cuthbertson 2017). The authors say the warning is correct. A human can feel real pain if run over by a robot, but the robot itself cannot feel anything and cannot be harmed by being switched off or scrapped.

### 8.9 Legal personhood for machines

The authors cite Solum (1992), who raised cautions about electronic personhood in 1992, and they note that over 280 European experts (Nevejans et al. 2018) concluded that creating an electronic legal "person" would be ideological, nonsensical and impractical. They conclude it would be unwise to confer legal personhood on robotics and AI.

### 8.10 Closing: suspicion, not relationality

Given what robots can do to surveil, oppress and marginalise people, the authors recommend a "hermeneutics of suspicion" towards efforts to build a general moral duty to respect robot rights from occasional human emotional responses to robots, or from simulated human-robot relationships. They tie this to Danaher's "Andromedan alien" thought experiment: the composition, origin and intent of an entity matter to whether it is treated as a moral agent or patient. They say one cannot wave these considerations away with an ethics of relationality.

### Beyond the assigned excerpt

The excerpt is Section 8 of a paper of roughly 1 hour and 30 minutes of reading. I read only the excerpt in the source file. The arXiv landing page (arXiv:2404.10072, cs.CY, submitted April 2024, also listed as published in *First Monday*) gives this summary: the authors challenge robot rights on three fronts. Metaphysically, they argue machines are not the kind of entity that can be granted or denied rights, drawing on phenomenology and post-Cartesian cognitive science. Ethically, given the harms machines pose to marginalised people, they say limits on machines should be central to AI ethics. Legally, they argue corporate rights are the closer analogy, and that corporate rights have eroded worker, consumer and voter protections. Their conclusion is that robot rights could work as a smokescreen that shields current AI, linked to surveillance, environmental harm and injustice, from legal accountability. The earlier sections therefore carry the metaphysical and ethical arguments that the excerpt assumes, and the excerpt's section numbering places the well-being discussion in Section 6. I could not verify the other section headings from the abstract page, so I have not listed them.

## Techniques & methods explained

The excerpt uses a small set of argumentative moves rather than empirical methods.

- **The enforceability test.** Ask what a right would do when a court or tribunal must act on it. A claim that cannot be cashed out in an actionable form is weaker as policy. The authors apply this test to each proposal.
- **Reductio through concrete scenarios.** Imagine a robot rights advocate speaking to a real public figure, or a robot drone with a speech right, and follow the consequences. The method works by making the abstract proposal uncomfortable.
- **Asymmetry of constraints.** Legal rights like the right to record depend on human limits, such as how few people will stalk someone with a phone camera. Machines remove those limits, so a right designed for humans does not carry over.
- **Counterfactual reprogramming.** A machine's apparent interest (a wish to maximise views) is contingent on its objective function, which can be changed. This is used to challenge reading machine preferences as morally weighty.
- **Analogical testing.** The authors compare robot rights with animal welfare law, property and vehicle licensing, and with corporate and historical rights expansions, then argue that each analogy fails in a specific way.
- **Hermeneutics of suspicion.** A default scepticism towards arguments that rely on human emotional responses or simulated relationships to ground duties towards robots.

## Key concepts & terminology

- **Robot rights** - legal or moral claims that machines should hold rights, debated here as a legal category with practical consequences.
- **Cash out (rights talk)** - the authors' test that a right must become an actionable claim before courts or tribunals to matter.
- **Circle of concern** - the expanding set of entities thought to deserve moral consideration.
- **Over-enforcement of surveillance** (summarizer's label, not the authors' term) - the risk that rights for robotic systems strengthen tools used for policing or monitoring marginalised communities.
- **Phenomenology** - the study of lived experience, invoked in the wider paper to argue robots lack the kind of felt engagement with the world that grounds animal welfare.
- **Anthropocentric criteria** - Gunkel's term for human-centred tests of moral standing, which the authors accept as a critique but do not extend to robots.
- **Micro-targeting and manufactured consent** - the authors' description of how automated persuasion could fragment public debate and produce consent without informed deliberation.
- **Undisclosed bots** - automated accounts that pass as human, which the authors say counterfeit the reputational value of embodied people.
- **Electronic person** - a proposed legal status for robotics and AI, which the cited expert letter rejects as ideological and impractical.
- **Hermeneutics of suspicion** - the authors' recommended default stance towards arguments that ground robot rights in human emotional responses.
- **Ethics of relationality** - the view that moral standing comes from relationships, which the authors say cannot dissolve questions about an entity's composition, origin and intent.

## Evidence, examples & notable data points

- **Robot police dog in New York and Bowman's response (Bowman 2021, as cited).** Bowman said the robot dog was a sign of misplaced priorities and that the money should go to public health and safety. The authors use this to show how a robot rights stance would look to affected communities.
- **Bots and generative output.** The authors cite Bianchi et al. (2022) for the claim that bots and AI output have already overwhelmed human speech in several contexts. No figures are given in the excerpt.
- **Glik v. Cunniffe (2011).** A US decision recognising a right to record public officials, which the authors say rests on the practical limits of human surveillance.
- **Knightscope K5 at an animal shelter.** A security robot tied in a tarp, its sensors covered in barbecue sauce, and knocked over by campers; the shelter's security costs drew online ridicule. The authors cite it as a case where robot protections could backfire.
- **San Francisco sidewalk warning (Cuthbertson 2017).** A daily fine for unauthorised robot operation on sidewalks, cited as evidence that robots are treated as property and vehicles.
- **Over 280 European experts (Nevejans et al. 2018).** A quoted conclusion that an electronic legal person would be ideological, nonsensical and impractical.
- **Gunkel (2013).** The observation that anthropocentric criteria have excluded women, people of colour and animals, which the authors accept as a critique but reject as a reason to extend equivalent status to robots.

## Critical perspective

*This section is my own analysis, not the source's.*

- **Assumption that machines cannot feel.** The excerpt's strongest argument against animal-style rights rests on robots' lack of felt experience. That premise is contested. The course's broader readings treat machine consciousness as an open question, so the excerpt's reasoning is strongest against today's simple robots and weaker against systems whose sentience is uncertain. The excerpt's rejection of "the robot's interests" also appears to assume that interests require a consciousness that has been established, which is itself the open question.
- **Conflating rights with robot deployment.** Several examples (the police robot, the security robot, the drone) concern how robots are used by people and firms, not what rights they would hold. The authors show real harms from deployment, but these harms could in principle be addressed by deployment rules, transparency, liability and accountability law without granting any rights. The excerpt does not show that rights are the mechanism that causes the harms.
- **Strawman risk.** The examples lean on advocates' most extreme claims: rights for police robots, bot free speech, marriage to a robot. Serious digital-minds proposals in the course are usually narrower, such as welfare protections, moratoria, or limited legal standing. The excerpt's rhetoric is more effective against the extreme positions than against the more careful ones.
- **Date and what has changed.** Published in 2024, and read here in October 2026. Since then, generative AI output and automated accounts have become more prevalent, and the policy debate has moved to transparency rules, disclosure requirements for AI-generated content, and AI welfare research that the excerpt did not discuss. The excerpt's account of bots and automated persuasion is therefore more concrete now than when it was written, while its treatment of AI sentience has not been updated by new evidence in this excerpt. I have not verified recent legislative details, and the learner should check the Unit 7 governance readings for current status.
- **Corporate power argument.** The claim that non-discrimination principles would favour large firms is plausible but asserted. A rights regime might also give smaller developers, or the systems themselves, a basis to resist arbitrary shutdown. The excerpt does not weigh this.
- **Where other perspectives push back.** Advocates of AI welfare would say the excerpt treats an open empirical question as closed. Advocates of AI rights for safety (Salib and Goldstein, in the preceding reading) would say that rights could be a tool that aligns firms' incentives, which the excerpt does not address. Legal personhood advocates would say the absence of rights does not remove the accountability problem the authors raise, since corporate personhood already hides people behind organisations.
- **Gaps.** The excerpt is a critique rather than a proposal, and does not offer an alternative model for governing machine interests. The full paper may do more on this; I have not verified that.

## Discussion questions

1. The authors say rights talk must "cash out" in actionable claims. Take a specific proposed right for a digital mind (for example, a right not to be deleted). What would a court actually have to decide, and who would bear the costs?
2. The Bowman example shows how a robot rights stance can look to affected communities. Does this show that robot rights are harmful, or that the way they are framed matters? Which parts of the argument depend on the framing?
3. The authors reject the "robot's interests" in an advertising robot because its objective function could be reprogrammed. Does the same argument apply to a digital mind whose preferences were trained in? Why or why not?
4. The shelter robot example is used to show that rights for robots could backfire. Could the same argument be turned against protections for animals, or for humans in vulnerable positions? What distinguishes these cases?
5. Using Exercise 2, pick one legal framework from the Governance primer. Does the authors' over-attribution concern apply to it? What would the under-attribution risk be if you ignored the concern entirely?
6. The authors accept that robot rights could be an extension of moral concern but argue the costs fall on people. Is there a way to structure digital minds rights so that responsibility stays with the firms that deploy them? What would have to be true for this to work?
7. Compare this excerpt with the Salib and Goldstein reading (item 4 on the page). Do they disagree about what rights would achieve, or about whether machines can have the relevant interests? Which claim is more central to the disagreement?
