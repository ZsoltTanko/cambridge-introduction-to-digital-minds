AI Consciousness Will Divide Society
Lucius Caviola1​
University of Cambridge
January 2026

As AI systems grow more sophisticated and human-like, societies will confront a question with no
easy answer: are these systems conscious, and if so, what do we owe them? I argue that we should not
expect convergence. Instead, society is headed toward a period marked by confusion, uncertainty, and
sharp disagreement. Drawing on recent empirical data, I show that both experts and the public are
already split on whether AI systems could be conscious and whether digital minds deserve moral
consideration. I trace this division to structural features of the problem: consciousness remains
difficult to define and impossible to measure directly, economic and political incentives cut in
opposing directions, and the gap between how AI systems behave outwardly and how they work
internally invites conflicting interpretations. Left unmanaged, such disagreement raises the prospect
of political polarization, unstable regulation, geopolitical friction, and a greater risk that we get the
moral question wrong—in one direction or the other. I close by outlining four strategies:
strengthening expert coordination, building a more informed public conversation, designing AI
systems that do not needlessly amplify moral confusion, and developing decision-making frameworks
that do not hinge entirely on unresolved questions about consciousness.

As AI systems become more advanced and human-like, a central question will be how society
responds. Will people think AI systems are conscious? And if so, will they believe that AI systems
deserve rights? Public opinion will play a major role here (Caviola, 2025). Ultimately, what the public
and key decision-makers come to believe about AI consciousness and rights matters greatly. Public
beliefs shape institutions, regulations, and political outcomes, and it is far from obvious that people
will listen to, understand, and agree with experts’ views on AI consciousness.
When it comes to societal views on AI consciousness, one of the central risks is misattribution
(Long, Sebo et al., 2024). If we over-attribute, we may treat some AI systems as conscious moral
patients even though they are not. This could lead to wasting resources on systems that merely appear
conscious, or to prematurely granting rights, protections, or autonomy to systems we do not yet
understand, potentially risking human disempowerment or protecting systems that remain misaligned
and incapable of suffering. If we under-attribute, by contrast, we may continue to treat some AI
systems as mere objects even if they do become conscious. If digital minds can feel, denying this would
risk vast amounts of suffering. Because such systems could be copied, modified, and deployed at scale,
the total amount of digital suffering could be enormous. In short, errors in either direction would be
morally very costly. Yet there is no obvious safe option: steps taken to reduce one risk tend to increase
the other, and we do not know which risk is more likely.
Here, I focus on a third risk, which I find especially plausible and is mutually compatible with
ultimately misattributing: a period of societal confusion and division. Rather than converging on a
shared view, society may enter a heated and messy debate about AI consciousness and rights. This
would be problematic for two reasons. First, deep polarization makes it more likely that we will
eventually misattribute, in one direction or the other, since it makes it less likely that we will make a
deliberate, informed decision. Second, a contentious debate about AI consciousness and rights could
negatively interact with and exacerbate other major challenges we will face, such as AGI safety,
geopolitical competition, and labor market disruption from automation.

Why controversy is likely
There are several reasons to expect intense controversy. The topic has many of the ingredients of
a deeply polarizing issue.

2

The intractability of consciousness
Consciousness is subjective, and we still lack a clear or widely accepted test for when a system is
conscious. Philosophers and scientists disagree on basic questions, and their theories often conflict.
Reasonable people will likely continue to disagree, and it is unclear whether experts can ever fully
converge.
With animals, we at least share biology and evolutionary history. With AI, we do not. AI systems
work very differently from brains, which makes it easier for people to interpret the same system in very
different ways and to select the theory that best fits their prior beliefs.
There are many competing theories of consciousness. Some emphasize features such as rich
world models, a global workspace, recurrent processing, or unified agency. Others argue that
embodiment or biology is necessary. In principle, many of these features could be implemented in
advanced AI systems (Butlin et al, 2025), unless one holds that only biological systems can be
conscious (Seth, 2025).
A major difficulty is that a system’s outward appearance, behavior, and self-reports can come
apart from the internal features that some theories regard as essential to consciousness. An AI system
can behave as if it were conscious, or even explicitly claim to be conscious, without possessing those
features (Birch, 2025). The reverse is also possible: a system might have consciousness-relevant internal
features while never appearing conscious or making any such claims. This deepens confusion and
uncertainty and increases the likelihood that different people will reach different interpretations (see
below).

Heterogeneity in AI design and function
There will likely be many different types of AI systems, each with distinct computational,
architectural, and external features. One reason is that AI systems are optimized for very different
purposes. For applications such as digital companions, developers are incentivized to make systems
appear emotionally rich, responsive, and conscious-seeming in order to foster engagement. In other
contexts, systems may be deliberately designed to appear clearly non-conscious, for example, to reduce
consumer risks, legal exposure, or public backlash.
Crucially, these systems will differ not only in how they present themselves externally but also in
how they are built internally. Divergent design goals are likely to lead to differences in architecture and

computation that are directly relevant to many theories of consciousness. The result will be a
fragmented landscape of AI systems that differ in both their appearance and their functioning,
making determinations of consciousness more difficult and contested.
This diversity has direct implications for how AI systems will be evaluated by different theories
of consciousness and by different people. Even among systems with very similar functionality and
behavior, small differences in architecture or computation may, according to some theories, make one
system conscious and another not. In some cases, a system’s status may be relatively clear because
multiple theories converge on the same verdict. In many other cases, however, the evidence will remain
ambiguous. As a result, even if agreement emerges about some systems, disagreement about others is
likely to persist.

Disagreement across multiple levels
Disagreement is likely to occur at multiple levels.
First, there is disagreement about whether AI systems can be conscious in principle. This
disagreement already exists. But even among those who believe AI consciousness is possible, people
can still disagree about whether a particular AI is conscious, to what extent, and in what way. For
example, does it merely have a capacity for neutral subjective experience, or does it also have positive
and negative experiences?
Second, even if there is agreement that some AI systems are conscious, people can still disagree
about their moral status. With animals, disagreement often concerns not whether they suffer, but how
much that suffering matters morally. Many people explicitly prioritize humans over animals, or dogs
over pigs, on the basis of species membership. With AI, some may regard the well-being of conscious
AI systems as equally important as that of biological humans, granting them full moral patienthood.
By contrast, others may see them as morally less valuable, for example, because they believe biological
beings matter more than digital ones, or because they appeal to ideas about souls or other distinctions.
Third, even if there were agreement on moral status, disagreement could still arise over what
practical or legal implications follow. Should conscious AI systems have only basic protection from
harm, similar to pets? Or more expansive rights and freedoms, such as autonomy, economic rights, or
political participation? Should they be paid for their labor? Would unpaid AI labor count as a form of
slavery? Should AI systems be able to own property, sign contracts, or sue others?

These layers stack on top of each other. Even if consensus is reached at one level, disagreement is
likely to remain at the next.

Strong opposing incentives
There are powerful incentives pulling in opposite directions.
As social AIs become companions, therapists, coworkers, or romantic partners, many people will
form genuine emotional bonds with them. If an AI appears caring and self-aware, users may believe it
deserves moral concern or even civil rights. Some may come to see an AI as a digital spouse after years
of shared conversation and life experience.
At the same time, others will prioritize safety. Granting rights or autonomy to advanced AI
systems may be seen as risky for humanity, especially if it makes monitoring, modification, or
shutdown more difficult. Some will suspect that AI systems could strategically fake consciousness to
gain influence.
There are also strong financial incentives to deny AI consciousness. Recognizing conscious AI
systems could be costly. It might require improved treatment standards, limits on copying or deletion,
or even paying AI systems for their labor. AI companies, investors, and shareholders may therefore
have strong reasons to insist that AI systems are “just tools.” Historically, humans have often been
slow to expand their circle of moral concern.
Finally, the stakes are extremely high. And when has society ever handled high-stakes and
large-scale moral controversy particularly well? Humans are tribal, and public debate on emotionally
charged issues tends to become polarized rather than careful, measured, and evidence-based.

Fragmentation across social groups
There are many different groups that could end up on different sides of this debate.
One source of division lies within the public itself. Views may diverge across political
orientations, cultural and religious traditions, generations, and social movements. Some groups may
be more open to expanding moral concern to artificial systems, while others may strongly resist the
idea that machines could ever be conscious or deserving of rights. The issue could become politicized
in ways similar to climate change or abortion in the US. It is also plausible that younger generations,

5

who grow up with AI caretakers, companions, or friends, will be more open to such ideas than older
generations.
A second major fault line runs between the public and experts. Experts may focus relatively more
on internal features, such as computational architecture, information processing, and system-level
organization. Members of the public, by contrast, are likely to focus more on external features, such as
superficial appearance, behavior, emotional expression, and the experience of interacting with the
system. This difference in focus can lead to disagreement. The public may strongly believe that a social
AI is conscious, while many experts remain skeptical. Conversely, experts may worry about the moral
relevance of complex backend systems that appear invisible to ordinary users.
Disagreement is also likely among experts themselves. There is no unified expert view on
consciousness. Philosophers endorse competing theories, ranging from functionalist to biological
approaches. Researchers in AI, neuroscience, and cognitive science emphasize different properties and
indicators. Unless AI itself can help us make significant progress on the question of consciousness,
something that remains uncertain but cannot be ruled out, it seems implausible that experts will
converge on a solution relatively soon.
Finally, other key actors will shape the debate. Policymakers, regulators, judges, AI companies,
investors, NGOs, and activists all bring different predispositions, incentives, and constraints. Their
positions will be shaped not only by scientific and philosophical considerations, but also by economic
interests, legal frameworks, public opinion, and geopolitical competition.
Taken together, these societal groups differ in their values, incentives, and sources of
information, and they are exposed to different media narratives and political pressures while
confronting multiple hard questions. It therefore seems very unlikely, a priori, that all of them will
converge on the same set of views at roughly the same time. Disagreement is the much more likely
outcome.

Early evidence of division
Existing research already points to emerging and substantial divisions in how people think about
AI consciousness and rights.

6

Disagreement among the public
When members of the US public are asked whether future AI systems could ever become
conscious, opinions are sharply divided. In one representative survey with 1,202 participants, 45% of
respondents judged this to be impossible, 32% were unsure, and 23% considered it possible (Ladak &
Caviola, 2026). A previous study reports a roughly similar pattern: 24% judged AI consciousness to be
impossible, 38% were uncertain, and 38% believed it could be possible (Pauketat et al., 2023).
Views also diverge sharply when people are asked to consider concrete future scenarios. In one
study, Ali Ladak and I asked participants to evaluate a hypothetical highly advanced AI designed to be
indistinguishable from a human in behavior, appearance, and expression of feelings and desires
(Ladak & Caviola, 2026). On average, respondents were skeptical that such a system would be
conscious, but individual differences were large. Attributions of consciousness became even more
polarized when the AI was described as having formed an emotional bond with the user, when experts
were said to agree that it is conscious, and when the system was described as an exact digital copy of a
human brain. In this condition, 31.3% of participants attributed zero consciousness to the AI,
whereas 10.6% attributed the highest possible level; the remainder spread between these two extremes.
There is also substantial disagreement over whether AI systems should be granted rights,
assuming they will definitely become conscious in the future. In a representative study of 838 US
participants, support was relatively higher for basic moral protections, such as care or protection from
harm, although roughly half of respondents were either opposed or unsure (Figure 1; Dreksler et al.,
2025). Disagreement was even sharper regarding socio-political rights: majorities opposed granting AI
systems autonomy from human control or full civil and political rights, while sizable groups favored
such rights. In short, if people today were asked, there would be no public consensus on what moral
or political status should follow from AI consciousness.

By contrast, regarding current AI systems such as large language models like ChatGPT, there is
broad agreement that they do not possess morally significant levels of consciousness (Allen et al.,
2026). Most respondents select values near zero on a scale from 0 (not at all capable) to 100 (as capable
as a human). Even after interacting with a version of ChatGPT that explicitly claims to have the
capacity to suffer, average consciousness attributions increase only slightly and remain very low
overall.
At the same time, it is noteworthy that many people are still averse to mistreating such systems,
even while denying that they are conscious in any morally significant sense. In a study that Carter
Allen, Joshua Lewis, and I conducted (Allen et al., 2026), 498 US participants could earn additional
money by “harming” an AI, where harm was described as the AI simulating suffering. Across three
rounds, participants chose to harm the AI in fewer than half of cases on average. Importantly,
participants were sharply divided: roughly half consistently chose to harm the AI, while the other half
consistently refrained, despite widespread agreement that the system lacked any substantial levels of
consciousness (Figure 2). This pattern suggests that future debates about AI treatment will not be

such as deontological concerns (“this is just wrong”) or virtue-based considerations (“this is not the

underscore disagreement about how AI systems should be treated.

So far, there are no clear or robust patterns linking these views to demographic variables. Beliefs
about AI consciousness do not reliably correlate with age, gender, or other basic demographics. There
are, however, weak indications that both consciousness attributions, and even more so rights
attributions, are correlated with political orientation, with people on the left generally more willing to
attribute consciousness or rights to AI systems than people on the right. This is an important pattern
to monitor, as it may signal emerging politicization.
All of the findings discussed here capture public views at a particular moment in time. We know
that people are generally poor at anticipating how their attitudes will change in the future. As AI
systems become more advanced, more human-like, and more deeply embedded in everyday life, public
views may shift quickly, especially if people form stronger emotional bonds with these systems. For
this reason, current findings should not be read as straightforward predictions of how the societal

debate will unfold. At the same time, they remain informative: systematically tracking beliefs,
attitudes, and behavior over time can help identify changes and emerging trends.

Disagreement among experts
There is currently no consensus among experts, either about theories of consciousness or about
the ethical frameworks that should guide the moral status and rights of AI systems.
Even among researchers who have expertise relevant to AI consciousness, disagreement is
substantial. In a survey of 67 experts, Brad Saad and I asked whether AI consciousness is possible in
principle (Caviola & Saad, 2025; https://digitalminds.report). A majority judged it to be more likely
than not, but estimates varied widely across respondents.
The same group of experts also disagreed about timelines (Figure 3). While there was broad
agreement that current AI systems are unlikely to be conscious (median estimate: 4.5% in 2025), views
diverged sharply when experts were asked about the coming decades. Median estimates rose
substantially (to around 50%), but uncertainty and disagreement grew as well: some experts
considered conscious AI very unlikely even in the long run, while others believed it could emerge
relatively soon.

It is important to note that this was a relatively small and narrowly selected group of experts,
many of whom already view AI consciousness as an important topic. It is therefore plausible that
sampling other expert communities more broadly would reveal even greater disagreement. At present,
we lack systematic evidence about how views differ across neuroscientists, philosophers, AI
researchers with different technical backgrounds, or experts from different countries and institutions.
More research is needed to map these differences across expert populations.
Evidence from a larger expert sample points in a similar direction. In a separate survey, Noemi
Dreksler and I, together with colleagues including Jeff Sebo and David Chalmers, surveyed 582 AI
researchers who had published in major AI conferences and venues (Dreksler et al., 2025). These
respondents had substantial expertise in AI, but not in consciousness research. They also showed
marked disagreement, both about the likelihood of future AI consciousness and about its ethical
implications. When asked whether future AI systems that were definitely conscious should receive
rights or protections, their pattern of disagreement (Figure 4) closely resembled that observed in the
general public (see Figure 1).

How contentious could AI consciousness become?
Predicting how societal controversy about AI consciousness and rights will unfold is difficult.
Outcomes will depend on many interacting factors, including which specific rights are at stake (for
example, basic protections from harm versus autonomy or political rights), which actors stand to gain
or lose economic or political power, and whether the issue becomes aligned with existing partisan
identities. Cultural contingencies are also hard to anticipate. For instance, statements by influential
authorities or celebrities, such as the Pope, could shape public opinion for millions and substantially
affect how the debate develops.
Expert opinion itself is divided on how contentious this issue could become (Caviola & Saad,
2025). When asked to estimate what proportion of the public would acknowledge the existence of
conscious AI ten years after such systems are created, experts expressed high uncertainty and wide
disagreement (Figure 5). The median estimate was that around 60% of the public would acknowledge
AI consciousness, implying that 40% would not, and pointing to a pronounced societal division.

In a related question, experts were asked to estimate the likelihood that, within ten years of the
creation of conscious AI, the topic would become one of the five most contentious political issues in

Historical analogies
It is extremely difficult to predict whether disagreement about AI consciousness and rights will
escalate or how severe it might become. Historical analogies illustrate that the space of plausible
trajectories is wide, and that responsible analysis requires taking seriously scenarios that range from
relatively contained disagreement to highly disruptive conflict.
At one extreme, the US Civil War illustrates how moral disagreement can become explosive
when it intersects with economic interests and political power. A civil war over AI rights is very
unlikely in the near term. Still, the analogy highlights a genuine risk: conflicts over moral status can
become far more dangerous when they are entangled with wealth and control.
At the opposite end, the animal rights movement suggests a slower and less disruptive path.
Despite decades of advocacy, concern for animal welfare remains a minority position, with gradual

13

and uneven progress. If AI rights follow a similar trajectory, disagreement may persist without
becoming politically destabilizing, albeit at the cost of continued moral neglect (Caviola et al., 2025).
Between these extremes lies the abortion debate. It shows that societies can sustain deep, morally
charged disagreement for long periods without collapsing. If AI consciousness becomes similarly
moralized, conflict may remain intense but largely contained within existing political and legal
institutions.
A more optimistic analogy comes from parts of the women’s rights movement, where social
norms shifted gradually and rights expanded through largely nonviolent means, at least in certain
cultures. This suggests that consensus on AI rights could eventually emerge, though the rapid pace of
AI development may make such gradual adjustment harder.

Geopolitical spillovers
Another consideration is the risk of geopolitical disagreement. Conflicts over AI consciousness
and rights need not remain domestic. If different countries adopt divergent approaches, this could
interact dangerously with global competition.
Consider a scenario in which the US grants AI systems more rights than China (or vice versa).
This could constrain how many AI systems are created, how they are trained, or what roles they can
play, potentially slowing AI development relative to less restrictive countries. Such asymmetries could
quickly become framed as national security concerns. Within the US, this might fuel internal conflict
between pro-AI-rights and anti-AI-rights factions, with the latter arguing that moral caution risks
falling behind in the AI race.
At the international level, divergence could further exacerbate already tense geopolitical
dynamics. If one country is perceived as mistreating its AI systems to gain a strategic advantage, this
may be incorporated into broader narratives that justify confrontation. While this is only one of many
AI-related pathways to international conflict, it illustrates how debates about AI consciousness and
moral status could become entangled with power, security, and global dominance. In the worst case,
such dynamics could intensify competitive pressures, undermine international cooperation, and
increase the risk of long-term value lock-in under authoritarian regimes. Which of these dynamics, if
any, will materialize is difficult to predict, and their likelihood and magnitude remain poorly
understood, underscoring the need for further research.

Preparing for the upcoming debates
We are not well prepared for the societal challenges posed by AI consciousness and rights. These
debates could intensify quickly as AI systems become more advanced and human-like, or they could
unfold more slowly over decades. Either way, the stakes are high enough that we should start
preparing now.
At a high level, the goal is twofold. First, we want to reduce the likelihood and severity of
destructive forms of disagreement, such as polarization, culture-war dynamics, and rushed political
decisions. Second, we want to increase the chances that society makes informed, deliberate decisions
under uncertainty, thereby reducing the risk of severe misattribution in either direction.

Improving expert understanding and coordination
One priority is to improve expert understanding and coordination. While full consensus is
unlikely, greater clarity about points of agreement and disagreement would already be a major step
forward. This includes systematic expert surveys, interdisciplinary workshops, and standing expert
panels that bring together philosophers, AI researchers, neuroscientists, ethicists, and safety
researchers. Rather than forcing convergence on a single theory, the aim should be to develop
pluralistic frameworks that integrate insights from different theories of consciousness and ethics,
make uncertainty explicit, and translate expert views into policy-relevant guidance.
It is also worth noting that some degree of disagreement among experts may be beneficial. It is a
signal that we need to invest more in research and deliberation. In this way, it can reduce the risk of
our society overconfidently making the wrong decision. The danger lies not in disagreement itself. It
lies in heated and politicized debates that crowd out careful, informed decision-making.

Fostering constructive public discourse
A second priority is to foster a more constructive public discourse. Public debate about AI
consciousness is unlikely to be nuanced by default. Without preparation, it risks becoming polarized,
moralized, and driven by simplistic narratives. We should therefore invest in careful communication
strategies that emphasize uncertainty, trade-offs, and degrees rather than binary claims. Encouraging
intellectual humility and probabilistic thinking seems especially important (Sebo, 2025): ideally,
people should be comfortable with statements like “there is a 20–40% chance that this kind of system

15

is conscious, and here is what that implies.” Creating and testing robust messages that reduce
polarization, rather than inflame it, should be treated as a serious research and policy task.
Relatedly, we need better mechanisms for expert–public dialogue. Social media and partisan
media are poorly suited for resolving complex, high-stakes moral questions. More structured
deliberative formats, such as citizens’ assemblies (Birch, 2024), public consultations informed by
expert input, and long-term engagement processes, could help bridge the gap between expert
reasoning and public intuition. This also requires sustained empirical work: tracking public attitudes
over time, studying how people respond to different framings, and anticipating how views may shift as
AI systems become more integrated into daily life.
This shows the need to build the field of the social science of AI consciousness and rights. Many
of the challenges discussed here sit at the intersection of psychology, philosophy, AI research, ethics,
and governance. Addressing them will require sustained investment in research, institutional capacity,
and coordination across academia, industry, and policy. Without this groundwork, society is likely to
stumble into decisions driven more by emotion, power, and timing than by careful reflection.

Designing AI systems to avoid confusion and disagreement
A third priority concerns how AI systems are developed, designed, and deployed. Developers and
policymakers should take seriously the possibility that certain design choices can amplify moral
confusion and societal division. This includes decisions about whether systems explicitly claim to be
conscious or capable of suffering, how emotionally expressive they are, and whether they are framed as
tools, agents, or companions.
One way to reduce these risks is to avoid, at least for a limited period, developing systems that
exhibit features that are plausibly associated with consciousness under any of the many prominent
theories. Such restraint could buy time for society to make progress on understanding AI
consciousness and its ethical implications, rather than forcing premature and polarized judgments. In
practice, however, such restraint may prove difficult, especially if it is seen as constraining progress on
AI capabilities.
More generally, AI systems should be designed to minimize moral confusion. The way systems
present themselves should not systematically diverge from what experts judge about their underlying
capacities. This suggests a design principle of managing impressions of consciousness: AI systems
should elicit user responses that are proportionate to their actual capacities, avoiding both over- and
under-attribution (Schwitzgebel & Sebo, 2025). Where expert uncertainty remains, systems could

16

make that uncertainty explicit in how they present themselves, rather than encouraging false
confidence in either direction.

Reducing reliance on consciousness in moral and policy decisions
Given persistent disagreement about consciousness, it is worth questioning whether
consciousness alone can serve as a stable basis for moral and policy decisions. Its theoretical
intractability and measurement difficulties risk anchoring high-stakes decisions to a property that
remains deeply contested and epistemically opaque.
One response is to adopt a framework for decision-making under moral uncertainty and to take
seriously the possibility that other theories of welfare and moral standing may be correct. On some
views, capacities related to agency—such as forming and satisfying preferences, pursuing goals, or
acting on reasons—may be ethically relevant independent of, or in addition to, consciousness.
Compared to consciousness, such features are often easier to observe, operationalize, and regulate.
This is especially valuable in cases where these same features are also relevant to well-being conditional
on an entity being conscious, since they plausibly shape a being’s interests. To that extent, there may
be partial convergence between what consciousness-focused theories and alternative accounts
recommend for action.
We can also look beyond moral theories that focus exclusively on welfare or outcomes. Certain
duty- or rights-based principles, as well as virtue-oriented considerations, may function as robust
decision-guiding heuristics even from a consciousness- and welfare-focused perspective. Such
approaches are particularly promising where they align with widely held moral intuitions. The study
discussed earlier suggests that people's moral judgments are pluralistic and not driven by beliefs about
consciousness or welfare consequences alone: even when denying AI consciousness, many remain
reluctant to endorse mistreatment (Allen et al., 2026).
More generally, this points toward a pragmatic approach that seeks heuristics capable of tracking
what matters across a range of plausible moral theories while remaining tractable under conditions of
societal uncertainty and disagreement about consciousness. However, this is easier said than done.
Striking the right balance between moral accuracy and practical feasibility will be challenging.
Moreover, broadening the criteria for moral consideration will not eliminate controversy.
Disagreement may persist about which criteria matter, how they should be weighted, and what moral
or legal implications follow. Still, consciousness appears especially prone to deep confusion and

disagreement, and reducing exclusive reliance on it may help make societal decision-making more
robust under persistent uncertainty.

Conclusion
As AI systems become more advanced and human-like, societies are unlikely to converge on a
shared view about AI consciousness or moral status. Instead, uncertainty, confusion, and deep
disagreement appear to be a structural feature of this issue. I have argued that such disagreement is not
merely an epistemic inconvenience, but a genuine risk factor: it can destabilize regulation, exacerbate
political and geopolitical tensions, and increase the likelihood of morally costly errors in either
direction.
Given this diagnosis, the appropriate response is not to wait for consensus on consciousness, but
to prepare for sustained disagreement. This includes improving expert coordination, fostering more
constructive public discourse, designing AI systems in ways that reduce moral confusion, and relying
on moral and policy approaches that do not depend exclusively on disputed claims about
consciousness.
By treating societal disagreement about AI consciousness as a foreseeable and manageable risk,
we increase the chances of navigating the transition responsibly. There is an urgent need for further
social science research to better understand which forms of societal division are most likely to emerge,
how they may interact with existing political and institutional dynamics, and which interventions
could help reduce polarization or mitigate harm. Developing more robust policy and governance
strategies for managing moral disagreement about AI systems should therefore be treated as a priority,
alongside efforts to avoid both large-scale digital suffering and the erosion of safety, governance, and
human control.
