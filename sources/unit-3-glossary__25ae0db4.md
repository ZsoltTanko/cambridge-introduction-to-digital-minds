Unit 3 Glossary
The below terms may be useful in Unit 3: 


AI Interpretability: a field of study aimed at understanding the internal workings of AI models. There are different approaches to this – e.g. mechanistic interpretability seeks to understand causal structures, algorithms or circuits in model weights, where other methods look for higher level patterns and correlations e.g. attention visualisations. These are relatively young disciplines, and critics worry that models may be too complex for the simplifications they require. For more on mechanistic interpretability, see Anthropic’s work, or Dan Hendrycks’ and Laura Hiscott’s critique or Neel Nanda’s podcast. 

Gaming Problem (The): the idea that tests for consciousness in AI are unreliable because AI systems are given the tools to ‘game’ them. As Jonathan Birch and Kristin Andrews point out, LLMs are trained on huge amounts of human data (including data about consciousness and experience), often with the aim of appearing human-like. As Birch later clarified, this can apply not just to self-report but also to behavioural tests for consciousness (e.g. making trade-offs under simulated conditions of pain). While this is more commonly associated with the risk that we overattribute consciousness to LLMs, it’s worth noting that this may apply to underattribution if not claiming consciousness is incentivised.

Interpretivism: the view that we can reliably infer mental states when reasoning from observed behaviour. Different strands of interpretivism have different emphases, but one states that we can have confidence our interpretation is correct when it 1) makes sense of behaviour and 2) helps to predict further behaviour. Most philosophers hold, however, that such interpretability alone is not sufficient for genuine mental states. 


Test Validity: the extent to which a test accurately measures the phenomenon it is designed to measure. A common way of operationalising this is in terms of false positives (the test reads positive but the phenomenon is absent) and false negatives (the test reads negative but the phenomenon is present): a test is considered valid when these error rates are known and within accepted bounds. As regards tests for consciousness, Bayne et al (2024) distinguish between various distinct types of validity: 
* Construct validity = whether the test measures the phenomenon of interest; in this case, phenomenal consciousness. A related concept is discriminant validity – whether the test probes only consciousness or also other (potentially related) phenomena e.g. reportability.
* Content validity = does the test accurately probe for all relevant aspects of the target phenomenon. In consciousness tests, this applies if the test is intended to assess the potential contents of consciousness – not just whether there is ‘something it is like’ to be an entity, but what it is like as well. 
* Criterion validity = does the test have predictive value, i.e. if a human in a minimally conscious state tests positive for consciousness and then fully recovers, that would support the test’s validity. This is hard for AI systems: we don’t know what a positive consciousness test might predict.
* Face validity = do the results of the tests echo – or radically differ from – our existing assessments of consciousness.
