﻿Introduction to Digital Minds, April 2026
  

Glossary
For the purposes of this course, we will use the following definition of terms: 


A


AI Alignment: making AI systems do what we intend them to do (also referred to as intent alignment). An AI system is considered to be aligned if it advances its intended objectives. An AI system is misaligned if it pursues objectives that are not intended and at odds with its intended objectives. AI Alignment also refers to the goal of designing artificial intelligence systems in such a way that their objectives and behavior are aligned with the values and goals of human users or society at large. 


AI Ethics: a field that works to reduce current and near-term risks to humans associated with AI. These include harmful content, privacy violations, mental health impacts, and biased models already leading to discrimination and real-world harms. Note: the boundaries between AI Safety and AI Ethics are blurred, with areas of interaction and support and an overall shared objective of making AI safe for humans. At the same time, there are areas of tension, different emphases on particular risks are correlated with different groups.
AI Interpretability: a field of study aimed at understanding the internal workings of AI models. There are different approaches to this - e.g. mechanistic interpretability seeks to understand causal structures, algorithms or circuits in model weights, where other methods look for higher level patterns and correlations e.g. attention visualisations. Both are relatively young disciplines, and critics worry that models may be too complex for the simplifications they require. For more information on general interpretability, see Christoph Molnar’s book. For more on mechanistic interpretability, see Anthropic’s work, or Dan Hendrycks’ and Laura Hiscott’s critique or Neel Nanda’s podcast. 
AI Psychosis: Mania-like episodes, delusional thinking or paranoia that can emerge or worsen through AI chatbot use. This is a relatively new phenomenon, with some dispute as to whether it is a media label or a relevant medical category. Still, other research appears to link it with sycophancy in AI models, and users' perceptions of sentience or deification of the chatbot as a supreme or cosmic intelligence. Such experiences can culminate in grandiose delusions (feeling chosen or messianic), paranoid claims (being spied on or targeted) and - tragically - instances of violence towards self and others. See Microsoft’s definition here or a 2025 academic study here.


AI Safety: a broadly technical field that considers whether and how AI development can be made safe for humans, particularly in terms of avoiding catastrophic (or even existential) harms. Actors within this field have different emphases. Some worries include superintelligent models aiding the concentration of power and the lock-in of a bad governing system; models pursuing unintended objectives (without us being able to stop them) or being misused by bad actors in ways that exacerbate risks from - or create impacts similar to - chemical, biological and nuclear weapons. Note: the boundaries between AI Safety and AI Ethics are blurred, with areas of interaction and support and an overall shared objective making AI go well for humans. At the same time, different emphases on particular risks are correlated with different groups.


AI Welfare: the potential wellbeing of AI systems; or the field of AI welfare, which studies whether AI systems can have interests, experiences, or preferences that matter morally— and if so, how (and whether) we ought to protect or promote them. Distinct but related to the field of AI safety, which considers whether and how AI development can be safe for humans.


Agency, Robust: ‘some sophisticated capacity or capacities to set and pursue goals via mental states that function like belief, desires and intentions’ (Long et al, 2024). The “robust” qualifier describes whether a system has the right kind of representational structures. For example, a reinforcement-learning agent may exhibit goal-directed behaviour without the representational structure that robust agency requires. This is distinct from commercial ‘AI agents’, though some such agents may have properties that could qualify as robust agency. An entity can be robustly agentic without being conscious (and vice versa). Under certain theories, robust agency can qualify an entity for moral patienthood.


Anthropomorphism: When humans attribute human characteristics, emotions, or mental states to non-human entities. In the context of digital minds research, anthropomorphism is of particular interest as a potential source of cognitive bias: a tendency to perceive or report consciousness, sentience, or moral status in AI systems based on superficial behavioural or linguistic cues, that may have been engineered as part of the product design e.g. the choice to make a chat interface with a name.


Anthropodenial: When humans reflexively deny or dismiss human-like characteristics, emotions, or mental states in non-human entities. In the context of digital minds research, some argue that scepticism about AI consciousness or moral status may reflect motivated reasoning or cultural assumptions about the uniqueness of human minds.


Artificial General Intelligence (AGI): An AI system that matches or exceeds human cognitive abilities across virtually all domains - able to reason, learn, transfer skills between tasks, and solve novel problems without task-specific programming. The way that this term is used or defined changes with time and by group - with some disputing whether it remains useful at all.  For example, OpenAI describes it as “a highly autonomous system that outperforms humans at most economically valuable work”, while DeepMind has recently published work on defining different levels and a group of prominent researchers recently suggested AGI to be systems that match ‘the cognitive versatility and proficiency of a well-educated adult’.




B
Behaviorism: a family of approaches that grounds study of psychology in observed behaviour. There are different levels to this. The least controversial seeks behavioral evidence for psychological phenomena. The most extreme, however, holds that psychology is the science of behavior (rather than of subjective experience) and that 1) behavior can - and should - be explained without reference to inner states. 




C


Conscious/consciousness: If something is conscious, there is ‘something it is like’ to be that thing (Nagel, 1974). By default, we use ‘conscious’ to refer to phenomenal consciousness, or subjective experience. We are not talking about intelligence or sense of self or other related concepts - we are focussing on whether something has the capacity for experience. So when it ‘feels like’ something to be a conscious system, there is a conscious experience happening – whereas it doesn’t feel like anything to be an unconscious system, and there is no conscious experience happening. Put another way, ‘consciousness’ is the thing that goes away when we go into deep, dreamless sleep, though there is some debate around whether consciousness is an on/off phenomenon or can have more borderline cases. Importantly, conscious experience need not involve emotional content or even a sense of self; any kind of conscious experience will do. 


Consciousness - access consciousness: states or contents of mind that are available for report and reasoning, but that are not necessarily experienced subjectively. This contrasts with phenomenal consciousness (the ‘what-it’s-like’ feel), though there is debate around how they may interact. In access consciousness, availability for report/reasoning/control is a hallmark; mere influence on behavior is not enough. For more on these distinctions, see Block here. 


Conscious AI, seemingly: “seemingly conscious AI” (SCAI) are AI systems that imitate consciousness convincingly enough that humans believe them to be conscious; or AI systems with features that encourage users to attribute consciousness to them. These features may include, for example, anthropomimetic features like voices, faces or particularly human-like socio-relational skills. 


Consequentialism: is a family of ethical theories on which whether an action is right is  determined by its consequences.




D
Deontology: a family of ethical theories. Often defined in opposition to consequentialism, in that - where consequentialism focuses on the outcome - deontology focuses on whether an action itself is in keeping with moral norms. Under deontology, moral duties and rights constrain what may be done in pursuit of good outcomes. Associated with Kant's (1785) principle that rational agents must be treated as ends in themselves, never merely as means. Critiques include that excessive adherence to duties can lead to bad outcomes in edge cases, and that there are not clear rationales for the different permissions and constraints suggested.
Digital Minds: A field concerned with computer systems’ potential mental states, the possibility of computer systems mattering morally, and related issues. ‘Digital minds’ is also used for the kinds of computer systems that the field studies. There is some variation in exactly how ‘digital minds’ is defined within the field. Standard uses include: computer systems with a capacity for consciousness, computer systems that matter morally for their own sake, and computer systems that merit moral consideration, owing to their potential for morally significant mental states. ‘Digital’ is often used loosely, with the field considering potential analogue or other non-digital computing systems alongside AI and other digital systems. 



F

Foundation models: AI models that are pre-trained on large amounts of data, allowing them to do (or be adapted to do) a range of tasks. LLMs are a prominent class of foundation models.

Functionalism: Functionalism holds that consciousness turns on functional organisation. The functional organisation of a system i.e. the relationships between its possible internal states and its inputs and outputs, determines whether it is conscious. This means – roughly – that consciousness depends on what a system does, not on what it is made out of (Putnam, 1975). Applying this, systems with the same functional organisation have the same experiences even if they have different substrates.


Functionalism, computational: Computational functionalism is the claim that the kind of functional organisation that matters for mind in general, and for consciousness in particular, is computational in nature (Putnam, 1988; Shagir, 2010). More specifically, that consciousness depends only on the manipulation of information by an algorithm, regardless of whether the system performing these computations is made up of neurons, silicon, or any other physical substrate. There is some debate as to what kind of computation is involved, as Anil Seth explains. 




G


Gaming problem (the): the idea that tests for consciousness in AI are unreliable because AI systems are given the tools to ‘game’ them. As Jonathan Birch and Kristin Andrews point out, LLMs are trained on huge amounts of human data (including data about consciousness and experience), often with the aim of appearing human-like. As Birch later clarified, this can apply not just to self-report but also to behavioural tests for consciousness (e.g. making trade-offs under simulated conditions of pain). While this is more commonly associated with the risk that we overattribute consciousness to LLMs, it’s worth noting that this may apply to underattribution also if not claiming consciousness is incentivised.


Governance, Digital Minds: policies, regulations, and institutional frameworks designed to manage the risks and ethical questions arising from AI systems that are perceived as - or may actually be - conscious or morally significant. It crosses over with broader AI governance, and encompasses questions about how societies should respond to uncertainty about AI moral status.




I


Intelligence: the “ability to achieve goals in a wide range of environments” (Legg & Hutter, 2007). While this definition is often used, it is controversial. For example, as Ned Block has pointed out, a giant look-up table would be considered intelligent under this definition - but not under our intuitive understanding of intelligence, which places internal constraints on how cognitive processes are used to solve problems. Others have pointed out that the abilities to choose and amend goals and to efficiently acquire skills may be required, or that intelligence itself is a resource (not an entity), multidimensional rather than scalar and poorly defined. Either way, intelligence is distinct from, and need not require, consciousness - for more on the differences see Anil Seth’s 2025 talk).


Interpretivism: The view that we can reliably infer mental states, when reasoning from observed behaviour. Different strands of interpretivism have different emphases, but one states that we can have confidence our interpretation is correct when it 1) makes sense of behaviour and 2) helps to predict further behaviour. Most philosophers hold, however, that such interpretability alone is not sufficient for genuine mental states. 


L
Large Language Models: The most prominent class of foundation models, including GPT, Gemini, Grok and Claude. Trained with a transformer architecture, these models infer statistical patterns across large amounts of training data in order to predict the next token in a sequence. See: this demonstration. After this pre-training step, LLMs typically go through additional post-training stages.  This often involves reinforcement learning with human or AI feedback (RLHF or RLAIF), in which humans or AIs give models a reward signal to try to ensure that the models behave broadly in ways that humans/the AI company like - such as helpful, honest assistants or fluent conversation partners. The resulting models remain poorly understood, hold security and safety challenges and develop capabilities that are not directly trained. In some cases and under some theories, these may be relevant to consciousness or other morally relevant states (e.g. introspective abilities, theory of mind, potential pleasure/pain trade-offs in behaviour). But - as we will explore in subsequent weeks - this is disputed.
Today’s LLMs are often multimodal, in that they are trained on and can complete tasks involving multiple forms of data (e.g. audio and images, as well as language). They are also increasingly deployed with agentic harnesses and scaffolding - software frameworks that provide access to tools, memory and external data sources. This is the basis for AI agents like OpenClaw. LLMs also underlie chatbots and AI companions such as Replika and Character.AI. These use cases have driven much of the conversation around AI consciousness.


If you’re not familiar with the core principles of how LLMs work, watch this 8 minute video for an easy to understand explainer / refresher. 


M


Moral agent: an entity that can bear moral duties and responsibility. For example, humans are normally considered moral agents, while non-human animals are not. Importantly, moral agency and moral patiency can come apart. A human baby is a moral patient but not (yet) a moral agent, and one can imagine an AI system that is a moral agent without being a moral patient (without experiences that matter for their own sake). 

Moral Circle: The set of entities a person or society considers to be moral patients (Singer, 1981, Sebo, 2025). Historically the moral circle has expanded to include people previously excluded, and more recently, to include non-human animals. 

Moral patient: an entity that matters for its own sake. For example, we do not consider rocks to be moral patients, and therefore we move / break rocks without consideration for them. Humans (and many non-human animals), however, are moral patients and therefore deserve certain consideration, rights and protections. 


Moral Standing: whether or not an entity merits ethical concern or consideration. 

Moral Status: the degree to which an entity merits ethical concern or consideration. Many ethical traditions hold that some entities have more moral status than others (e.g. humans more than mice) though others, notably animal-liberation positions descending from Singer (1975), argue for equal consideration of comparable interests across all species. 



N


Neuromorphic computing: Computer hardware that is designed to mimic the functioning of neurons. This comes in many different forms - some combining digital and analog information processing, others emulating the  spike mechanisms present in organic brains. Under some theories of consciousness, the greater similarity to neurons makes neuromorphic computing much more relevant to consciousness than traditional computer chips. Most neuromorphic computing deployments are still at pilot or early commercial stages, with challenges ahead in scaling chip production and developing software tooling. Still, the promise of major efficiency gains over traditional chips may grow interest and investment in the field.




P

Population Ethics: A branch of ethics concerned with evaluating welfare outcomes that involve different numbers of people or sentient beings, present and future. (Parfit, 1984; Greaves, 2017). A central question is whether and how to avoid the repugnant conclusion: the claim that for any flourishing population, there must be some much larger population whose existence would be better in terms of total welfare, even though its members' lives are only barely worth living. Most find this counterintuitive.


Personhood, Legal. Key part of Western law, allowing a person, corporation or other entity to engage in the legal system as the subject of specific rights and duties. So, giving something legal rights and duties means making it a legal person. This concept has been in use since Roman times, with a wide variety of entities covered from ships to corporations to rivers. While humans are naturally considered legal persons with a greater range of rights, purely legal persons have different standings - for example, corporations can own property but cannot marry. See this historical analysis of legal personhood applied to AI for more discussion. 

Personhood, Moral. Equivalent moral standing to a human. There is some discussion as to what properties lead to this level of standing. For more detail, see the SEP here.


Precautionary Principle: the principle that when there is a threat of significant harm, scientific uncertainty should not be used as a reason to delay or refrain from undertaking proportionate measures to protect against that harm. Various formulations of this principle exist, and may make different recommendations for actions at different levels of uncertainty. One relevant approach is the EU’s, which recommends additional measures (non-discrimination, cost-benefit analysis, consistency and an attempt to build more scientific understanding) for policies premised on precautionary thinking.



Q


Qualia: the introspectively accessible, phenomenal aspects of mental states - the “what-it’s-like” character of experience (eg. the redness of red, the sting of pain). In standard usage, qualia name the felt qualities of perception and sensation - not their functional or representational roles. For more, see the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy here. 




R


Rights, Negative/Positive: rights are entitlements (not) to perform certain actions, or (not) to be in certain states; or entitlements that others (not) perform certain actions or (not) be in certain states. For example, negative rights might include duties of non-interference such as rights not to be harmed or destroyed, and not to be deceived or manipulated. On the other hand, positive rights (duties of provision) might include the right to maintenance, continued existence, access to information, or the conditions for flourishing. 




S


Sentience: valenced subjective experience.  If something is sentient, there is ‘something it is like’ to be that thing (Nagel, 1974), plus these experiences feel good or bad e.g. pleasure or pain (Thompson, 2022). On many views, sentience suffices for moral standing.


Substrate independence: The claim that the same kind of mental states (including conscious states) can occur in systems with a wide range of physical substrates (Seth, 2025). This idea can also be referred to as ‘substrate flexibility’ as a reference to the idea that (conscious) mental states are not (necessarily) tied to carbon-based biological substrates (Kirkpatrick, 2022). 


Suffering-risks: risks of astronomical (future) suffering or “s-risks” for short. For more discussion, see the Center for Reducing Suffering here. 


Superintelligence, artificial (ASI). Superintelligence refers to AI systems that greatly exceed human cognitive abilities across all domains of knowledge and reasoning. This concept (and its implications) is explored in Bostrom’s book of the same title.




U


Uncertainty, descriptive: uncertainty about matters of fact, when we don’t know what is true of the world. This may be contingent (resolvable in principle with better evidence or theory) or intractable (resistant to resolution due to fundamental limits on what can be known). In the AI consciousness context, both forms apply: we lack adequate theories and tests, and may face the deeper problem that subjective experience is not straightforwardly accessible to third-person scientific methods.

Uncertainty, moral: when we don’t know what is ethically right, or which ethical values, principles, or frameworks should guide action. It can arise even when the relevant non-moral facts are known.
Utilitarianism: a classic consequentialist theory, holding that the right action maximises overall welfare — typically the sum of pleasure minus suffering across all affected beings (Bentham, 1789; Mill, 1863). Utilitarianism comes in many varieties, but the core commitment is that what makes an action right is its contribution to overall welfare. Critiques include that it errs in ignoring distinctions between persons, and that it can justify serious harms to individuals when aggregate outcomes appear mathematically favourable .

W


Welfare subject: an entity that can accrue welfare goods (things that are good for the subject, matter intrinsically to them or raise their level of wellbeing) or welfare bads (things that are bad for the subject or lower their level of wellbeing). For more detail, see Bradford 2022. 


Whole brain emulation (WBE): Replicating all the main functions of a brain in a computer system. Related to the idea of ‘mind uploading’ or continuing conscious experience digitally. Recent efforts have focussed on mapping and replicating how the neurons of brains are connected (‘connectomes’). This is a hard challenge: as of 2026, we have mapped the connectomes of C. Elegans and the Drosophila fruit fly, and made progress towards functional simulations. Doing this in humans needs better, less dangerous techniques to map and handle the complexity of 86 billion human neurons. There are then further challenges related to replicating subtler details of how brains work, e.g. neuromodulation, actions by glial cells. Even so, some believe recent advances in AI may make this more feasible, and Eon’s 2026 behavioural emulation of fruit fly brain and behaviour also sparked interest in the field (albeit with some controversy around what the achievement). For more information on WBE generally, see this article or the Foresight Institute’s lecture series here. 





T


Transformative AI (TAI): AI that precipitates a transition comparable to (or more significant than) the agricultural or industrial revolution. Unlike the related terms Superintelligence and Artificial General Intelligence, which refer to specific capabilities the AI may have, Transformative AI refers to the effects the AI would have on humanity's well-being; through impacting the global economy, state power, international security, etc.




V

Virtue ethics: An ethical theory focussed on character rather than rules or outcomes — asking what a person of good character would do, and what virtues we would like to cultivate  (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics). Critiques include that it can provide limited guidance in novel or uncertain situations.

Z


Zombie, philosophical: a philosophical thought experiment - a being that behaves exactly like a human, but lacks subjective experience.
