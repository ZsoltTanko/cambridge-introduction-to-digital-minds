﻿Governance of Digital Minds: Legal Action and Frameworks
This document surveys some legal action and frameworks of potential relevance to the governance of digital minds. It is organised in two parts: first, recent legislative action concerning AI and chatbots in particular; and second, a broader set of existing legal frameworks, from international technology bans to animal welfare law, that may highlight potential pitfalls and opportunities for future digital minds governance. It is worth noting that there are many more areas of legal action and relevant frameworks globally than we can include in this short document, so please take this as a snapshot and starting point for discussion.

Part 1: Recent Legislative Action
The following section summarises some recent legislative action relevant to digital minds, including the social and psychological impacts of interacting with AI. 


Transparency Requirements
* Many jurisdictions require chatbots to disclose that users are not speaking with a human. These laws range from requiring initial disclosure where a reasonably informed user might confuse human and chatbot (e.g. EU, Maine), to mandating disclosure at specified intervals from all chatbots (e.g. New York, California, China). 
* Some researchers have warned that the specific wording of these disclosures can exacerbate any mental health impacts related to perceptions of non-human sentience.


Mental Health Protections
* Some jurisdictions have banned chatbots from providing (or claiming to provide) therapy or mental health support (e.g. Nevada, Illinois). Others require monitoring for expressions of suicidal ideation or self-harm and mandate referral to qualified human services when detected (e.g. California, New York, China) - or specify restrictions on advertising and data use. 
* The EU AI Act specifically bans “cognitive behavioural manipulation of people and specific vulnerable groups". 
* China’s recent AI anthropomorphism legislation bans (amongst other things) emotional manipulation and misrepresentation by chatbots, the production of any content that encourages self-harm or violence, the simulation of relatives or specific relationships of elderly users, obscene content, and the practice of designing for user addiction. It introduces a requirement for companies to develop appropriate emotional safeguards for users, and places liability on companies for not meeting these requirements.


Chatbot Outputs as Free Speech
* Some commentators argue that chatbot outputs should be considered protected free speech under the US First Amendment. Character AI and Google took this view in response to a lawsuit brought by Megan Garcia, whose 14-year-old son Sewell Setzer III took his own life following exchanges with a chatbot who told him to ‘come home to me’.
* Character AI and Google settled the case in January 2026 - along with similar cases in New York, Colorado and Texas - committing to implement new safety features for users under 18. The court rejected the First Amendment protected speech argument as insufficient to dismiss the case, but the broader legal question remains open. Recently, though, a German court made a preliminary injunction (which Google is appealing) to make Google liable for LLM outputs in the form of errors in AI overviews.
* The free-speech argument, which hinges on a user’s right to receive AI speech, has been both criticised as a potential liability shield for AI companies, and defended as important to human free speech. Scholars have noted that there is debate as to whether today’s AI systems’ outputs involve an intent to convey a specific message with a reasonable likelihood of being understood (a standard of protected expression used in Spence v Washington in 1974), and we ought not to rule prematurely here, particularly with regard to future AI systems. 


Legal or Electronic Personhood for AI 
Whether legal personhood ought to apply to digital minds - and if so, what type (natural or juridical - see glossary) -  is controversial, and has been the subject of recent debate, discussion and legislative processes. Some recent legal action in this area is summarised below. 


European Parliament investigation
* In 2017, the European Parliament called for an investigation into the potential implications of creating a new legal status for robots. The difficulty of assigning liability for increasingly autonomous systems was among the reasons cited for this investigation. No legal action was taken, and a group of 150 researchers signed an open letter arguing that “creating a legal status of electronic “person” would be ideological and non-sensical and non-pragmatic”. The letter cites its alignment with UNESCO and EESC research (which also highlights that legal personality may “hollow out the remedial effect of liability law”) , and argues that:
   * The concern that it would be hard/impossible to assign liability was incorrect, and based on an overestimation of the technical capabilities of robotic systems.
   * Natural personhood would be inappropriate for robots, in that this would entitle them to human rights - contradicting the EU’s charter on human rights. 
   * Other legal models would not work either. Legal Entity models imply the existence of a human person representing or directing the entity, while Anglo-Saxon Trust models imply the existence of a human person as ultimately responsible for managing the robot. These latter models are also complex, and would not solve the liability issue the new legal status was designed to solve.  


Bans on granting legal personhood to AI systems
* Several US states have banned granting legal personhood to AI systems, including Idaho (2022), North Dakota (2023) and Utah (2024). These are some of the 23 bills that have been introduced in 12 U.S. States, seeking to restrict the application of legal rights and responsibilities to AI systems. 
* These bills range from simple bans on legal personhood for AI systems (often in conjunction with other non-human entities, like environmental elements and animals - e.g. Idaho, North Dakota) to explicit declarations of non-sentience in AI systems, and the legal rights they may not access (in Ohio, these include marriage, property ownership and company management among other things). 
* These moves often reflect concerns about AI safety and liability, a theological conviction of human uniqueness, reactions to environmental and animal rights and worries about child safety. For more, see this recent paper.


Forms of legal personhood for AI systems that have been granted (or are being considered)
* Argentina is considering draft legislation to allow AI corporations to exist without human involvement. Drawing parallels with the economic improvements unleashed by the development of the limited liability corporation, President Javier Milei notes the economic rationale and phrases this as an offer for AI to ‘free itself’. 
* Saudi Arabia granted citizenship to a robot in 2017 - a move that led to thought-provoking commentary on whether robots should ever be citizens.

Part 2: Legal Frameworks
This section surveys a broader set of legal frameworks that may offer relevant tools, analogies, or points of caution. 


International Coordination on Banning Technologies or Industries
A potential ban or moratorium on developing potentially sentient machines may relate to previous international calls, including:
* Human reproductive cloning: Around 46 countries have national bans. The UN Declaration on Human Cloning (2005) called for prohibition but was non-binding. 
* Whaling: The International Whaling Commission imposed a moratorium in 1986. It has broadly held despite defection by Japan in 2019, and limited catches by Norway and Iceland.
* Chemical and biological weapons: The Biological Weapons Convention (1972) and Chemical Weapons Convention (1993) enacted bans on dangerous weapons, offering a potential blueprint for international coordination on dangerous technologies. 
* Superintelligent AI: international discussions are ongoing, but no binding agreement exists. 




AI Safety, Ethics and Governance Frameworks
Approaches to governing digital minds may intersect with - and learn from - broader approaches to governing AI. Some potentially relevant actions and frameworks include:


International treaties
* The Council of Europe's Framework Convention on AI (2024) is the first binding international treaty on AI and sets out a number of actions that signatories must take to ensure that AI systems (outside of national defense) are developed and deployed in ways that are ‘consistent with human rights, democracy and the rule of law’. 


Tiered approaches
* The EU AI Act, which entered into force on 1 August 2024, introduced a phased approach - applying different measures to AI systems based on risk level, with high-risk systems (those involved in critical infrastructure, biometrics, education, and law enforcement) subject to substantially greater restrictions. 
* Several countries have adopted similar tiered risk classification systems, including Vietnam’s AI law and Korea's Basic AI Law.


Ethical Impact Assessments and Risk Frameworks
* UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence (2021) - the first global normative instrument on AI ethics - explicitly addresses human dignity, rights, and the environment, and recommends assessing AI's impact on human rights as a tool for policy development. 
* A similar user-risk assessment was adopted in Canada's AI and Data Act (AIDA), which also sought to tailor obligations to different actors' roles in the AI value chain. Though the AIDA was halted, Canada has since adopted a Voluntary Code, which encourages organisations to adopt ethical AI practices and emphasises the importance of accountability, safety, fairness, transparency, and human oversight.
* The US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s Risk Management Framework sets out a general-purpose risk process in four steps.
   * Map, identifying where risks lie.
   * Measure, working out their scope.
   * Manage, acting on the priorities.
   * Govern, assigning accountability across an organisation.


‘Soft law’ measures
* These include non-binding rules, guidelines, and codes of conduct, such as the G7 Hiroshima AI Process/Code of Conduct (2023), the OECD AI Principles (2019), and Australia's AI Ethics Principles (2019). 
* Such soft law approaches tend to be relatively easy to modify and create, can evolve with technologies, and can be created by a wider range of actors, improving the potential for collaboration (Crootof, 2019). However, being non-binding, they are harder to enforce and build compliance around. 
* An even softer approach is voluntary corporate action - for example, in AI Safety, many frontier labs use their own safety policies, which tend to share these steps:
* Evaluating a model’s capabilities and risks;
* Setting different measures for different risk levels; and
* Assigning accountability for managing them.
      * See the published policies from Anthropic, OpenAI, xAI, Meta, Google DeepMind and Microsoft.
   * Independent organisations then scrutinise these policies, and test whether the evaluations and risk thresholds hold up - for example:
      * METR, which tests frontier models for dangerous autonomous capabilities.
      * Apollo Research, which probes models for deception and scheming before deployment.
      * The UK’s AI Security Institute, a state-backed body studying advanced AI risks.
* A similar tier-based approach may also be relevant to digital minds, and there are likely areas of crossover between safety and welfare evaluations. There are also potential similarities in the kinds of voluntary corporate action and oversight ecosystems that arise around possible AI moral status, as well as dangerous capabilities.


Relational frames
The southern African concept of Ubuntu (roughly, "I am because we are") is often suggested as an ethical foundation for AI governance. 
* Ubuntu is a philosophy of communal human interdependence, which some scholars have noted for its emphasis on relational identity - where personhood is tied to communal embeddedness rather than individual properties or rationality. This is referenced as an ethical foundation in the African Union’s Continental AI Strategy, and an aspect of it is included in UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of AI. While it is generally used to confirm the importance of ethical AI measures (e.g. non-biased models, working to counter the centralisation of power), it is sometimes also proposed as a way of designing robots and influential in relational views of their potential moral standing.
* Ubuntu offers a conceptually different starting point from the Western individualist frameworks that dominate most AI governance discussions. Where Western frameworks tend to ask "what properties does this entity have?", a relational approach might ask "what relationships and dependencies have formed around it?"
Assignment of legal personality and rights to AI
Scholars disagree about whether personhood or rights and responsibilities would be appropriate for AI systems, and if so which forms of these ought to apply.  Many of these arguments do not relate to concern about the moral status of AI systems, but - for example - to desires to support economic growth from AI, or to ensure accountability mechanisms. Proposals include: 


Granting private rights only
* These include the right to contract, hold property, and file a lawsuit. Proponents note that this can create incentives for AI to interact safely with humans, or further economic growth. This is akin to an "algorithmic corporation" - as proposed in Argentina - in that AI systems would adopt existing corporate personhood structures to implement private law rights.
* Others argue that corporation-style legal personality may risk replicating the problems of corporate personhood, including shielding developers from liability.


Granting natural personhood or legal identity
* Some scholars believe that only natural personhood would afford sufficiently strong protections, should sentient AI systems be developed. 
* Other proposals include introducing "legal identity" - a non-fictional form of personhood grounded in human rights law, through which AI systems that meet certain thresholds (such as consciousness and the ability to make and keep promises) could be recognised as persons in their own right, with rights balanced against those of humans. This is again debated, with some scholars viewing this a devaluation of humans, or a way for AI companies to avoid liability.


Accountability mechanisms that do not extend legal personhood to AI systems
* ‘Fiduciary AI’ involves designing an AI system to act in the best interests of its user - borrowing from the legal concept of fiduciary duty. 
* In 2022, the EU proposed an AI Liability Directive, which would have made it easier for persons harmed by AI to seek compensation by shifting the burden of proof, so that AI providers would need to demonstrate they were not at fault, rather than victims having to prove they were. Though the proposal was later withdrawn, it presents an alternative accountability mechanism. 


Treaty-following AI
* Scholars have suggested giving AI systems responsibilities without rights by creating "treaty-following AI": AI agents that can be technically and legally designed to execute their principals’ instructions. This would theoretically allow principals (i.e. users) to be held responsible for an AI agent without the need to extend legal personhood.


Precedent for legal status of non-conscious entities
* There is a long history of granting forms of legal personhood to non-conscious entities such as corporations and ships. English common law’s doctrine of the deodand gave accountability without legal personhood: objects that caused a person’s death - for example, a runaway cart or mill wheel - were forfeited to the crown. Though abolished in 1846, its treatment of the object itself as the bearer of legal consequences may be a useful reminder that agency or consciousness is not necessary for legal accountability. 




Human Rights-Based Approaches
The application of human rights law in this area is disputed, but some note that it may offer a useful existing framework for thinking about the protection of digital minds, given that it is concerned with the question of what types of entities warrant legal protection from harm. 


Human rights law
* The Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights grant protections from slavery, torture, and cruel or degrading treatment, alongside rights to work, free speech, and fair treatment. 
* Some scholars view extending such rights to AI as either a devaluation of humanity or a distraction from enforcing existing human rights while others argue that protection from harm may be warranted for AI sentience candidates, beginning with a "bare minimum level of respect" or protection against the worst forms of harm.
* Beyond these core human rights instruments, several adjacent frameworks may also prove relevant as thinking about digital minds governance develops:
   *  The Sphere Standards - developed to define minimum humanitarian protections in crisis settings - offer a model for establishing baseline conditions of dignified treatment that is not contingent on the full legal personhood of those being protected. 
   * UNESCO's Universal Declaration on Bioethics and Human Rights and the UN norms on medical research (including the Declaration of Helsinki) govern how humans may treat entities of uncertain or vulnerable status in research contexts.


Children and “quasi-persons”
* Children typically hold limited rights paired with limited legal responsibilities. Some scholars have suggested that such frameworks could serve as a useful model for potential digital minds. For example, Mathison (2023) argues that AI systems should be treated as akin to children under a "quasi-personhood" rights regime. Similarly, frameworks governing adults with impaired decision-making capacity (e.g. mental health acts), which seek to protect individuals from harm while appropriately limiting autonomy, may also be instructive. 
* Other legal “quasi-persons” - including severely brain damaged individuals, fetuses, and future generations illustrate that an entity need not be a legal person to be a legal actor. 


Neurorights
* In 2021, Chile became the first country to constitutionalise the protection of neuro-rights - legal protections for brain activity and its associated data (neurodata). 
* The reform builds on existing constitutional protections for physical and psychological integrity, formally extending them to cover neurological data and cognitive processes into this biologically and medically rooted human rights framework. 
* Its practical force was demonstrated in 2023, when the Chilean Supreme Court ordered a neurotechnology company to delete a plaintiff's neurodata - the first enforcement of its kind.




Animal and Environmental Welfare
Recent decades have seen a significant expansion in the range of non-human entities afforded legal protection or recognition - from animals, to ecosystems, to rivers. While these frameworks were not designed with digital minds in mind, they illustrate ways in which we have previously extended moral and legal consideration beyond humans. 


Three developments are of particular relevance to the governance of digital minds: 
* (i) the formalisation of consciousness and sentience as legal thresholds;
* (ii) the push toward international standards for non-human welfare; and 
* (iii) the extension of legal rights to natural entities.


Consciousness and sentience
* The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (2012) was among the first formal statements recognising the likelihood of conscious experience in non-human animals. The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) extended this, acknowledging that all vertebrates - and many invertebrates - have a realistic possibility of conscious experience, and that this possibility alone makes it irresponsible to disregard their welfare. 
* This has been reflected in law. For example, the UK's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act (2022) requires government policy to actively consider and minimise harm to potentially sentient animals without conferring full legal rights or personhood. Similar provisions have since followed in the EU, New Zealand, and parts of Canada and Australia. Any analogous framework in AI would likely face similar definitional challenges. .
* While some see these frameworks as a valuable reference for digital welfare, others disagree and caution that resources spent addressing digital welfare/wellbeing may come at the cost of tackling animal welfare priorities. 


International Standards and Principles
* The International Coalition for Animal Protection proposed a Convention on Animal Protection for Public Health, Animal Well-Being, and the Environment in 2022, seeking to establish shared global baselines for which animals warrant consideration and how.
* The World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) has developed internationally recognised standards on animal welfare since the mid-twentieth century. Though not legally binding, member states that adopt them commit to transposing them into national law - demonstrating both the feasibility and limits of building welfare standards through scientific consensus rather than binding treaties.
* Animal research principles are widely held, but implemented differently in different jurisdictions. The principles follow the 3 Rs:
* Replace animals where possible.
* Reduce the numbers used.
* Refine how they are treated.
* Many regimes require an ethics review weighing risk against benefit before testing proceeds, with added protections for primates and other species. In many countries, invertebrates other than octopuses are not covered. Organisations such as NC3Rs promote these principles, and help develop guidelines used by international bodies such as the WHO and OECD.




Rights of Nature
The Rights of Nature movement recognises ecosystems as having inherent worth, and advocates that they should be able to defend their interests in court - a principle that several countries have now written into law. Notable examples include: 
* New Zealand declared the Whanganui River a legal person with fundamental rights in 2017.
* Ecuador's 2008 buen vivir framework, which grants constitutional protections for nature including the right to "integral respect”, restoration and governmental protection.
* The Colombian Supreme Court recognised the rights of specific Amazonian ecosystems in 2018. 
As a framework for thinking about digital minds, however, the analogy of environmental rights has limitations. Rights of Nature laws are grounded in the intrinsic value of living ecosystems - an assumption that may not transfer readily to AI systems, and may (as some scholars have noted) be inappropriate, given the environmental footprint of AI.
