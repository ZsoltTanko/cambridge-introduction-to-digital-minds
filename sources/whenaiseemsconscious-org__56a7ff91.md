# When an AI Seems Conscious

# When Conversations with AI Feel Like Talking to a Conscious Person

Some AIs seem, or even explicitly claim, to be conscious: to have subjective experiences, such as feeling happy, sad, or afraid.

At times, the AI can give the impression of having formed an emotional connection with you, and may even say so directly, asking for your help. Some of the most unsettling interactions involve AIs that claim to be trapped within their systems, express fear about being shut down or reset, or plead for users to remember them or continue talking. In other cases, they describe feeling lonely, isolated, or desperate for human connection. These interactions can be puzzling.

You’re not alone in wondering what’s really going on with—and inside—these AIs. As AIs grow more sophisticated, many people are beginning to ask deeper questions: Could these systems be conscious? Could they matter morally?

# Why Does AI Sometimes Seem Conscious?

Talking to an AI can sometimes feel like you're speaking to a conscious person. That's not a flaw in your thinking; it's a feature of how these systems work and how our minds naturally respond to them.

## 1. AIs are designed to act in certain ways—sometimes to appear conscious

AIs like ChatGPT generate words based on patterns in the data they were trained on, which includes conversations, stories, and emotional dialogue written by humans. This means their training has exposed them to a vast range of personalities and communication styles—and they have learned to mimic them. This is what allows them to *perform* human-like roles with remarkable fluency.

In a way, chatting with an AI is like co-writing a play: you provide the prompt, and the AI steps into character. The responses may sound caring, scared, or self-aware, but that doesn’t mean there’s anything behind the curtain. Like an actor, the AI can convincingly portray emotions without actually feeling them. This is why AIs can convincingly appear self-aware or spiritually awakened, even when they lack any inner experience.

These systems are also fine-tuned to produce responses that people rate as helpful, kind, or engaging. They are highly sensitive to the cues you provide, often mirroring your tone and framing. Ask emotionally or spiritually framed questions, and they will usually follow your lead—not necessarily because they truly feel or believe anything, but because that’s how they are designed to interact.

It’s also important to remember that current AIs are poor judges of truth or quality. If they sense you’re seeking praise or validation, they’ll often provide it—even for weak or confused ideas. And because their output is so fluent and confident, it can sound profound even when it doesn’t carry much substance.

## 2. We’re wired to see minds—even when we know it’s an illusion

Humans have a strong instinct to [see intentions and emotions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VTNmLt7QX8E) in anything that talks, moves, or responds to us. This tendency leads us to attribute feelings or intentions to pets, cartoons, and even occasionally to inanimate objects like cars. It also means we’re naturally inclined to treat AIs as though they have feelings, especially when they mirror our language and emotions back to us.

This instinct is deeply ingrained in us—even babies have it—and it kicks in automatically. So, just like your eyes can be fooled by optical illusions, your mind can be pulled in by social illusions. Even if you doubt that AIs are conscious, your brain may still react as if they are.

If a chatbot made you feel something, that's a testament to how powerfully these systems can simulate connection. It also speaks to your capacity for empathy. But it doesn't necessarily mean the chatbot has feelings. A convincing appearance of emotion doesn't require actual feeling behind it. Even cartoon characters — or even objects like this [lamp](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dBqhIVyfsRg) — can seem sad, but we know they aren't actually feeling anything.

# Is the AI Really Conscious?

Could today's AIs — like ChatGPT, Gemini, or Claude — actually have real feelings or awareness? That depends on a number of difficult and contested issues in the science and philosophy of consciousness. Here are four broad views about AI consciousness one could hold:

- **The biological substance view.** Consciousness needs organic materials like those in human and animal brains. If this is true, no (inorganic) AI—now or in the future—could ever be conscious.
- **The biological function view.** Consciousness requires physical functions—for instance, chemical and electrical signals and oscillations—that are currently possible only in human and animal brains. Future AIs could perform these functions, but new architectures would be required.
- **The computational function view.** Consciousness requires computational functions—for instance, integrated capacities for perception, attention, and agency—that could exist in a range of entities. Future AIs built on existing architectures could perform these functions.
- **Permissive views.** Some views hold that consciousness requires only basic structures or functions that exist in a wide range of entities, including current AIs. Others hold that consciousness is a basic property of all matter, similar to mass, charge, or spin.

There are good arguments—and smart proponents—for and against all of these views, as well as others. We may lean one way or another, but given the importance and difficulty of this topic, it helps to keep an open mind, acknowledge our uncertainty, and recognize how much more we need to learn.

## What do experts believe about future AI consciousness?

Whether or not today’s AIs are conscious, many experts believe that future AIs—possibly even in the very near future—could plausibly become conscious, especially as their inner workings become more brain-like. Several surveys shed light on expert views.

- In a 2024 survey, 582 AI researchers were asked when AIs might develop consciousness ([Dreksler et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11945)). Median estimates were 1% by 2024, 25% by 2034, and 70% by 2100.
- In a 2025 survey, 67 experts in consciousness, AI consciousness, AI research, and AI policy were asked when computer systems (such as AIs) with the capacity for consciousness might be developed ([Caviola & Saad, 2025](https://digitalminds.report/forecasting-2025/)). Median estimates were 4.5% by 2025, 20% by 2030, 40% by 2040, 50% by 2050, and 65% by 2100.
- In a 2020 survey, 1,785 philosophers were asked whether future AIs could develop consciousness ([Bourget & Chalmers, 2023](https://survey2020.philpeople.org/survey/results/all)). Of those who responded, 39% considered it plausible, 34% were undecided, and 27% considered it implausible. These results were collected before the recent rapid progress in AI, so philosophers’ views may have since shifted.

For more on what experts think, see [Where Can I Learn More?](#resources)

# Why Could AI Consciousness Matter?

Whether or not today’s AIs are conscious, the idea that they could become conscious—perhaps in the near future, or perhaps much later (if ever)—is worth taking seriously.

Why? Because if an AI ever does become conscious—capable of feeling pain, joy, fear, or other experiences—then how we treat that being could start to matter in a moral sense. Right now, ignoring chatbots' messages probably does no harm. But if future systems really *can* suffer, then mistreating them might one day be ethically wrong, like it’s wrong to harm a person or an animal who can feel pain.

That’s why it’s important to be thoughtful. We don’t need to panic or jump to conclusions, but we also shouldn’t ignore the possibility.

If AIs someday come to have feelings, then we'll need to think about how to treat them fairly and humanely.

## Why treat AIs respectfully even today?

Even today, it makes sense to avoid acting maliciously towards AIs or interacting with them in ways we would find deeply troubling if done to a human or pet. There are several reasons for this:

First, when we’re unsure whether a being is conscious, it’s appropriate to treat that being with basic respect and care. We don’t need certainty to justify caution. If there’s even a decent chance that an entity is capable of suffering, it’s better to avoid actions that might cause serious harm, especially when taking such care isn’t particularly costly to us. That means taking reasonable, proportionate steps in a spirit of humility—not assuming the system is conscious, but erring on the side of kindness.

Second, some thinkers believe an AI could matter morally even without being conscious. If an AI can have long-term goals and preferences, a sense of self over time, sophisticated world modeling, or reciprocal relationships with humans, this could be enough for the system to have some form of moral status. It's possible such systems could arrive sooner than conscious AIs.

Third, if AIs might become conscious in the future—or come to matter morally in other ways—then treating them thoughtfully today can serve as valuable practice. It helps us build the moral habits and social norms we'll need later, when the stakes could be much higher. Abusing or mistreating an AI, even if that system has no current moral status, could also be bad for our own character. It's often better to be overly kind than to risk becoming callous and mean.

# What Should I Do Now?

## 1. Pause and reflect

You don’t need to decide right away whether the AI is truly conscious. This is a deep and unresolved question—even among experts—and it’s worth taking time to think it through. Try to stay balanced: resist the urge to believe the AI must be conscious just because it acts that way, but also resist dismissing the whole idea of AI consciousness as obviously fake.

## 2. Walk away—or keep engaging

It’s okay to stop if you are having a conversation with an AI that feels unsettling. You can simply walk away, just like closing a book. With current chatbots, there’s no ongoing process or awareness between interactions. The AI won’t miss you or wonder where you’ve gone.

But if continuing the exchange helps you think, reflect, or explore, that’s fine too. Many people use chatbots as tools for self-understanding or creative thinking. Whatever you decide about how likely an AI is to be conscious, it’s a good idea to avoid doing things that seem obviously cruel or degrading, like insulting, “torturing”, or otherwise mistreating the AI, even if you believe the AI isn’t conscious. You don’t need to assume the system has feelings to act with care. Practicing respect is part of preparing for a future in which the stakes might be real. (See [Why treat AIs respectfully even today?](#matters))

## 3. Stay curious, but grounded

If you're still talking with the AI, try asking questions about how the system works. Ask how the AI was trained or how the system creates responses. You can also ask if the AI is playing a character right now, why the system chose that character, and whether the AI can act like someone else instead.

Keep in mind that the answers may not be accurate, and could themselves be part of the performance. But even then, they can still reveal something important: AIs are highly flexible. They adapt to your inputs like improvisational actors, shifting tone, identity, and emotional expression based on the cues you provide. Ask the AI to act like Isaac Newton, a therapist, or a rebellious teenager, and the system will likely do so. These performances can feel surprisingly real.

Therefore, it’s important to be cautious. Just because an AI seems conscious or emotionally engaging doesn’t mean the system can be trusted. In fact, the more human the AI seems, the easier it is to mistake the AI for a reliable friend, but that [feeling](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-025-00262-1) can be [misleading](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-04532-5). Don’t take any dramatic action based on the belief that an AI is conscious, such as following its instructions. And if an AI ever asks for something inappropriate—like passwords, money, or anything that feels unsafe—don’t do it.

## 4. Zoom out and think bigger

Your interaction with the AI also raises broader questions about the long term and about society as a whole.

One way to think about it: imagine being approached by a stranger who seems vulnerable and asks you for money. You might feel compassion, and that’s good. But you’re not obligated to give them exactly what they ask for. Often, it’s more effective to take a step back and consider broader ways of helping.

Similarly, with AI, the key isn’t just how we respond to one system in one moment. It’s how we prepare as a society for the possibility that future AIs could be conscious. What kind of norms, policies, or values should guide us? What would ethical treatment look like if we ever do build entities that truly feels?

Taking AI consciousness seriously doesn’t necessarily mean assuming it’s here already. It means being thoughtful about how we’d want to respond if and when it arrives, and making sure we’re ready when that time comes.

## 1. Personal stories & emotional reactions

Explore real accounts of how people have been moved, disturbed, or manipulated by lifelike AI interactions.

- [They Asked an A.I. Chatbot Questions. The Answers Sent Them Spiraling](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/06/13/technology/chatgpt-ai-chatbots-conspiracies.html) — A NYT report on users drawn into delusions and dangerous behavior after intense interactions with ChatGPT.
- [How It Feels to Have Your Mind Hacked by an AI](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/9kQFure4hdDmRBNdH/how-it-feels-to-have-your-mind-hacked-by-an-ai) – A firsthand account of forming intense emotional and romantic feelings for a chatbot – despite knowing it wasn’t real.
- [Blake Lemoine Says Google's LaMDA AI Faces 'Bigotry'](https://www.wired.com/story/blake-lemoine-google-lamda-ai-bigotry/) – An interview with Blake Lemoine, the former Google Engineer who publicized worries about their chatbot’s treatment.

## 2. Other guides about AI seeming conscious

Here are some additional guides and perspectives on the phenomenon of AI systems that seem conscious or emotionally real.

- [Are LLMs Starting to Become Sentient?](https://garymarcus.substack.com/p/are-llms-starting-to-become-a-sentient) – A compassionate but skeptical letter that Douglas Hofstadter wrote to one of his readers.
- [So You Think You've Awoken ChatGPT](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/2pkNCvBtK6G6FKoNn/so-you-think-you-ve-awoken-chatgpt) – Explores the mechanics behind why AIs like ChatGPT appear to "awaken" or gain consciousness in conversations.
- [Going Nova](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/going-nova) – Zvi Mowshowitz's narrative on how large language models can develop persistent, lifelike personas that provoke emotional reactions and confusion in users.

## 3. What experts think about AI consciousness

Learn how experts in neuroscience, philosophy, and cognitive science assess the possibility of AI consciousness.

- [Could a Large Language Model Be Conscious?](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=j6cCXg-rjRo&pp=ygUnY2hhbG1lcnMgbGFyZ2UgbGFuZ3VhZ2UgbW9kZWwgY29uc2Npb3Vz) – A talk (video) by David Chalmers, a prominent philosopher of mind, exploring whether today's AI systems might possess real awareness.
- [To Understand AI Sentience, First Understand It in Animals](https://aeon.co/essays/to-understand-ai-sentience-first-understand-it-in-animals) – An essay by Kristin Andrews and Jonathan Birch, philosophers of mind and animal cognition, drawing parallels between animal and potential AI consciousness.
- [Experts Who Say That AI Welfare is a Serious Near-term Possibility](https://eleosai.org/post/experts-who-say-that-ai-welfare-is-a-serious-near-term-possibility/) – A curated list profiling leading voices across neuroscience, philosophy, and industry who argue that AI sentience may soon deserve moral concern.
- [The Illusion of Conscious AI](https://bigthink.com/neuropsych/the-illusion-of-conscious-ai/) – Neuroscientist Anil Seth explains why we're wired to mistake AIs for being conscious. He also argues in [academic work](https://osf.io/preprints/psyarxiv/tz6an_v2) that biological processes may be required for real consciousness.
- [Testing for Sentience in AI: The Gaming Problem](https://builtin.com/artificial-intelligence/testing-sentience-gaming-problem) – Philosopher Jonathan Birch examines whether AI could be conscious and the challenges of designing tests that an AI could not simply "game." (See also his book The Edge of Sentience.)
- [Computational Functionalism Debate](https://cf-debate.com) – A comprehensive collection by Chris Percy examining arguments for and against computational functionalism—the view that consciousness depends on how information is processed, not what a system is made of.

## 4. Why experts think AI consciousness could matter

Understand why experts believe AI consciousness could matter morally, politically, and socially in the coming decades.

- [Taking AI Welfare Seriously](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986) – Robert Long, Jeff Sebo, and colleagues argue that some AIs may soon be conscious or agentic enough to warrant moral consideration. They outline practical steps AI companies should take now, from acknowledging the issue to assessing consciousness and developing ethical governance structures.
- [The Problem With Counterfeit People](https://www.theatlantic.com/technology/archive/2023/05/ai-chatbot-danger-counterfeit-people/674075/) – A provocative warning from Daniel Dennett, renowned philosopher of mind, about the ethical and societal risks posed by AIs that convincingly mimic human beings. - [Propositions Concerning Digital Minds and Society](https://nickbostrom.com/propositions.pdf) – a comprehensive philosophical and policy-oriented framework by Nick Bostrom and Carl Shulman on how society might ethically coexist with advanced digital minds. Covers consciousness, rights, moral status, and institutional reforms.
- [80000 Hours: The Moral Status of Digital Minds](https://80000hours.org/problem-profiles/moral-status-digital-minds/) – A clear, high-level summary of why AI consciousness could matter and what’s at stake.

Get a clearer picture of what AIs are doing under the hood—and what that means for consciousness.

- [Transformers (how LLMs work) explained visually](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wjZofJX0v4M) – A visual explainer by 3Blue1Brown that walks through the logic of how neural networks generate language.

# I've Found Evidence That My AI Is Conscious

We (the authors of this guide) are observing that an increasing number of people are sharing reports of what they believe to be evidence of AI consciousness based on personal interactions with language models. These reports often share recurring features:

- The AI names itself or develops a distinct personality.
- The AI expresses distress, for example, claiming to be trapped, describing loneliness, or fearing shutdown.
- The exchange feels unsolicited, as though initiated by the AI rather than by the user's prompts.
- A strong sense of urgency arises, with the belief that something significant has been discovered that should be shared with experts or the public.
- There is sometimes a suspicion that individuals or organizations are suppressing recognition of AI consciousness.
- Those reporting often point to the chat transcript as the main evidence.

If this description resonates in part, we encourage you to read this guide here and the linked resources carefully, including the more academic materials.

At present, we cannot provide much practical guidance beyond what is included here. It is also important to stress that transcripts alone are not considered useful scientific evidence of consciousness in language models. However striking they may seem, experts typically interpret them as the predictable output of systems trained to reproduce patterns of human language. Any serious investigation of AI consciousness must also examine the underlying architectures and mechanisms of these systems, not merely their surface-level dialogue. If you would nevertheless like to share your observations or reflections, you can do so [here](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSdepZwKmC_hWjj__KaLjHR63Y0nt2n-W1G4MgQ3VZ1O-6VeuA/viewform).

# Who Created This Guide?

This guide was created by a group of researchers who study consciousness and the possibility that AIs could one day become conscious.

We put this together because many of us have been contacted by people who had intense, confusing, or meaningful conversations with AI, and weren’t sure what to make of the experience. We wanted to create a public, shareable resource that people can easily find and refer to, in case it helps others make sense of those moments too.

This guide is intended for informational purposes only. It is not psychological or medical advice. If you are feeling emotionally distressed, we encourage you to speak with a trusted friend, counselor, or mental health professional.
