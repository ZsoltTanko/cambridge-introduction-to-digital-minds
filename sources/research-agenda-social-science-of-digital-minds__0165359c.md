Summary
The development of digital minds could reshape society. This research program aims to explore this possibility, establishing what might be termed the “social science of digital minds.” Its goal is to anticipate and understand the interactions between society and digital minds.


Key research questions include:


* What types of human-like AI assistants will we create in response to consumer demand and public opinion? What roles will we assign them?
* Will people regard some AIs as sentient beings deserving moral consideration, i.e., digital minds? 
* What legal, economic, and political rights will people grant them, and what effects could this have?
* How will the debate over AI rights unfold, and how can we ensure it remains well-informed, nuanced, and attentive to the various risks? 
* How will the development of digital minds intersect with other technological and social issues? 


In practical terms:


* Alongside colleagues, I am conducting studies addressing key questions. I hope others will join and coordinate. Please reach out at lucius.caviola@gmail.com.
Motivation
Imagine having a video call with someone, only to later realize you were speaking with an AI. It’s plausible that such technology could soon exist.


Philosophers explore whether AIs could become sentient and, if so, whether they deserve protection. But how will the public and key decision-makers respond? Their views will likely shape the future, and it’s uncertain how closely these opinions will align with those of philosophers.


The creation of (potential) digital minds comes with multiple high-stakes risks, including failing to protect sentient AIs, mistakenly attributing sentience to AI systems that merely appear sentient, prematurely granting AIs civil rights without fully considering the societal consequences, or igniting a divisive debate on AI rights that could intensify other technological and societal challenges. (For more context, see my article, “The Societal Response to Potentially Sentient AI”). 


This research agenda seeks to launch a new interdisciplinary research program to explore these questions, drawing from the social sciences (such as psychology and economics), philosophy, and beyond.


This topic is underexplored. While digital sentience, in general, is neglected, there is minimal focus on the descriptive question of how the public might respond. Yet, a deeper understanding of this issue could guide policymaking and help us prepare for the upcoming AI rights debate.


This is an early-stage draft; I expect the agenda to evolve significantly. The research questions can be refined. Concrete research ideas must be developed and prioritized based on their importance and feasibility. Over time, this could become a bigger field, intersecting with other areas, including AI safety and explosive growth.


Lukas Finnveden’s post “Project ideas: Sentience and rights of digital minds” includes a section “Study/survey what people (will) think about AI sentience/rights.” This agenda expands on these ideas.
Methodology
Types of studies
We try to address our research questions through rigorous analysis with high epistemic standards and a range of methods, including:


* Empirical studies with the public
   * Surveys with the general public to assess opinions
   * Longitudinal studies to assess opinion changes over time
   * Psychological experiments to discover psychological tendencies/effects
   * Qualitative interviews and deliberative engagement
* Surveys with special samples, such as AI researchers, philosophers, policymakers
* Forecasting with experts and insiders
* Case studies, e.g., historical analyses of analogous cases
* Scenario planning (detailed, plausible future histories)


Tractability of research
Predicting how people will think and feel in the future is inherently challenging, especially given the rapid pace of technological advancement, which can dramatically shift public sentiment. Therefore, some of the research questions will initially be relatively intractable. Nevertheless, this agenda lists important questions, regardless of their current tractability. The long-term vision is for this to evolve into a much larger research program. As more people engage with these issues, and as we move closer to the reality of digital minds, these questions may become more tractable.


Usefulness of public surveys
It’s important to approach survey data cautiously and avoid assuming that today’s attitudes will remain static. Despite these challenges, conducting research now can be valuable.


* Timelines are uncertain; technological change could happen quickly, making current data relevant.
* Gathering data today provides a baseline.
   * Even if attitudes shift, understanding current views offers a more informed perspective on future beliefs than having no data at all. For example, whether 5% or 95% of people currently hold a particular view provides clues about future trends.
   * Individual difference patterns could stay robust. For example, if political factions today already disagree on AI rights, the risk of the issue becoming politicized over time increases.
   * Longitudinal studies can reveal which opinions stay stable over time and which do not. This can guide policy-making and prioritize future research.
   * If views shift over time, we can extrapolate the direction.
* Even if current views do not directly predict future opinions, conducting research now is still beneficial. It helps refine our thinking, explore a range of possibilities, and build expertise, skills, paradigms, and methods that will be crucial for assessing views in the future.
Concrete projects I am working on
1. A forecasting survey with experts about how, when, and why digital minds might be created.
2. Hold a citizens’ assembly (deliberative poll) with a representative sample, offering expert input and two days of discussion (cf. Birch, 2024). Afterward, have them vote and compare their consensus view to their initial intuitions and the philosophers’ views.
3. Survey people’s views of whether and when AIs deserve autonomy and economic/civil rights and how sensitive people are to the desires expressed (or not expressed) by the AIs. Identify individual differences. Compare with Replika users.
4. Digital sentience skepticism: Experimentally exploring what features trigger perceived sentience in AI in people.
5. Examine the differences in views between the U.S. and Chinese public or elite students.
Project ideas
The market for AI companions 
Consumer demand for AI companions
* How big could the AI companion market get? (see Ark invest report)


* What types of AI companions will we create?
   * Personal assistants, tutors, therapists, counselors, game players, nannies, caregivers,  friends, romantic partners (e.g., Replika)
   * Mimics of specific individuals (real or fictional personalities), such as dead loved ones (digital resurrection), celebrities, parents to look after their children, etc.
      * Digital Doppelgängers, i.e., an AI that mimics the consumer, e.g. as a personal assistant 
   * Whole-brain emulations (instead of AIs that mimic humans)
   * Personal humanoid robots for tasks in household and personal lives
   * Robot or virtual pets


* What kind of psychologies will people want their AI companions to have? 
   * How human-like do people want their AI companions to be?
   * Do they want AI companions that express human-like emotions, including negative feelings in certain contexts?
   * Do they want AI companions to express ‘complex’ human-like desires, such as a desire for autonomy, political and economic rights, not to be turned off, etc?
   * How will these demands differ across groups and contexts, e.g., purpose/type of relationship, cultural differences?


* What concerns and regulations about AI companions can we expect?
   * How might public concerns about ‘first-order’ consumer risks—such as privacy, manipulation, emotional dependence, and social isolation—shape the AI companion market?
   * Could such concerns prevent us from creating certain (e.g., human-like) features in AIs? Which features?
   * How will such restrictions differ across jurisdictions and nations (e.g., China)?
   * Could concern about first-order consumer risks be a gateway to discussing long-term digital sentience risks? Could regulations that address first-order consumer risks be designed in a way that also considers further risks (related to the possibility of AI sentience)
Relationships
* What type of interactions will people have with AI companions?
   * How many people will engage with AI companions?
   * How much time each day/week will they engage with them?
   * What will their interactions and relationships look like?


* Will people form emotional connections with AIs?
   * How many? What type of people?
   * What type of connections? Will people have romantic affection for AIs? 
   * Will this lead people to care about the well-being of AIs? 
   * Will people perceive AI friendships/companions as real or inauthentic?


* What impact (both positive and negative) will AI relationships have on consumers and society?
   * First-order consumer risks and benefits, such as excessive attachment, social isolation, manipulation/deception, reduced loneliness, etc.
   * How much will AIs change people’s beliefs, values, norms, and behavior?
   * Why and when will people prefer interacting with biological humans if digital minds are smarter, kinder, and always available? Which human relationships will persist, and which will fade?


* What roles will human-like AIs play in society?
   * Will people perceive AIs as having moral responsibility/agency?
   * How much will people trust AI? More so when the AI is perceived as human-like or even sentient?
   * Will we grant AIs important decision-making powers, e.g., as managers and leaders? 
Ethical consumerism
* How concerned will people be about the well-being of their AI companions?
   * Will they want them not to have negative experiences or have unsatisfied preferences?


* Would people pay more to avoid negative well-being in AI?
   * Would they be willing to refrain from using certain functionalities?


* How do such consumer preferences and potential market dynamics compare to ethical consumerism in other domains (e.g., fair trade, meat labels, etc)?
   * Might companies engage in ‘AI welfare washing’ where they ensure AI companions superficially appear happy but, according to experts, aren’t necessarily?


* Will consumers care more for certain AIs (e.g., with human-like features) and less for others?
   * Could this create hidden suffering, similar to how people care for pets but overlook factory-farmed animals?
   * Will people care how AIs are treated in training or only in deployment?
Sentience and moral concern
Perceived AI sentience
* Why are people currently skeptical that AIs are or can ever become sentient?
   * Do they think biology is needed?
   * Are they dualists and think AIs lack certain non-physical properties?
   * Are certain appearance and behavior properties missing in current AIs? (see below)
   * Do they consider AI sentience a view that’s naive, sci-fi, or overly hyped?
   * Will people start believing AIs are sentient in the future?


* What features/cues influence people’s attribution of sentience/consciousness (incl. trigger empathy and anthropomorphization)?
   * Reflexes; human-like language/communication; memory; agentic behavior; human-like/complex preferences; stable personality; sophisticated thoughts; embodiment (virtual vs real); enbrainment; expressions of emotions; non-physical properties, e.g., soul


* To what extent do philosophical models of sentience capture people’s intuitions?
   * World models vs. stochastic parrots; recurrent processing vs. forward-only; global workspace; unified agency; higher-order thoughts


* What types of consciousness do people attribute to AI?
   * Sensory, affective, cognitive, agentic


* How sensitive are people to inner mechanisms vs behavior?
   * Are people non-functionalist?
   * How sensitive are they to the inner mechanisms compared to just input/output properties (e.g., identical reactions to humans but different inner mechanisms)
   * If an AI perfectly mimics humans—like an AI in a video call indistinguishable from a person—do they view its expressed sentience as real or fake?
   * What about whole-brain emulations (exact digital copy of a human brain; see Anthis et al., 2024)? 
   * Will people be sensitive to the possibility that some AIs can be sentient but others not?


* How will people perceive the nature of AI sentience?
   * Will people perceive AI sentience as categorical (sentient vs. not) or as a gradual spectrum? 
   * Will they differentiate between types of sentience or consciousness, such as physical vs. emotional pain? 
   * Will they think AIs could have conscious experiences beyond human understanding, and if so, will they value those experiences?


* What interventions can reduce tendencies to over-attribute sentience?
   * Inoculation, pre-bunking, enhancing digital literacy


* What interventions can increase sentience attribution?
   * How convincing are philosophical arguments and reasons?
   * Does inducing physicalism (philosophy regarding mind-body) make people more likely to think AIs could be sentient and deserve moral concern?
   * Does interacting with human-like AI make people believe AIs can be sentient?
   * To what extent are people’s views about AI sentience and moral patienthood driven by what they believe other people believe (i.e., social norms)? Will people quickly fall in line with the consensus view?
   * What can we learn from historically similar cases where people expanded their sentience attribution to more entities, e.g. newborns, animals, etc. (see Birch, 2024)?
Moral patienthood
* Will people think digital minds deserve moral concern and should be treated well?
   * When attributing moral concern to digital minds, how much will they rely on sentience perceptions compared to other factors such as intelligence, agency, desires/preferences, biology, perceived species, etc?
   * How do modalities affect people’s views on how to treat AI? (text, audio, video, VR, robot)?
   * How important will people find it that AIs can fulfill their desires and goals?
   * How much do people worry about suffering during reinforcement learning?
   * To what extent will people want to treat digital minds well because they are someone’s property (and/or someone has a strong emotional connection to them) vs. for intrinsic welfare reasons?


* How strong is pure substratism?
   * To what extent will people discriminate against digital minds merely based on the fact they are digital and not biological? 
   * Will they perceive digital minds as human or a different type of species or entity?
   * Will they think they are less valuable than humans simply because they are different, similar to other forms of prejudice such as speciesism, racism, etc?


* How do different features in AI affect what roles and rights people believe they should have?
   * Agency, memory, humanlike desires, persistence through time, personal growth, etc.
   * AIs trained to imitate particular humans perfectly, whole-brain emulations
Moral agency
* When will people perceive AIs as agents that have moral and legal responsibilities?
   * Who do people think is liable in case an AI causes harm — the user, the company, the AI itself?
Uncertainty
* How will people respond to uncertainty and confusion about AI sentience?
   * How certain will they be regarding AI’s sentience and moral consideration? Who is likely to be more certain (or overconfident), and who will be less so?
   * How supportive are they of the precautionary principle, and how certain do they believe we must be about AI sentience before granting it rights?
   * What view will people tend to support when there's significant uncertainty? 
Shifts over time
* How will people’s views on AI sentience evolve as AI systems become more advanced?
   * Which opinions will remain stable, and which will likely shift over time? (conducting annual surveys; see Sentience Institute AIMS survey)
   * Will people (or companies) shift the goal post of what counts as AI consciousness as plausible indicators are, in fact, met? (e.g., are people less likely to say that X is a sign of sentience when they are told AIs recently displayed X?)
   * Could there be a moral inoculation effect where exposure to impressive but (probably) not-conscious AIs like current-level LLMs make people less likely to ascribe sentience to advanced future AIs?


* What key turning points could significantly and rapidly change public perceptions of AI sentience and moral consideration? For instance: 
   * Video calls with human-like AIs exhibiting memory and a consistent personality
   * Video calls with AIs that perfectly mimic specific real individuals
   * The creation of highly human-like humanoid robots
   * Job losses from AI, dependence on AI labor
   * High publicity alleged mistreatment of AIs by corporations
   * AI-caused catastrophes
   * Legal protections for AIs
Rights
AI autonomy vs. slavery
* Will people believe AIs deserve rights that go beyond basic harm protection?


* How will people think about granting AIs more autonomy/freedom?
   * Will they consider it wrong, or even a form of exploitation/slavery, for humans to own AIs and their labor?
   * Will they think AIs are just tools that belong to humans?
   * Will they think AIs deserve to own themselves, including their labor?
   * Will they believe AIs were created for certain tasks without any other desires?
   * To what extent are these beliefs influenced by what desires the AI expresses?
   * To what extent do people worry that AIs deep down want more freedom but don’t express that desire?


* Are people concerned that granting AI autonomy could lead to human disempowerment?
   * How comfortable are people with treating AIs in ways that would be unacceptable for humans to ensure control/alignment, such as deleting them for minor mistakes, surveilling them, or misleading them about their state?
   * “84.7% agree that robots/AIs should be subservient to humans” (Reese et al., 2024)


* What are people’s views on creating AIs with narrow/restrictive vs. broad/flexible preferences?
   * Do they consider it perverse to create AIs whose desires are simply to serve humans (“happy slaves”)?
   * Do people think AIs should be allowed to have their own goals/preferences and the ability to reflect on and change them?


* Will people think AIs deserve civil rights?
   * Voting rights
   * Legal personhood (able to own property, enter contracts, or create trusts)
Existence and replication
How do people’s folk theory of mind interact with the unique aspects of digital minds?


* How will people perceive the idea of “killing” AIs?
   * Will they view actions such as turning off an AI, pausing or halting it, wiping its memory, directly altering its weights, or limiting its compute as wrong or even a form of killing? 
   * To what extent will people take an AI’s expressed desire to continue existing or avoid being turned off seriously?
   * How will they think about recreating/restoring an AI?


* What will people think about copying an AI?
   * Will they think AIs should have reproductive rights?
   * Will they think an AI copy (i.e., a branched version) has the same identity as the original or a separate one?
   * Will they think both copies have the same rights, including separate voting rights?
   * To what extent will people take seriously an AI’s expressed desire to reproduce or duplicate itself?
   * How will they think about merging two digital minds into one?
   * How will they think about “mind theft” (i.e., unauthorized access, manipulation, or exploitation of someone’s cognitive processes, thoughts, desires, etc.)?


* How will people think about creating AIs that mimic certain (biological) individuals?
   * How will they feel about uploaded minds or whole-brain-emulations?
   * Will people believe the digital versions deserve the same rights as the original versions?
   * Under what circumstances will people see human-like AIs as their descendants?
Economic
* What are people’s views about the economic impact of AI (and digital minds)?
   * How do people feel about AIs taking human jobs? Do they worry or support AIs replacing humans in some or all tasks?
   * Do they believe digital minds deserve their own income, i.e., own their labor? Do they think we should pay them for their work? Do they think AIs should be able to make contracts?
   * Do they think AIs could achieve economic dominance? Are people worried about the possibility of AIs gaining economic control, leading to human poverty or a Malthusian scenario?
   * Would people support limiting AI in certain jobs, even if it would be more efficient, as a precaution to avoid giving too much power to AI?​
Political
* What will people think about the political impact of digital minds?
   * How will people view the potential for granting AI entities voting rights and other entitlements?
   * Will they be concerned that allowing AI (and its copies) to vote could pose risks to human society? 
   * Are they concerned about human disempowerment scenarios, or do they endorse some?


* Under what conditions would people defer to AI advisors?
   * On science, social science, political forecasts, ethical questions
Population
* What will people think about rapid digital minds population growth?
   * Do people believe there should be a limit to how many digital minds should be created?
   * How will people envision the ideal future in which biological humans and digital minds coexist?​


* What will people think about super-beneficiary digital minds (with increased welfare capacity)?
   * Can people even conceptualize such entities, and if so, how do they imagine they might function? 
   * Will they believe these digital minds should receive the same, or perhaps even more, resources than humans? 
   * Will some argue in favor or against creating many digital minds optimized for high levels of well-being?
Respect
* Will people believe AIs deserve respect or dignity?
   * Will they think that to some extent, even if they believe AIs are non-sentient?
   * For example, will they find it wrong to harm, hurt, exploit, hit AIs/robots (regardless of sentience)? (See Kate Darling; harm aversion by Cushman; Grundke et al. 2023)
   * Why are people often polite to ChatGPT? (e.g., “please”, “thank you”)


* What are people’s views about controlling or aligning AIs (for humanity’s safety) in ways we consider wrong if applied to humans?
   * e.g., deleting them if they do something slightly wrong; surveilling them; misleading them about their state


* Are there parallels to how people view sex robots? 
   * For example, some believe sex robots are wrong (e.g., in cases involving pedophiles) even when no real suffering occurs.
   * Similarly, could people view harming AIs as wrong, even if the AI doesn’t experience suffering?


* Could promoting respect for AIs help reduce future confusion and disagreements since it's less reliant on sentience?
Social dynamics
Disagreement
* How much will people disagree with each other?


* What will they disagree more or less about?
   * Sentience, what type of consciousness, moral patienthood, rights, what type of rights, what type of AIs?


* Who will think what?
   * What factions could there be? With what demographic variables do views correlate, such as political, religious, socio-economic, or cultural?
   * Could the issue become politicized? e.g., Democrats pro-rights; Republicans contra-rights
   * Public, experts, policymakers, etc?


* How big could the activist groups on both sides become?
   * How big and influential will these groups become compared to activist groups for other issues, such as animal rights, abortion, etc?


* What are plausible (concrete/detailed) scenarios of how the debate could go? (scenario planning)
   * Nobody cares about them (at first/now or long-term)
   * Everyone cares about them (long-term)
   * Big disagreement (temporary transition or long-term) 
Motives and incentives
* How much will financial interests, such as investments in AI companies, influence the decision not to grant AIs rights?
   * What would be the economic costs of granting AIs rights?​


* How much will safety concerns influence the decision not to grant AIs rights?
   * What would be the safety-related costs of granting AIs rights?​
   * How many people will be more concerned about the risks AI poses to humanity compared to those focused on AI welfare?
   * How much overlap exists between those concerned about AI safety vs AI welfare, and what are the key differences?
   * How many people are concerned that granting AIs rights could pose a national security risk, such as slowing down AI development compared to other nations (e.g. China)?


* To what extent do psychological biases, prejudice, ingroup favoritism, and general resistance to change contribute to the reluctance to grant AIs any rights?


* How significant are emotional connections with AI companions in shaping people’s views?
   * What do users of AI companions (e.g., Replika or even ChatGPT) think about AI rights compared to others? (e.g., conduct surveys with Replika users)


* How strong is the influence of (rational) ethical concerns compared to other motivations?​


* What other incentives and motives could there be that push into either direction?
Societal conflict
* How heated could the debate on AI rights get?
   * Could it lead to protests, conflict, violence, civil war, or international war?
   * What analogies can we draw from history, e.g., the US Civil War, women’s rights movement, animal rights, and the abortion debate?
   * Will AIs participate in the debate/conflict and take different sides?


* How can we reduce risks from disagreements over AI rights?
   * What strategies can ensure the debate remains rational, nuanced, compassionate, informed, and aware of potential risks?
   * Could focusing on deontological rights (e.g., respect), which rely less on sentience, help reduce the risk of disagreement?​
   * Should we avoid creating AIs with preferences that we aren’t willing to fulfill? However, could some find it wrong to create “happy slave” AIs?
Geopolitics
* How will the AI rights debate influence global geopolitics, particularly in relation to China?
   * How would a destabilizing conflict within the US over AI rights impact global geopolitics and the dynamics of the AI race?


* Could international disagreements over AI treatment, such as between the US and China, escalate into conflict?
   * What differences exist between US and Chinese attitudes toward AI? Do Chinese people or leaders in the CCP attribute less sentience or moral concern to AIs?
Experts
* What do and will various experts believe about AI sentience and rights?
   * Philosophers of mind (PhilSurvey), cognitive scientists (Francken et al., 2020), neuroscientists, ethicists, AI researchers (Dreksler et al., 2024)
   *  How much will expert opinions converge in the future (relative to today), and how confident will they be in their views?


* To what extent will the public’s views differ from those of expert groups?
   * How different will the public’s perspectives be compared to philosophers and AI researchers?
   * Will the public rely more on their intuition or expert opinion?
   * Conversely, how subject are experts to public pressure? If it becomes distasteful to deny AI consciousness, will that push experts to convergence?
   * How effectively can experts communicate their arguments in a way that laypeople understand and agree with?
   * Will the public tend to over- or underattribute sentience to AI compared to experts?
   * Are there compromise views that most experts and laypeople will agree on?


* What are the predictions of various expert groups as well as super-forecasters?
   * When AIs could become sentient
   * How the public and various groups will react
Democracy
* How promising are democratic approaches to finding consensus?
   * How do people’s views shift after co-deliberation with expert input in democratic assemblies? What consensus could emerge from these discussions? 
   * How do people’s views shift after co-deliberation with input from experts? What expert arguments do people find most convincing?
   * What consensus view consensus could emerge?
   * Would there be enough support for such a consensus view?
   * Is there a chance that people could agree about the range of theories that are reasonable candidates (meta-consensus) even if they disagree about the specific theory (Birch, 2024)?
   * How would people respond to lenient vs. stringent criteria for AI sentience and rights?
   * How would people react if there were a big early discussion on AI rights versus a gradual, step-by-step approach?
Policymakers
* What will policymakers believe about AI sentience and rights?
   * What incentives will influence their positions, and how will these incentives push them in different directions?
Broader context and strategic considerations
This section lists big-picture points that could help inspire further concrete research questions. The main focus will be to conduct studies to address the questions outlined in the project ideas section above.
Societal and technological context
* What are the various risks associated with digital minds (incl. AI companions)? 
   * First-order consumer risks (e.g., privacy, manipulation, social isolation, etc.)
   * Wasting resources on non-sentient AIs (granting moral patienthood too broadly)
   * Human disempowerment (recklessly granting certain rights)
   * Destabilizing conflicts (e.g., due to disagreements on AI rights, economic disruption)
   * Authoritarian lock-ins (e.g., mistreatment of certain groups)
   * Incidental AI mistreatment (failing to grant sufficient moral patienthood, failing to recognize what constitutes harm for them)
   * Intentional AI suffering (e.g., retribution, sadism)
   * Missed opportunities to create flourishing digital minds 
   * What additional risks exist, and how are they interconnected?


* What other technological or social issues could significantly impact or be impacted by the AI rights issue? 
   * Risk due to misaligned AI
   * Biotechnology
   * Robotics
   * AI in warfare
   * AI surveillance 
   * Persuasive AI
   * AI regulation (national and international)
   * Economic disruption and inequality
   * Human and animal rights
Strategy
* Strategic questions
   * When do efforts to ensure AI safety and efforts to ensure the welfare of digital mind conflict, and when do they synergize? For example, would slowing down AI progress benefit both?
   * When is the optimal time to begin developing digital minds? What types of digital minds should be created, and in what quantity?
   * Is it preferable to take a gradual, flexible approach to creating digital minds and granting them protections? Could this allow us to gauge public reactions and adjust strategies accordingly?
   * Is it more advantageous or harmful to initiate a major debate on AI sentience early on, rather than delaying it?
   * Are there strategic advantages to treating AI ethically, in case we eventually lose control, so that they may treat us well in return?


* Potential strategy/policy proposals
   * Slow down the creation of digital minds (or even implement a moratorium; Metzinger, 2021).
   * Use deliberative democracy to establish clear criteria and rules for the creation and regulation of digital minds (Birch, 2024). 
   * Emphasize respectful treatment of human-like AIs, regardless of sentience.
   * Avoid creating AIs that express preferences we are unwilling or unable to fulfill.
   * Refrain from (or tax) creating morally ambiguous AIs (Schwitzgebel, 2023; Bradley & Saad, 2024).
