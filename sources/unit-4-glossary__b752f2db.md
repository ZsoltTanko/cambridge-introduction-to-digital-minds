Unit 4 Glossary
The below terms may be useful in Unit 4: 




AI Psychosis: mania-like episodes, delusional thinking or paranoia that can emerge or worsen through AI chatbot use. This is a relatively new phenomenon, with some dispute as to whether it is a media label or a relevant medical category, and the extent to which chatbot use plays a causal role or is merely correlated. Still, other research appears to link mental health harms with sycophancy in AI models, and users’ perceptions of sentience or deification of the chatbot as a supreme or cosmic intelligence. Such experiences can culminate in grandiose delusions (feeling chosen or messianic), paranoid claims (being spied on or targeted) and – tragically – instances of violence towards self and others. See Microsoft’s definition here or a 2025 academic study here.

Anthropomorphism: when humans attribute human characteristics, emotions, or mental states to non-human entities. In the context of digital minds research, anthropomorphism is of particular interest as a potential source of cognitive bias: a tendency to perceive or report consciousness, sentience, or moral status in AI systems based on superficial behavioural or linguistic cues. These cues may have been engineered as part of the product design, e.g. the choice to make a chat interface with a name.


Anthropodenial: When humans reflexively deny or dismiss human-like characteristics, emotions, or mental states in non-human entities. In the context of digital minds research, some argue that scepticism about AI consciousness or moral status may reflect motivated reasoning or cultural assumptions about the uniqueness of human minds.


Seemingly Conscious AI (SCAI): AI systems that imitate consciousness convincingly enough that humans believe them to be conscious; or AI systems with features that encourage users to attribute consciousness to them. These features may include, for example, anthropomimetic features like voices, faces or particularly human-like socio-relational skills.
