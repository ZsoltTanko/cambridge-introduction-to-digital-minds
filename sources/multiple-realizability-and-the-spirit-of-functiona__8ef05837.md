Multiple realizability and the spirit of functionalism
Rosa Cao1

Received: 14 April 2021 / Accepted: 25 November 2021

Abstract
Multiple realizability says that the same kind of mental states may be manifested by
systems with very different physical constitutions. Putnam (1967) supposed it to be
“overwhelmingly probable” that there exist psychological properties with different
physical realizations in different creatures. But because function constrains possible
physical realizers, this empirical bet is far less favorable than it might initially have
seemed, especially when we take on board the richer picture of neural and brain
function that neuroscience has been uncovering over the past forty years, in which
all sorts of brain activities play crucial roles beyond all-or-nothing electrical signaling. Because of its evolutionary history, the brain’s metabolic and informational
processes are inextricably intertwined. The resulting complex integrated functions
impose more constraints on possible realizers than the clean, single-purpose functions usually cited as examples in discussions of multiple realizability—with ramifications for the functionalist and computationalist foundations of cognitive science,
artificial intelligence, and the philosophy of mind.

I want to show that this bet is far less favorable than it might initially have seemed.
Far from freeing us from worrying about substrates, functional roles constrain physical realizations. It is only in virtue of some of its physical properties that any particular component is able to play its particular functional role. Conversely, having to play
a particular functional role imposes constraints on what kinds of material stuff can do
the job. Ned Block (1997) calls this the “Disney principle”—but it’s really more of
an anti-Disney principle: you can’t have dancing cups or plates made out of the stuff
that normal plates and cups are made of.
What is the relevant sense of “can’t” here? Discussions of multiple realizability have often been concerned primarily with metaphysical possibility.1 But if we
care about functionalism as a guide to cognitive science and artificial intelligence
research, big science connectomics or even the fringe hope of uploading one’s mind
into a computer, then nomological possibility (in the relatively conservative sense of
physically-possible-in-this-world-if-we-had-enough-resources) is the more relevant
standard.
This paper can be seen as an cautionary note, joining a chorus of work over the
last few decades, all aimed at downgrading our confidence in intuitions about how
the mind works that abstract away from the messy details of physical implementation in a biological organism.2 Prominent earlier arguments against this canonical
version of MR include Bechtel & Mundale (1999), Polger (2004, 2008), and Shapiro
(2000, 2004, 2008), describing problems with traditional formulations (and putative
instances) of multiple realization.

brain function that neuroscience has been uncovering over the past forty years, in
which all sorts of brain activities beyond electrical signaling play crucial roles in the
functional architecture and operation of the system, multiple realizability will seem
less obvious, at least at the level of neural function and organization.
Here’s the basic argument: Functions impose constraints on their realizers. Complex integrated functions impose more constraints than ones that can be decomposed
into simpler independent functions. Mental functions are both complex and thickly
integrated with physiological functions. So the functional organization of the brain at
the cellular level exhibits characteristics that make it unlikely to be multiply realizable at that level of description.

at the personal level, at the level of subpersonal processing, and at the fine-grained
neural level. I will start by arguing against the nomological possibility of MR on

presumption of MR more generally. MR at the neural level entails MR at higher

2 Why neurons—and brains—are hard to replace
2.1 The neural replacement scenario
In one vivid example, Chalmers (1995) describes a scenario in which a person’s neurons are replaced, one by one, with silicon functional duplicates, until all the work
of the brain is performed by an entirely artificial system.7 The resulting alternative
realization is supposed to be an exact functional duplicate of the original over a short
period of time.
Its replacement is a silicon chip that performs precisely the same local function
as the neuron. We can imagine that it is equipped with tiny transducers that take
in electrical signals and chemical ions and transforms these into a digital signal
upon which the chip computes, with the result converted into the appropriate
electrical and chemical outputs. As long as the chip has the right input/output
function, the replacement will make no difference to the functional organization
of the system.
It is true that if the silicon chip has the right input/output function, then the replacement will make no difference to the functional organization and behavior of the system, since that is just what the functional organization is supposed to capture. The
key question is: can a silicon chip do these things, given the nomological constraints?

To begin with, notice that by focusing on neuron replacement, the replacement
theorist is tacitly accepting whatever turns out to be the best scientific account of the
brain’s functional units — not just picking them out, but also characterizing their
functional role. That means that as our best scientific account of neural function
improves, proponents of MR should update their bets accordingly. I would add that
since these are empirical claims, the burden of proof should lie at least as heavily on
proponents of MR as on its opponents.
In order to specify the input-output function of a silicon chip replacement, we need
to specify the input-output function of the neuron that it is replacing. On our best neuroscientific understanding, the input-output function of a neuron is not exhausted by
electrical excitation on the input side and an all-or-nothing signal on the output side.
At the very least, the I/O profile of a successful replacement neuron should be sufficient to account for the original’s contribution to behavior and to the electrical excitation of other neurons—and what is required for that is considerable more complex:
spatially varying, history-dependent sensitivity to a variety of biochemical impingements on the input side, and then outputs that include self-modification8 and changes
in connectivity as well as action potential generation and neurotransmitter release.
These requirements provoke constraints that are not merely practical or technological ones. Rather, they are nomological constraints of the same kind as those that tell
us that that water is not superconducting at room temperature, or that the biochemical constitution of DNA molecules limits their possible three dimensional configurations. I’ll examine the requirements and constraints one at a time below.
2.2 Why neural replacement is hard
a) Spatiotemporal characteristics of a neuron’s spiking responses.
Neurons are not simple bipolar devices. Rather, they are spatially extended, with
broad dendritic arbors (these may span a volume hundreds of microns across, while
the cell body is only about 40 microns across) and often significant axon lengths (e.g.
across the corpus callosum). As a result, inputs to the cell are spatially distributed
in functionally significant ways; how they combine to produce outputs (which are
also spatially distributed) is related to their relative arrival times as well as intracellular propagation speeds. Furthermore, those conduction speeds change constantly,
since the conductance of the cell membrane changes depending on what is currently
happening.9

b) Transducers and chemical signaling
We also have to take into account the many kinds of input: excitatory, inhibitory,
and other biochemically mediated modulations that affect the particular way that
excitatory and inhibitory signals are combined. Action potentials depend on tens of
thousands of selective ion channels, with dynamics that are sensitive to the local biochemical milieu. In order to duplicate the functional sensitivity of a single neuron to
its neighbors, we would need a large number of sensors/transducers.
How many? We would need one for every ion channel (of which there are tens of
thousands in a cellular membrane, each with highly specific conductance curves for
different ions14) and receptor protein in the neuronal membrane. They would need to
mimic not only the particular ion specificity of the original channels, but also their
widely varying gating capacities, including non-linear voltage sensitivity (the occurrence of which in natural sodium channels is responsible for neural spiking) and
ligand sensitivity (in which their conductance is modulated by signaling molecules).
We also have to deal with instances of diffuse signaling, which are not limited to
a discrete and specifiable number of synaptic locations on the cell’s membrane. One
kind of signaling involves nitric oxide (NO), which is one of several short-lived and
highly reactive molecules synthesized on demand by endothelial cells in the brain’s
blood vessels, as well as by astrocytes and neurons. Post-synaptic neurons use it to
signal to pre-synaptic neurons (one of the mechanisms for depression and facilitation of synaptic transmission). Nitric oxide diffuses freely across cell membranes.15
How does a piece of silicon, not naturally sensitive to nitric oxide, either produce this
molecule or respond to it?

not suffice: because NO can drift through the cell membrane, the silicon replacement

c) Biophysical sensitivities
Our brains are sensitive to temperature, so that neurons have more trouble firing as a
brain region is cooled (on a scale of 1–2 degrees C). Temperature fluctuations in our
brains, perhaps caused by changes in local activity and blood flow, can have functional consequences. For a silicon chip to duplicate those dispositions to respond, it
would need temperature sensors as well.
Taken together, the individual constraints surveyed so far point to a more general
principle: that silicon chips are likely not to be the best substrate for an alternative
realization, because responses that come naturally to chemical and biological systems have to be engineered explicitly into the silicon.16 That work is done for free in
the original system—and in fact, it is probably because those physical features were
already part of the original system that they were exploited for the system’s function.
I’ll come back to this idea in Sect. 3, but the general idea is just the old observation that evolution tends to exploit existing features and recruit them to new uses. I
think it’s important not to underestimate efficiency of analog machinery compared to
simulating everything digitally when there are an astronomical number of interacting
components to simulate.

e) The functional role of glia and other non-neuronal cells
There are also biochemical functional interactions on both the input and output
sides with non-neuronal brain cells (glia) which do not themselves produce action
potentials. We know that astrocytes play important roles in the regulation of synaptic
transmission, partly through their re-uptake of neurotransmitters at the synapse, and
partly through their own release of neurotransmitters.18 As we’ll see, the replacement
theorist reasonably agrees that if glial cells play a non-trivial role, they will also be
replaced as functional components of the system.
An astrocyte replacement would look very much like the silicon neuron replacement we have already described. It would have to be able to contact the hundreds of
neurons (or neuron-replacements) within a normal astrocyte’s 200-300 micron range.
It would have to be able to send its modulatory packages of transmitters at the right
times, in the right places. These are daunting challenges, given the tiny space we have
to work with, and the material constraints imposed by the necessity of having the
replacement be sufficiently physically compliant and immunologically inert so as to
not damage its neighbors. Inflammation would be a major problem for any replacement that was unable to soothe the immune system.

We might be tempted to say that glia contribute to mental states only through their
modulation of neurons. That would mean that there is after all no need to include
astrocytes themselves—all we would need to do is build into the neuron-replacements the same patterns of activation that they would have had if there had been
astrocytes. Such an attitude leads inexorably to behaviorism.
Consider: what does any particular central neuron do? Unless it is among the
extreme minority that receive direct input from the periphery, or project out of the
brain, what it does is modulate other neurons, its own physiology, and perhaps its
own morphology. The neurons which receive its outputs then do the same. In essence,
only the “output” layer, or the set of neurons immediately prior to muscle activation,
can be said to be doing anything beyond modulation. The argument against including
astrocytes in our alternative realization works just as well against neurons—which is
to say, too well; we’d be throwing out the brain with the plumbing if we only considered the most superficial input and output layer of neurons as functionally relevant.
More generally, for the system to transition from state to state via a different process is for it to exhibit a difference in functional organization. If the system reaches its
current state B as a consequence of the actions of glia and neurons in the immediately
preceding state, then it is exhibiting a different functional organization than that of
a system which reaches the identical state B through the actions of neurons alone, at
least if the functional profile we are trying to duplicate is given at the single cell level.
Thus the temptation to subsume inconvenient functional components into their
more convenient neighbors (although as we have seen, neurons are not so convenient either) should be resisted if what we are after is a duplicate that preserves the
functional organization of the system. It’s true in any modular organization that we
can shift the functions of some components into others—but then we have changed
the organization. And if the internal organization doesn’t matter, then we are back to
a kind of behaviorism—and that is a view most proponents of multiple realizability
would not endorse.
2.3 Wholesale replacement and the pervasiveness of interface problems
Now it might seem that all these nasty complications arise only when we try to replace
a single cell. For the purposes of an argument for multiple realizability, what really
matters is the wholesale replacement scenario, in which all these messy interfaces
between cells could be eliminated in favor of clean and efficient signaling connections of whatever kind turn out to be most easily implemented in silicon. Instead of
changing the physical shape of cells and whether or not they physically contact each
other, or changing their protein configurations in order to modulate their sensitivity,
couldn’t we simply insert updating functions that tell them which inputs to pay attention to, and how much? This brings us to the next part of the replacement scenario,
where, as Chalmers describes it:

need sensors, because in order to produce the appropriate responses to characteristics of the local non-neuronal environment, the relevant characteristics must first be
detected. For example, we need to know what the concentrations of various molecules are at different locations on the interface between our wholesale replacement
and the biochemistry at the borders. And we still need transducers capable of generating biochemical output if we are to maintain an indistinguishable interface with the
tendrils of the rest of the biological body that are everywhere in the brain, from the
vascular system to the cerebrospinal fluid.

associated with the cerebral vasculature. In addition to transporting metabolic fuels,
oxygen, and removing waste products, blood flow is also responsible for transporting
signaling molecules from elsewhere in the body, including small molecules that cross
the blood-brain barrier (or else psychotropic drugs taken orally would not work).
These molecules include ones that signal satiety from the gut, feedback from skeletal
muscles, and hormones, which affect cognition as well as behavior.

The key point is that it is not enough for the replacement theorist for a replacement brain merely to produce the same outputs given the same inputs, if it does so

The output of the blood vessels, to which every cell in the brain is exposed, makes
it so that there is no clear boundary to the brain within which we can rest assured
that the only inputs a brain cell receives are from other brain cells. The blood vessels
carry causal influences to and from the rest of the body in ways that affect the inner
functional organization of the brain, and not just its sensory inputs and behavioral
outputs.19 Can we replace the blood vessels as well? Not without re-jiggering the rest
of the circulatory system and the body.
The problem is that “the periphery” is actually everywhere, and interactions
between the brain and the rest of the body are not neatly confined to small and
localized interfaces (say at the spinal cord, cochlea, and retina). So even “just” the
biochemical mechanisms at the periphery will still present a significant challenge.
To make neural replacement plausible, we need to be able to partition off the parts
important for mental functioning from the rest of the body. But contra armchair neural replacement enthusiasts, no matter how many neurons (and glia) we replace, there
will always remain significant biochemical residue, because the functionally important parts of the brain are inextricably bound up with rest of the body. As Dennett
puts it in Kinds of Minds (1996), “one cannot tear me from my body, leaving a nice
clean edge.”20
But even if we can’t tear the brain from the body, we might ask: why not an alternate realization of the whole body-brain bundle? The response is the same: Even
if it were possible for such a bundle to realize the same behaviors, it would not be
due to its identical functional organization. Rather, it will be because differences in
its functional organization accommodate the differences in its material constitution,
with mutual compensation allowing the system to approximate the original behavior.21 These compensatory differences, rather than making an alternative functional
organization possible, instead rule it out: by the standards of the non-behaviorist
functionalism we started out with, differences in functional organization entail differences in mental properties, including potentially differences in the qualitative features experienced. This is not a problem for functionalism per se, but only a problem
for consequences of functionalism that rely on or entail multiple realizability.

3 Generative Entrenchment and the Multiplication of Constraints
The importance of biological details described so far goes against the received view
in the philosophy of mind. But perhaps this shouldn’t be surprising, but rather, exactly
what we should expect, given that neurons and brains are biological entities evolved
within a biological organism. Complex integrated functions are less likely to be multiple realized than simple modular ones, and the principle of generative entrenchment
tells us that we should expect complex integrated functions in evolved systems.
3.1 Multiplexed functions multiply constraints
From an everyday perspective, it may seem obvious that function constrains material
make-up. A bicycle chain cannot be made of just anything—certain physical properties are required in order for it to perform as required within the functional organization of the bicycle. Swiss cheese would make for a terrible bicycle chain, let alone a
mind. Functional properties are not nomologically independent of material properties. Furthermore, not all physical materials are candidates for all functions. There
are excellent reasons that our computer chips are made of an exact combination of
silicon doped with other elements, and not, say, water with a bit of CO2. We could
make some kind of computer out of water and pipes, but it would not have the same
fine-grained functional profile at the level of speed, size, or reliability.24
The usual examples used to illustrate the independence of physical description
from functional description tend to be relatively simple man-made artifacts, such as
mousetraps and corkscrews. These artifacts, unlike the brain, do have easily decomposable non-holistic structure. That is, there is both a clear functional decomposition
of the task (bottle opening) into subtasks (grasping the cork, transmitting external
force to lift the cork relative to the bottle), and a clear assignment of these subtasks to
specific physical subcomponents of the artifact (the screw, the lever arms). Moreover
the interface of the physical components with each other is simple (low-bandwidth),
and only depends on a few of the physical properties of the components. This modular organization makes it easy to imagine replacing any one component with another
one that does the same thing (relative to the relevant level of description)—“has the
same input/output profile”—but is made of a different substrate.
Even the more sophisticated examples of multiple realization involve domainspecific activities. If we consider the achievements of expert systems such pokerbots,
Watson, deep neural networks for image recognition, or self-driving cars, it seems
plausible that highly domain-specific capacities are multiply realizable. But plucking

3.2 Generative Entrenchment
Whence this difference between brains and artifacts? The source of the brain’s entangled internal organization is what Wimsatt calls generative entrenchment: 27
A deeply generatively entrenched feature of a structure is one that has many
other things depending on it because it has played a role in generating them
… Systems shaped by selection processes show significant degrees of adaptation, so things that become entrenched in them are commonly elements or parts
of functional designs …with accumulating dependencies, seemingly arbitrary
contingencies can become profoundly necessary, acting as generative structural
elements for other contingencies added later.
(pp 133–135, Re-Engineering Philosophy for Limited Beings, 2007)
As systems evolve and become more complex over time, many functions tend to get
multiplexed onto the same components, because that infrastructure is already there
and available—in some sense, “free.” So components that previously played only
supportive or metabolic functions in an organism might become involved over time
in realizing functions that seem more paradigmatically mental—for example, producing coordinated responses to a changing environment. And because the metabolic
functions are crucial to the survival of the system, they cannot be eliminated without
bringing the whole edifice down with them.
As the discussion on neural replacement above shows, brain function relies on a
larger subset of the material properties of its realizers than human artifacts such as
mousetraps. The more material properties a system exploits in order to perform its
function, the harder it will be to replace the material the system is made of. Over the
course of the evolution of the very complex system that is the brain, readily available properties have had their effects co-opted as functions which then contribute to
the operation of the system as a whole. So while a mousetrap has plenty of “unemployed” properties to spare after its behavior and functional organization have been
accounted for—properties that could be different in an alternate realization without
affecting the function—the brain does not.28
Moreover, the specification of a particular substrate even at the sub-cellular level
produces constraints that ramify upward to the cellular level. Once a few simple
molecules or components are fixed (say water or oxygen), they then constrain the
components that can smoothly interface with them in the right ways, which pose their
own constraints in turn on other components, until possible realizations of the whole
system have been clamped down to options not significantly different from our own
biological one. Being embedded in a biological system locks down the kinds of roles
that functional components must play. To quote Dennett again,

3.3 Examples of generative entrenchment in the brain

novel functions. Glia precede neurons in development; they are necessary for neural
survival in virtue of providing metabolic support, and they modulate neural activity
responsible for cognitive activities such as memory formation. More interestingly,
there is some evidence (e.g. Han et al., 2013) that glia play a more substantive cognitive role in humans than in simpler organisms (which don’t exhibit those cognitive
capacities in the first place). So we see a component that initially plays only a supportive or permissive metabolic role becoming integrated into the cognitive architecture over evolutionary time. This pattern of dependence between metabolism and
what might be termed “informational” capacities shows up at many different scales
within the organism.

synapse to “recharge” after releasing neurotransmitters. But in sophisticated nervous
systems, adaptation has come to be exploited by the system as a way of detecting
novelty—a change in the patterns of stimulation produces more activation than the
same stimulation over and over again. Given a framework in which lowered activity
functions to signal lack-of-novelty, further mechanisms can then be scaffolded on, in

3.4 Why can’t multiplexed functions be unbundled and separately realized?
Why is it that the multiple functional roles played by particular components cannot
be decomposed, allowing individual functional roles to be alternatively realized by
separate components? Why can’t we separate out the energetic role of ATP, and each
of its distinct signaling roles from each other? Why not have one component that
plays the energetic role, another that plays the extracellular signaling role, a third that
plays the intracellular modulation role, and so forth? Suppose we did, along with further components that make sure that these functions are all appropriately coupled, so
that when ATP is depleted, they all slow down (i.e. preserving the original functional
profile of the component). What then prevents us from putting a black box around
the whole ensemble and saying, this is a single functional component that plays the
ATP-role in the system.
Such an approach may work if there are not too many components or too many
functions per component. But when there are many components and functions that
would cross-cut the compositions of individual black-boxes, then attempting to
duplicate functional interactions among them would result in a combinatorial explosion of further components to discipline those interactions in the same way that they
are constrained by their substrates in the original system. If on top of this there are
space, timing or robustness requirements on the behavioral profile of the system, as
we would expect for a functional duplicate of a mind, then the requirements will be
difficult to satisfy.

4 Can we loosen the constraints on multiple realization?
We’ve now reviewed some specific empirical facts that militate against the plausibility of neural replacement. I’ve argued that the principle of generative entrenchment
can help us to see why it is not surprising that the functional causal roles in a complex
evolved system will be interwoven so as to produce many constraints in their possible
realizers. But it may seem that the argument proves too much. If the relevant “behavioral” profiles are specified by maximally fine-grained response properties in every
possible context, then it might turn out that nothing is multiply realizable: if there is
any physical test that could distinguish the original from its putative duplicate, then
they are not functionally identical with respect to their responses to that test.

Standard functionalism, of the kind adopted by cognitive scientists and more

that would enforce that level of abstraction the way that we do in our built computational machines. Until cognitive science or neuroscience provide evidence (and not

remains empirically open.

A natural level of abstraction at which to characterize the functional profile comes
from the more scientifically pragmatic question: what is the functional organization
that characterizes a typical brain—and is the duplication of that functional organization sufficient to reproduce whatever psychological features (themselves specified to
a pragmatically reasonable degree of precision) are exhibited by creatures with those
brains?37 That means that the level of detail we are committed to duplicating is just
that level at which we can plausibly say that different individuals within our group of
interest count as having the same functional organization, in producing behaviors at
the level of detail that we are interested in differentiating within. As we know, there
is substantial diversity among humans in both brain architecture and in functional
profile. What we all have in common is a typical human psychological profile realized by a typical adult human brain. Those typical functional states would gloss over
individual differences, so that a range of exact states would qualify as instances of the
same functional state. I will come back to this approach in the last section.
A second way to pull back is to characterize the functional organization itself at
a coarser grain, breaking down the system into larger functional components. Now
the relevant functional states will be those displayed by a group of neurons that have
particular statistical or group dynamical properties over some period of time, with
the presumption that those ensemble properties are the right level at which to specify
the functional role of the component (which is now a group of cells that we have reason to believe tend to work together). This population coding assumption is widely
accepted in contemporary neuroscience. Not only is population activity often more
practically accessible, but it seems clear that groups of neurons that exhibit considerable individual variation at the cellular level (since two cells in two mammals are
exactly alike) still perform very similar functions in the respective brains in which
they reside.

In fact, part of the original motivation for multiple realizability comes from a sympathetic evaluation of the animal kingdom. It certainly seems as though many aspects
of a basic mental profile are realized by many animals, including animals which display significant variation in gross morphology. If their bodies are unlike ours while
their minds are like ours, don’t they stand as actual living examples of multiple realization? I say no, because to suppose this would be to ignore the fact that at a cellular
level, animal brains are very much like ours, even if their gross organization and
outward appearance seem utterly alien. Putnam’s octopus, for example, shares many
features of our pain-related architecture, including peripheral touch receptors mediated by chemical neurotransmitters, sensory areas that are distinct from motor areas
in a brain full of neurons, and endogenous opioids that act on internal receptors that
are genetically similar to ours in much the way that our opioids act on our opioid
receptors. So at least some apparent differences are belied by underlying similarities
when we consider the relevant physical properties important for realizing the relevant
functional and behavioral properties. This is not to deny that octopuses do have quite
different nervous systems than we do at some levels of description, but then, they also
have quite different behavior from ours, issuing from those very physical differences.

To defend multiple realizability, the functionalist has to be able to separate out the
parts of the causal structure that matter in the brain from the ones that don’t (if any).
Because of the nested and intertwined nature of processes undertaken by cells in the
brain, the result of generative entrenchment, this separation seems unlikely at the cellular and molecular level. The brain is an evolved biological entity tightly integrated

for alternative realizations would require changing the very functional organization
that the functionalist wants to preserve.

5 Implications for Functionalism
Functionalism as originally conceived by Putnam took “functional organization” to
be the organization of states of the whole system, given by the machine table of contingencies and transitions between states of the entire system, which could in theory
be understood independently from the physical organization of discrete components
(though of course it needn’t be). It was that conception of functionalism, with token
identities such that functions did not need to depend on any particular individuation
of functional parts, that gave us materialism as merely a light cloak on a relatively
unconstrained theory of mental states.
Machine functionalism has mostly been left behind, but many philosophers still
take the spirit of functionalism to be freedom—freedom from material constraints
and the messy details of biology, freedom from chauvinism and reductionist naïveté,
and freedom to embrace a clean computational model of the mind. I’ve argued that
standard functionalism, understood in terms of the causal roles played by the constituents of a complex system, is more like an iron cage—and that the kind of causal
structure necessary for a mind like ours can be satisfied only by a body like ours with
a brain like ours.
Everything that we know about neurons suggests that it would be very difficult
for a silicon chip to replicate the function of a neuron at the finest level. If it is possible, it seems to me the burden of proof is on the functionalist to show that it is. If
I’m right that fine-grained replacement is not just practically unfeasible, but actually nomologically impossible, then the only functionally isomorphic systems (even
when individuated more coarsely) are systems made of biologically similar stuff, and
we are stuck inside the biological cage. Abstract functional organization, it will turn
out, is not so abstract that it allows us to ignore the material substrate—rather, it is
sensitive to biological realities and the biological (physical and chemical) properties
responsible for them.41

If all we want from functionalism is a conceptual analysis of mental state talk—
that analytical functionalism in no way threatened by the implausibility of multiple
realizability.42 Some functionalists, including Lewis and Armstrong, and perhaps also
Clark (2008) are understanding their project in this way. David Lewis (1972) outlined
a project for characterizing functional roles at the personal level—to be derived from
the platitudes, suitably regimented. But at that maximally coarse-grained level, functionalism says very little if anything about causal roles at any subpersonal level. And
even at the top (personal) level, this kind of functionalism is only a schema, and one
that has yet to be filled out. Perhaps folk psychology is rich enough to specify all the
functional roles. But it is not the sort of thing that can tell us more than we already
knew from the armchair.43
In The Mind Incarnate, Shapiro writes, “Functionalism’s great promise lay in
its suggestion that it is possible to investigate minds at a level of description that
makes unnecessary an appreciation of the bodies and brains in which they are implemented.” What Dennett (1986) has called “High-Church Computationalism” relies
heavily on this promise, in its rejection of the relevance of neuroscience to the study
of the mind. But this promise depends entirely on the truth of the empirical bet of
multiple realizability. Losing the bet means that functionalists have to embrace the
hard work of getting to know the messy biological details, if functionalism is to serve
as the foundation for a productive research program.
But let me end on an ecumenical note. Arguably what the functionalist really
wants (and needs) is not the possibility of some other substrate playing the same
functional roles as our biological stuff—but rather, the validation of an abstract level
of description that is sufficient to generate the phenomenon of interest. If it is possible to specify the functional roles and organization at an informative level of detail

different domains. So we might be relatively optimistic about the promise of neural
prostheses and practical brain-computer interfaces in just those cases where we are
concerned with a narrowly delineated task. This is because when the interface is
low-dimensional and context-specific, many of the holistic constraints on the system
are lifted. With fewer employed properties, and more leeway in producing functional
differences elsewhere in the system (since we are only interested in duplicating a
particular behavioral capacity), the options for compensatory adjustments are abundant. The same may also be true for neural network models of particular aspects of

details matter for maintaining complex integrated functional profiles, then we should
be skeptical about the possibility of uploading one’s entire cognitive economy to a
virtual environment (realized in an artificial computational system) while preserving
the relevant cognitive capacities.
Functionalism was originally supposed to be the best of both worlds: as materialistically respectable as the identity theory, but more informative than behaviorism,
while avoiding both chauvinism and liberality. Ironically, taking functionalism seriously points us back in both directions at once: Towards a methodological behaviorism on the one hand—where we specify capacities and phenomena that we are
interested in, top-down, and then ask a general computational question about what it
takes to realize those capacities and phenomena; and towards a biologically-focused
identity theory on the other—where we pay attention to the messy details of the intricate dance of complex biological processes selected over billions of years to produce
just what it takes to do well in a demanding world. If the constraints that complex
