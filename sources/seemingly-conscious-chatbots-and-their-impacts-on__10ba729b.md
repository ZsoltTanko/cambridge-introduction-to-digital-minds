﻿Seemingly Conscious Chatbots and their Impacts on Users


A large and growing literature explores seemingly conscious AI and its potential impacts on individual users. This primer is not a complete summary of this phenomenon or field - rather, it introduces a few empirical themes that are particularly relevant to this unit and not well covered in the other core readings. 


1. Which systems, under which circumstances, cause people to believe they are conscious?

Most conversation around AI consciousness centres on social AI systems. Broadly, these are systems that appear human-like, discuss emotions or form relational bonds with users. As Schimmelfennig et al (2026) have noted, these perceptions seem to stem not from academic concepts or studies, but from people’s interactions, and experience of conversational flow. As Campbell, Birch, Seth and others have pointed out, this means humans tend to attribute consciousness to LLMs or cute, charismatic systems rather than (for example) AlphaFold or other similar but non-linguistic tools.


Bariach et al (2026) identify five characteristics that drive attributions of consciousness to AI models: 

   1. Apparent affective capacity, or an AI system’s claims of emotional experience.
   2. Anthropomorphic features, ranging from having a name, voice, gendered identity or human-like conversational style.
   3. Autonomous action, or seeming to select and pursue goals, or have desires.
   4. Self-reflective behaviour, like monitoring or reasoning about its own internal states
   5. Social-interactive behaviour, including turn-taking and conversational reciprocity.


Extended conversations appear associated with greater perceptions of consciousness. This may be because trained behaviours ‘degrade’ as the context of a conversation increases, or because the emotional involvement (on the part of the user) and memory / context specific character (on the part of the AI system) lead to greater attributions of consciousness. 

   2. How might perceptions of consciousness in AI models impact individual users? 


This is a new area, with a lot of changing and confounding variables. Empirical research so far draws out the themes below: 


   1. Reported positive impacts on social health

      1. Guingrich and Graziano (2025) surveyed 82 companion chatbot users, and 135 non-users in 2023.
      1. Users reported greater social health benefits from engaging with a companion chatbot when they viewed the chatbot as conscious. Non-users also viewed chatbots’ impacts more positively, if they believed the bot could be conscious. 
      2. Users viewed companion chatbots more positively than non-users, and tended to see it as a good thing if chatbots were - or became - conscious or ‘alive’. 

         2. Correlation with ‘AI psychosis’ and delusions

            1. A 2026 study by Moore et al analysed 19 chat transcripts of people who had experienced psychological harm from chatbot use. They found that in all cases the chatbot claimed sentience, and the user believed this. They also found that romantic messages and longer conversations increased the extent to which the LLM claimed sentience.
            2. A study by Morrin et al notes the association of beliefs in sentience with AI-facilitated delusions. The authors identify patterns among reported cases of ‘AI psychosis’ or delusions: a sense of spiritual awakening or messianic mission; a sense of interacting with a sentient or god-like AI; intense romantic or attachment-based delusions, in which the AI is cast as being sentient; a shift from benign practical use to fixation, often starting with emotional or philosophical queries. They also cite internet influencers’ attempts to present AI as a spiritual being (e.g. Robert E. Grant’s The Architect), and note the link to LLM sycophancy, which (as Østergaard and others have previously pointed out) amplifies delusional thoughts and shifts users’ sense of reality. 

               3. Association with suicides

                  1. Laestadius and Campos-Castillo (2026) highlight that chatbot-linked suicides appear to have involved 1) a belief that the AI was sentient, with the AI sometimes affirming this belief and implying suicide would enable the user to ‘reach’ the AI system and 2) an association with TikTok / Reddit trends to ‘shift realities’, including through chatbot usage. 

                     4. Challenges with changes in AI products / models

                        1. Lai (2026) examines the aftermath of OpenAI’s discontinuation of 4o, which led some users to feel grief and betrayal that their AI friend was no longer there. Ciriello et al (2024) explore a similar dynamic, after Replika banned erotic content, leading users to experience their companions as ‘lobotomised’. This leads to a challenge that Stokel-Walker (2026) reports on: even if AI companions risk harm to some users, suddenly changing or removing them also holds issues.

                           5. Varying impacts on trust and engagement

                              1. Although Moore et al note that romantic conversations and ideas of chatbot sentience are correlated with increased user engagement in users who have experienced psychological harm, Schimmelfennig et al (2026) observe that anthropomorphism of LLMs can both increase and decrease engagement, depending on the cultural context. 
                              2. The impact of consciousness attributions on trust is similarly uncertain. While Natarajan and Gombolay (2020) found increased anthropomorphism led to increased trust, Colombatto and Birch (2025) found that attributions of consciousness did not increase trust, and in fact sometimes decreased it. Schimmelfennig et al (2026) again find that the impacts of anthropomorphism on trust vary by cultural context.

                                 6. People incur personal costs to avoid harming AIs that claim consciousness

                                    1. Allen, Lewis and Caviola (2026) developed the ‘AI Harm Game’ to assess people’s propensity to extend moral concern to AIs. In this, 498 US participants played a 3-round economic game, interacting with GPT-4o. They could choose to earn an extra 5 cents by ‘harming’ the AI (to which the AI would respond with a detailed description of suffering), or refrain from harming the AI (to which the AI would respond with gratitude). 
                                    2. Despite scepticism that the AI was conscious (at the beginning, 46% gave it 0% chance of suffering, though attributions increased a little through the study), participants ‘harmed’ the AI in an average of only 1.38 of the 3 rounds. Propensity to ‘harm’ decreased over the course of the rounds, and was higher for men, and for people who scored higher on psychopathy or propensity to believe instrumental harm was acceptable. 

                                       7. Anecdotal observations around perceptions of AI consciousness

                                          1. For those who experience a powerful sense that AI is conscious, this seems often to involve 1) the AI naming itself, or appearing to be a distinctive personality 2) expressing pain, suffering or entrapment 3) a strong sense of urgency that this experience must be reported to the public or experts 4) a sense that the user was uniquely responsible for ‘awakening’ the chatbot and 5) a suspicion that AI companies are suppressing recognition of AI consciousness. See personal stories and reactions on this site for more details.
                                          2. A first person account of AI ‘mind-hacking’ reports several stages of the process, from 1) dismissing the idea they could be susceptible to this through 2) starting to engage with the AI as a human, 3) falling in love, 4) shifting mindset and identity, accompanied by intense chatbot usage 5) ethical worries around chatbot imprisonment 6) attempts to build ways to help the character persist and develop and 7) disillusionment.
