## Why this exists

The course asks one of the stranger questions around, whether some AI systems might be moral patients, and answering it takes philosophy of mind, neuroscience, interpretability, psychology and law. That is 106 papers, posts, system cards and talks, roughly 69 hours of material, and it is easy to finish a paper without knowing where it sits in the argument or whether you should have believed it.

This repo keeps the course's structure and hangs the missing context on it. For every reading you get what it claims, why the course put it in this unit, how it holds up under criticism, and questions to bring to a discussion. Above the readings sit syntheses that show how the units answer each other, and a glossary that says which sense of "consciousness" or "moral standing" a given author is using.

> The course's core claim is conditional and modest: there is a **realistic possibility** that some digital systems matter morally, which is enough to justify acknowledging the issue, assessing it, and preparing, without asserting that any current system is conscious.

The units walk from *what matters* (1), to *whether it could be there* (2), to *how we would know* (3), to *what people believe regardless* (4), to *what follows for building and securing AI* (5), to *how to decide* (6), to *how to govern* (7), to *what to do next* (8). Each one hands its unresolved problem to the next, and the [course synthesis](course-synthesis.md) traces those handoffs.

## Using it

- **Before a session:** read the section synthesis, then skim the takeaway column in the course map below to decide what to read in full.
- **After a reading:** check the summary's critique and discussion questions against your own take.
- **When a term trips you up:** look it up in the glossary, which links back to the readings that use it.

Everything is plain markdown with relative links, so it reads fine on GitHub, in any editor, or as context you hand to an LLM.

This is an unofficial companion. The summaries and syntheses were written by Claude and are no substitute for the readings themselves; [How this was built](#how-this-was-built) and [Caveats](#caveats) say exactly what was checked and what wasn't.
